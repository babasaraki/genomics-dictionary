  • ACMG/AMP variant classification

    ACMG/AMP variant classification is a shared framework that clinical laboratories use to decide how confident they are that a DNA variant causes disease. It sorts evidence into weighted criteria and combines them into five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign and benign. The aim is that different laboratories reading the same evidence reach the same conclusion.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Admixture

    Admixture happens when people from populations that had been separated for a long time have children together, so their descendants carry chromosome segments of different ancestral origin. Over successive generations recombination cuts these segments into shorter pieces. Most human populations today show some degree of admixture, and it is a feature of a genome's history rather than a defect.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Adverse drug reaction (ADR)

    An adverse drug reaction, abbreviated ADR, is harm caused by a medicine given at a dose used for treatment or prevention. It differs from a medication error, where the wrong drug or dose was given. Some reactions are predictable extensions of the drug's action; others are rare, severe and immune-mediated, and a few are strongly associated with inherited variation.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Allele

    An allele is one of the alternative versions of a sequence found at a particular place in the genome. Because people inherit one copy of each chromosome from each parent, an individual carries two alleles at most positions, which may be identical or different. Alleles can differ by a single letter or by a larger stretch of sequence.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Amplicon sequencing

    Amplicon sequencing is a method in which chosen regions of a genome are copied many times by the polymerase chain reaction and only those copies are sequenced. Because the target is enriched before sequencing, very small amounts of pathogen material can be read even when a sample is dominated by human or environmental DNA. Tiled sets of overlapping primers can cover a whole small genome.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Aneuploidy

    Aneuploidy means having a number of chromosomes that differs from the usual complement for that organism, such as one extra copy or one missing copy. In humans it usually arises when chromosomes fail to separate correctly during the formation of eggs or sperm. Aneuploidy is also very common in cancer cells, where whole chromosomes are frequently gained or lost.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)

    Antimicrobial resistance, abbreviated AMR, is the ability of bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites to survive medicines that would normally stop them. The microbe becomes resistant, not the person or animal being treated. Resistance arises through genetic change or through genes acquired from other microbes, and it spreads when resistant organisms are passed on or when resistance genes move between them.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Antimicrobial resistance gene

    An antimicrobial resistance gene is a stretch of DNA whose product helps a microbe survive a drug, for example by destroying the drug, pumping it out or changing its target. Such genes may sit on the chromosome or on mobile pieces of DNA that move between microbes. Sequencing can detect known resistance genes directly from a genome.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Association versus causation

    An association means two things vary together: a genetic variant is seen more often in people with a trait, or a gene's expression tracks a disease state. A causal relationship means that changing one would change the other. Association is observed; causation is inferred, and the inference requires evidence beyond the observation that the two travel together.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Human and population genomics Published

  • Base pair (bp)

    A base pair is two nucleotide bases on opposite strands of a double-stranded nucleic acid that are held together by hydrogen bonds. In DNA, adenine pairs with thymine and cytosine pairs with guanine. Because of this fixed pairing, each strand carries the information needed to rebuild the other. Base pairs are also the standard unit for measuring the length of a DNA sequence.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST)

    The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, abbreviated BLAST, searches a query sequence against a database and returns the entries that resemble it most, together with a measure of how surprising each match is. It is the usual first question asked of an unknown sequence: what else looks like this, and how closely? Variants of the tool handle nucleotide and protein queries.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Pathogen genomics Published

  • Batch effect

    A batch effect is a systematic technical difference between groups of samples that were processed separately. Samples extracted on different days, sequenced on different instruments, or prepared by different staff can differ measurably for reasons that have nothing to do with biology. If those technical groupings line up with the comparison of interest, the artefact is indistinguishable from the biological signal.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Benefit sharing

    Benefit sharing means that the people and communities who contribute samples, data or effort to research receive a fair share of what the research produces. Benefits can be financial, but more often they are practical: training, equipment, authorship, local analysis capacity, access to results, or affordable access to any product that is developed.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Biobank

    A biobank is an organised collection of biological samples and linked data, stored so that many researchers can study them over long periods. Samples may be blood, saliva or tissue, and the linked data can include questionnaires, measurements and health records. Participants consent to this future use, and governance arrangements determine who may access which data and under what conditions.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Bioinformatics

    Bioinformatics is the discipline that stores, organises and analyses biological data using computers. Most of that data comes from sequencing DNA, RNA or protein, and it arrives in volumes far too large to inspect by hand. Bioinformatics supplies the file formats, databases, algorithms and statistics that turn raw instrument output into results a biologist, clinician or public health team can actually read and act upon.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Breadth of coverage

    Breadth of coverage is the proportion of the target region that was read at all, or that reached a stated minimum depth. It answers a different question from read depth: not how many times a position was read, but how much of the intended region was covered. It is usually reported as a percentage of target bases.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Broad consent

    Broad consent is agreement to the future use of samples and data for a described range of research, rather than for one named study only. Participants are told the kinds of research that may be done, the governance that will decide on access, and how they can withdraw. It is not blanket permission for anything whatsoever.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • CPIC guideline

    A CPIC guideline is a published document from the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium that explains what to do with a pharmacogenomic test result once it exists. It does not advise whether to order a test; it translates a genotype into a predicted phenotype and sets out prescribing implications, with the strength of evidence stated for each recommendation.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • CYP2C19

    CYP2C19 is a liver enzyme involved in processing several widely used medicines, including some that reduce stomach acid, some antidepressants and a drug that prevents blood clots by being converted into its active form. Inherited versions of the gene range from no activity to increased activity, which alters how much active drug a person ends up with.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • CYP2D6

    CYP2D6 is a liver enzyme that processes a substantial share of commonly used medicines, including some painkillers, antidepressants and heart medicines. The gene encoding it is unusually variable: people can carry versions with no activity, reduced activity, or extra copies that increase activity. This variability makes it one of the most studied pharmacogenes.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Carrier screening

    Carrier screening is testing that tells a person whether they carry one copy of a variant for a recessive condition. Carriers of such conditions are typically healthy, because the second working copy of the gene is enough. The information becomes relevant when two carriers of the same condition have children together, since the condition can then appear.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Cell-free DNA screening (cfDNA screening)

    Cell-free DNA screening analyses short fragments of DNA circulating in a person's blood. In pregnancy, a sample from the pregnant person contains fragments from the placenta as well as from their own cells, and the mixture can be analysed to estimate the chance of certain chromosomal conditions in the pregnancy. It is a screening test, not a diagnosis.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Centromere

    A centromere is the constricted region of a chromosome where the two copies produced by DNA replication are held together and where the cell attaches the machinery that pulls them apart during division. Its position gives each chromosome a characteristic shape under the microscope. Centromeres contain long stretches of highly repetitive DNA that are difficult to sequence.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Chromosome

    A chromosome is a long molecule of DNA wound around proteins and packaged so that it fits inside a cell and can be moved reliably when the cell divides. Human cells typically carry forty-six chromosomes arranged in twenty-three pairs, with one member of each pair inherited from each parent. Chromosomes become visible under a microscope when a cell is dividing.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA)

    Circulating tumour DNA, abbreviated ctDNA, is the fraction of DNA in a person's blood that came from tumour cells. Dying cells release short DNA fragments into the bloodstream, and in someone with cancer a portion of those fragments carries the tumour's changes. Testing blood in this way is often called a liquid biopsy.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Clade

    A clade is a group on an evolutionary tree made up of one ancestor and all of its descendants. In pathogen genomics, clade labels give convenient names to broad branches of a virus or bacterium, so that scientists discussing a seasonal influenza clade or a SARS-CoV-2 clade mean the same set of sequences. Clades are nested: a smaller clade sits inside a larger one.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Clinical actionability

    Clinical actionability describes whether knowing a genomic result could change what is done for a person's care. A finding is actionable when an established intervention exists, such as earlier surveillance, a preventive measure, a change of medicine or a different treatment. Many genomic findings are informative and interesting without being actionable in this specific sense.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Clonal evolution

    Clonal evolution describes how a tumour changes over time as its cells acquire new mutations and compete with one another. Cells whose changes give an advantage leave more descendants, so the makeup of the tumour shifts. Treatment is part of this process, because it removes sensitive cells and leaves resistant ones room to expand.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Codon

    A codon is a group of three consecutive bases in a messenger RNA that specifies one amino acid during protein synthesis, or signals that synthesis should stop. Reading a coding sequence three bases at a time gives the order of amino acids in the protein. There are sixty-four possible codons, and most amino acids are specified by more than one of them.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Companion diagnostic

    A companion diagnostic is a test that is formally tied to a particular medicine and is used to decide whether that medicine is suitable for a person. The test identifies a feature, often a genomic alteration or a protein level, that the medicine's evidence depends on. Regulators approve the test and the medicine as a linked pair.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Computational reproducibility

    Computational reproducibility means that someone else, given the same data and the same description of the analysis, obtains the same results. It is a weaker and more achievable goal than replication, which asks whether a finding holds in a new experiment with new samples. Reproducibility is about the transparency of the computation, not about whether the conclusion is true.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Confidence interval (CI)

    A confidence interval is a range of values, calculated from the data, that expresses how precisely a quantity has been estimated. A ninety-five per cent interval is constructed by a procedure that, repeated across many studies, would contain the true value in ninety-five per cent of them. A wide interval signals an imprecise estimate; a narrow one signals a precise estimate.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Consensus sequence

    A consensus sequence is a single representative sequence built from many overlapping sequencing reads covering the same part of a genome. At each position the base seen in most reads is chosen, and positions with too little data are usually masked with the letter N. For pathogens, the consensus genome is the file that is shared, compared and used to assign a lineage.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Containerisation

    Containerisation packages a piece of software together with the libraries and settings it needs, so that it runs the same way on any machine with a compatible container runtime. In bioinformatics it solves a persistent problem: analyses depend on long chains of tools and libraries whose versions differ between computers, and those differences quietly change results.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Contamination control

    Contamination control covers the laboratory practices that stop unwanted DNA or RNA entering a reaction or moving between samples. Because amplification and sequencing can detect tiny amounts of material, even invisible traces matter. Measures include separating work areas, using negative controls, handling reagents carefully and watching for suspicious results in the data.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Controlled access

    Controlled access means that individual-level genomic data are not downloadable by anyone, but released only to approved researchers who apply, state their purpose and agree to conditions. A committee checks that the request matches what participants consented to. Summary results from the same study are often published openly, because they carry much less risk of identifying anyone.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Copy number variant (CNV)

    A copy number variant, or CNV, is a stretch of DNA present in a different number of copies in one person than in another, either deleted or duplicated one or more times. Copy number differences are an ordinary part of human variation. Some change how much of a gene product is made, and a subset is associated with developmental conditions or disease.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Core genome MLST (cgMLST)

    Core genome multilocus sequence typing, abbreviated cgMLST, extends the idea of typing by gene alleles from a handful of genes to the hundreds or thousands of genes shared by almost all members of a species. Each gene is given an allele number, and two isolates are compared by counting how many of those numbers differ. The result is a portable, high-resolution measure of relatedness.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Cycle threshold (Ct)

    The cycle threshold, written Ct, is the number of amplification cycles a quantitative PCR test needs before its fluorescent signal becomes clearly detectable. A sample containing more of the target reaches that point sooner, so a lower cycle threshold indicates more starting material. The value depends on the particular assay and instrument used.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • DNA methylation

    DNA methylation is the addition of a small chemical group to a DNA base, most often a cytosine followed by a guanine. The sequence itself is unchanged, but the mark influences whether nearby genes are switched on. Patterns of methylation differ between tissues, are copied when cells divide, and change during development, with age and in disease.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • DPYD

    DPYD encodes the enzyme that breaks down fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy drugs such as fluorouracil and capecitabine. People who carry variants reducing this enzyme's activity clear the drug more slowly and face a much greater risk of severe toxicity. Testing for a small set of these variants before treatment is recommended in several health systems.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Data sharing

    Data sharing is making research data available to others, so that findings can be checked, combined and reused. In genomics it ranges from publishing summary statistics openly to releasing individual-level sequence data under application and approval. Sharing accelerates discovery, especially for rare conditions and outbreaks, but must respect consent, confidentiality and the interests of the communities who contributed.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • De novo assembly

    De novo assembly reconstructs a genome from sequencing reads alone, without using a reference sequence of the same organism as a guide. It is the approach taken when no suitable reference exists, when the sample may contain sequence the reference lacks, or when the aim is to describe structure rather than catalogue differences from a template.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Pathogen genomics Published

  • De-identification

    De-identification is the removal or masking of details that directly identify a person, such as name, address, hospital number and date of birth, before data are used or shared. It reduces risk but does not make genomic data anonymous, because a genome sequence is itself a distinguishing characteristic that can sometimes be matched to a person.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)

    Deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA, is the chemical that carries the inherited instructions used by almost every living organism. It is built from four chemical letters strung along two strands that wind around each other in a double helix. The order of those letters stores information that cells read to build proteins and to decide when each gene is switched on. Cells copy their DNA before dividing, so the instructions pass to the next generation of cells.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Diagnostic yield

    Diagnostic yield is the proportion of people tested in whom a test provides an explanation for their condition. If a laboratory sequences one hundred people with a suspected genetic disorder and reaches a confident answer in thirty, the yield is thirty per cent. Yield describes the performance of a test in a particular group of patients, not in everybody.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Differential expression

    Differential expression analysis asks which genes or transcripts are expressed at different levels between conditions, such as treated and untreated cells, or tumour and adjacent tissue. It compares normalised measurements across replicate samples, estimates how much each feature changes, and assesses whether the change is larger than the variability seen between replicates of the same condition.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Dominant inheritance

    Dominant inheritance describes a pattern in which a single altered copy of a gene is enough to produce a trait or condition, even though the other copy is unchanged. A person with such a variant has, on average, a one in two chance of passing it to each child. Dominant conditions often appear in several generations of a family.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Driver mutation

    A driver mutation is a change in a tumour's DNA that gives the cell a growth advantage and so contributes to the cancer developing or progressing. Only a small number of the changes found in a tumour are drivers. The rest are bystanders that accumulated along the way without helping the tumour grow.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Effective population size (Ne)

    Effective population size, written Ne, is the size of an idealised population that would lose genetic variation at the same rate as the real population being studied. It is usually much smaller than the number of people alive, because real populations have unequal family sizes, overlapping generations and uneven sex ratios. It is a bookkeeping device for predicting drift, not a headcount.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Epigenetics

    Epigenetics is the study of changes that affect how genes are used without altering the underlying DNA sequence. These include chemical marks added to DNA or to the proteins that package it, which influence whether a gene is switched on or off. Such marks differ between tissues and can change over time and with environment, and some persist through cell division.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Equitable research partnership

    An equitable research partnership is a collaboration in which the partners share decisions, resources, recognition and data rather than one group supplying samples while another does the science. It covers who sets the question, where the sequencing happens, who analyses the data, who is named on the paper, and who controls what happens to the data afterwards.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Exon

    An exon is a part of a gene that is kept in the finished RNA molecule after the gene has been copied and edited. Most human genes are split into several exons separated by sequences that are removed. Exons may contain the instructions for part of a protein, or they may carry untranslated sequence at either end of the RNA.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Expressivity

    Expressivity describes how differently a trait or condition can appear among people who carry the same genotype. Where penetrance asks whether a carrier is affected at all, expressivity asks how severely and in which tissues. Variable expressivity means that two relatives with an identical variant may have very different features, ages of onset or degrees of difficulty.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • FAIR principles

    The FAIR principles are a set of guidelines stating that research data should be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable. They describe how data and their descriptions should be organised so that both people and computers can locate and use them. FAIR concerns good data management; it does not mean data must be open to everyone.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • FASTA format

    FASTA is a plain-text format for storing sequences. Each record begins with a header line starting with a greater-than sign, followed by the sequence itself on the lines beneath. It is used for reference genomes, gene and protein sequences, assemblies and consensus sequences. Unlike FASTQ, a FASTA record carries no quality information, only the letters of the sequence.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • FASTQ format

    FASTQ is the standard text format for raw sequencing reads. Each read occupies four lines: an identifier, the sequence of bases, a separator, and a string of characters encoding how confident the instrument was about each base. Because the quality string travels with the sequence, downstream software can trim or discard unreliable positions rather than treating every base as equally trustworthy.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • False discovery rate (FDR)

    The false discovery rate, abbreviated FDR, is the expected proportion of mistakes among the results a study declares positive. If a list of one hundred differentially expressed genes is reported at a five per cent false discovery rate, about five of them are expected to be false findings. It describes the quality of a list rather than the status of any single item.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Founder effect

    A founder effect occurs when a small group separates from a larger population and establishes a new one. The founders carry only part of the original population's variation, so whatever alleles they happen to have become the starting point for everyone descended from them. An allele that was rare in the source population can end up far more common in the new one.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • GFF and GTF formats

    GFF and GTF are text formats that describe where features sit on a genome. Each line gives a sequence name, a start and end coordinate, a feature type such as gene, transcript or exon, a strand, and a set of attributes. They are how a genome assembly, which is only a string of letters, is linked to the biology layered on top of it.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Gene

    A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for making a product the cell uses, most often a protein but sometimes a functional RNA molecule. Each gene sits at a particular place on a chromosome and usually includes signals that control when and where it is switched on. People carry two copies of most genes, one inherited from each parent.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Gene expression

    Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make a product, usually a protein. It is also the term for how much of that product a cell makes. Expression varies between tissues, between individuals and over time, and it responds to signals, so the same genome produces very different cells.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genetic ancestry

    Genetic ancestry describes where in the past the segments of a person's DNA came from, reconstructed by comparing their genome with samples from many populations. It is a statement about lines of descent, estimated with uncertainty. It is not the same as race, nationality, ethnicity or the culture someone was raised in, all of which are social categories that people define and claim for themselves.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Genetic code

    The genetic code is the set of rules by which cells translate the sequence of bases in a messenger RNA into the sequence of amino acids in a protein. Bases are read in groups of three, and each group corresponds to one amino acid or to a stop signal. The same code is used by almost all living things, with a few known exceptions.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genetic counselling

    Genetic counselling is a conversation between a trained professional and a person or family about how a genetic condition or test result might affect them. It covers what a result can and cannot say, what it may mean for relatives, and what choices exist. The counsellor supports the person in reaching their own decision rather than directing it.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Genetic discrimination

    Genetic discrimination is unfair treatment of a person because of their genetic information or a presumed inherited risk, for example in employment, insurance or access to services. Fear of it is one of the main reasons people hesitate to take part in genomic research or to have a test, so protections matter for public trust as well as for fairness.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Genetic drift

    Genetic drift is the change in how common an allele is from one generation to the next, caused simply by chance in who reproduces and which copies are passed on. It occurs in every population but matters most in small ones, where frequencies can swing widely. Drift can make an allele common, or lose it altogether, without the allele doing anything useful or harmful.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Genetic recombination

    Genetic recombination is the exchange of segments of DNA between two chromosomes or DNA molecules. In people it happens mainly during the formation of eggs and sperm, when matching chromosomes swap sections. The result is that the chromosome a child inherits is a mixture of the two copies carried by that parent, so each child receives a different combination.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genome

    A genome is the complete set of genetic material in an organism or a cell. For a person this means all the DNA in the nucleus, inherited from both parents, plus the small separate genome inside mitochondria. The genome includes genes and the much larger amount of DNA between and around them that helps control how genes behave.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genome assembly

    Genome assembly is the process of reconstructing the long chromosomes of an organism from the many short or medium-length reads a sequencer produces. Overlapping reads are joined into longer stretches called contigs, which may then be ordered and oriented into scaffolds. The output is a set of sequences that approximates the genome, together with statistics describing how complete and contiguous it is.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

    A genome-wide association study, or GWAS, tests hundreds of thousands to millions of positions across the genome to see whether any version of a position occurs more often in people with a trait than in people without it. It scans the genome without a prior hypothesis about which gene matters, and it reports statistical associations rather than proven causes.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Genomic data protection

    Genomic data protection is the set of legal and practical measures that govern how genetic data about people are stored, used and shared. Because a genome is permanent, identifiable and informative about relatives, most data protection laws treat it as a special category requiring stronger safeguards than ordinary personal information.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Genomic surveillance

    Genomic surveillance is the routine sequencing of pathogens collected over time so that changes in the population of circulating strains can be seen early. Rather than testing one outbreak and stopping, laboratories sequence a sample of cases every week and share the results. This shows which lineages are spreading, when a new one arrives, and whether resistance or immune escape is increasing.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Genotype

    A genotype is the particular combination of alleles an individual carries at one or more places in the genome. It can describe a single position, such as which two letters a person has at one site, or a set of positions across many genes. Genotype is determined by inheritance and, unlike many observable traits, does not usually change during life.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genotype imputation

    Genotype imputation fills in positions that were not directly measured, by comparing the positions that were measured against a reference panel of people whose genomes are known in detail. Because nearby variants travel together on shared stretches of chromosome, matching those stretches lets software predict the missing genotypes, together with a confidence value for each prediction.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Germline variant

    A germline variant is a difference in the DNA sequence that was present in the egg or sperm cell from which a person developed. Because it was there from the beginning, it is carried in essentially every cell of the body and can be passed on to children. Most germline variants are harmless differences between people; only a small minority affect health.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • HLA-B*57:01

    HLA-B*57:01 is one version of an immune system gene that presents fragments of proteins to T cells. People carrying this version are at high risk of a serious hypersensitivity reaction to the antiretroviral medicine abacavir. Testing for it before the medicine is started is one of the earliest and clearest examples of pharmacogenomics in routine practice.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Haplotype

    A haplotype is a set of DNA variants that sit on the same copy of a chromosome and are usually inherited together as a unit. Because we carry two copies of most chromosomes, one from each parent, every person has two haplotypes across any region. Knowing which variants travel together often tells you more than knowing the list of variants alone.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Haplotype phasing

    Haplotype phasing works out which of a person's genetic variants sit on the same copy of a chromosome. Standard genotyping records that someone carries two different versions at two positions, but not how those versions are paired between the maternal and paternal copies. Phasing resolves that arrangement, either by statistical comparison with known haplotypes or by reading long DNA molecules directly.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE)

    Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium describes the simple relationship expected between how often each allele occurs and how often each genotype occurs, in a large population that mates at random and in which nothing is disturbing the alleles. If the two alleles have frequencies p and q, the expected genotype proportions are p squared, two pq and q squared. Real populations only approximate this, so it serves as a baseline for comparison.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Heritability

    Heritability is the share of the differences between people in a trait, within a particular population and set of environments, that can be attributed to differences in their genes. It is a property of a group rather than of an individual, and it changes when the population or the environment changes. A high value does not mean a trait is fixed or beyond modification.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD)

    Homologous recombination is a precise way of mending breaks that run through both strands of the DNA double helix, using the matching chromosome as a template. Homologous recombination deficiency, abbreviated HRD, means a tumour has lost this accurate repair route and must rely on error-prone alternatives. The resulting scars across the genome can be measured.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)

    Horizontal gene transfer, abbreviated HGT, is the movement of genetic material between organisms other than from parent to offspring. Bacteria can take up free DNA from their surroundings, receive DNA through direct contact with another cell, or be injected with it by a virus. This is why a resistance gene can appear in a species that never had it before.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Immune escape variant

    An immune escape variant is a version of a pathogen carrying changes that make it less easily recognised by antibodies produced after infection or vaccination. The changes usually sit in the parts of a surface protein that antibodies bind. Escape is partial and relative: it reduces the protection a population has built up rather than abolishing immunity.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • In silico serotyping

    In silico serotyping means working out which serotype an organism belongs to by examining its genome sequence on a computer, instead of testing it with antibodies in the laboratory. The method looks for the genes that produce the surface structures the antibodies would have recognised. It gives results that can be compared with decades of older laboratory typing data.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Informed consent

    Informed consent is the process by which a person agrees to take part in research, or to a test, after being given clear information about what is involved, what will happen to their samples and data, and what the possible benefits and harms are. It is a continuing conversation, not just a signature, and participation can be withdrawn.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Insertion or deletion (indel)

    An insertion or deletion, often shortened to indel, is a variant in which extra DNA letters are added or existing letters are removed compared with a reference sequence. Indels range from a single letter to many. Within protein-coding sequence, an indel whose length is not a multiple of three shifts the reading frame and usually disrupts the protein that follows.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Intron

    An intron is a section of a gene that is copied into the initial RNA molecule but then cut out before the RNA is used. The remaining pieces, called exons, are joined together. Introns are common in the genes of plants, animals and fungi, and although they are removed, sequences within and around them help control how the RNA is assembled.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Karyotype

    A karyotype is the complete set of chromosomes in a cell, and also the laboratory image in which those chromosomes are arranged in order by size and banding pattern. Examining a karyotype shows how many chromosomes are present and whether any large pieces are missing, duplicated or rearranged. It is one of the oldest techniques in genetics and remains useful.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Library preparation

    Library preparation is the set of laboratory steps that turns extracted DNA or RNA into a form a sequencing instrument can read. The material is cut or copied into fragments of suitable length, short known sequences called adapters are attached to the ends, and sample-specific barcodes are added. The quality of this step largely determines the quality of the data.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Linkage disequilibrium (LD)

    Linkage disequilibrium, or LD, means that particular versions of two nearby DNA positions turn up together more often than chance alone would predict. It arises because stretches of chromosome are passed down intact, and recombination only occasionally shuffles them. LD generally weakens with distance along the chromosome and with the number of generations since the alleles first appeared together.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Locus

    A locus is a specific location in the genome, described by the chromosome it sits on and its position along that chromosome. A locus can be as small as a single base or as large as a whole gene or region. The plural is loci. Giving a locus precisely requires stating which reference assembly the coordinates refer to.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Long-read sequencing

    Long-read sequencing reads very long stretches of DNA in one piece, often tens of thousands of bases, rather than many short fragments. Long reads can span repeated regions and large rearrangements that short reads cannot cross, so they make assembly and structural analysis easier. Historically they made more errors per base, although the chemistries have improved substantially.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Loss of heterozygosity (LOH)

    Most human chromosomes come in pairs, so a person can carry two different versions of a gene. Loss of heterozygosity, abbreviated LOH, happens when a cell loses one of those versions, leaving only one kind present at that location. In tumours this often removes the remaining working copy of a gene that was restraining growth.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Machine learning in genomics

    Machine learning covers methods that learn patterns from data rather than following rules written by a programmer. In genomics it is used to predict regulatory elements, classify tumours from molecular profiles, call variants, assign taxonomy to sequences and predict protein structure. A model is trained on examples with known answers, then evaluated on data it has never seen.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Material transfer agreement (MTA)

    A material transfer agreement, abbreviated MTA, is a contract between institutions setting out the terms on which biological material is sent from one to another. It records what is being transferred, what the recipient may do with it, what happens to anything derived from it, and who may publish or own resulting intellectual property.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Measurable residual disease (MRD)

    Measurable residual disease, abbreviated MRD, refers to the small number of cancer cells that can remain after treatment when standard scans and ordinary microscopy no longer detect anything. Sensitive molecular or cellular methods can sometimes find this remaining disease. The term was previously rendered as minimal residual disease, and both forms are still encountered.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Meiosis

    Meiosis is the specialised form of cell division that produces eggs and sperm. It halves the number of chromosomes so that each gamete carries one copy of each chromosome instead of two. When egg and sperm combine, the full number is restored. Meiosis also shuffles the genetic material inherited from each parent, so siblings are genetically different.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Mendelian inheritance

    Mendelian inheritance describes traits and conditions determined largely by variants in a single gene, passed on in predictable patterns first described by Gregor Mendel. The main patterns are dominant, recessive and those linked to the X chromosome. Many rare conditions follow these patterns, while most common traits and diseases involve many genes together with environmental influences.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Messenger RNA (mRNA)

    Messenger RNA, usually abbreviated to mRNA, is the type of RNA that carries the instructions from a gene to the machinery that makes proteins. It is produced by copying a gene, edited to remove intervening sections, and then read three bases at a time to assemble a protein. The amount of a given messenger RNA in a cell reflects how active that gene currently is.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Metaboliser status

    Metaboliser status is a summary of how quickly a person is predicted to process a particular medicine, based on the pair of gene versions they carry. The usual categories run from poor, through intermediate and normal, to rapid and ultrarapid. It is a prediction from genotype, not a measurement of the drug in the body.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Metagenomics

    Metagenomics is the sequencing of all the genetic material in a sample, without first growing or targeting any single organism. From a swab, a stool sample or river water, it produces reads from bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites and the host together. Analysts then sort the reads by likely origin to see what is present.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Microarray

    A microarray is a slide or chip carrying many thousands of short known DNA sequences arranged in fixed positions. Labelled material from a sample sticks to the spots that match it, and the pattern of signal shows which sequences were present and in what quantity. Arrays measure predetermined targets rather than reading sequence directly.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Microsatellite instability (MSI)

    Microsatellites are short stretches of DNA in which a very small motif repeats many times. These regions slip easily during copying, and a cell with working repair machinery corrects the errors. Microsatellite instability, abbreviated MSI, is the pattern of varying repeat lengths seen in a tumour whose repair system is no longer correcting those slips.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Minor allele frequency (MAF)

    Minor allele frequency, or MAF, is how often the less common of two versions of a DNA position is seen in a group of people. If eight in every hundred copies of a position carry a G and ninety-two carry an A, the minor allele frequency is 0.08. The figure describes a sampled population, not an individual, and the same allele can be common in one population and almost absent in another.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR)

    Mismatch repair is the system that proofreads newly copied DNA and corrects mispaired bases and small slips. Mismatch repair deficiency, abbreviated dMMR, means that this system is not working in a tumour. Errors then accumulate, especially in repetitive stretches, giving the tumour an unusually large number of mutations compared with repair-proficient tumours.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)

    Mitochondrial DNA, or mtDNA, is the small circular genome inside mitochondria, the compartments that generate most of a cell's usable energy. It is separate from the chromosomes in the nucleus, present in many copies per cell, and in humans it is inherited from the mother. It carries a small number of genes, all serving energy production or the machinery that builds mitochondrial proteins.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Mobile genetic element (MGE)

    A mobile genetic element, abbreviated MGE, is a piece of DNA that can move, either within a genome or between organisms. Plasmids, transposons, insertion sequences, integrons and bacteriophages are all examples. Mobile elements matter in pathogen genomics because they carry resistance and virulence genes from one microbe to another, often across species.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Molecular clock

    A molecular clock is the idea that genetic changes accumulate in a population at a roughly steady average rate, so the number of differences between two sequences can be used to estimate how long ago they shared an ancestor. In outbreak work, clocks let analysts put dates on the branching points of a tree and estimate when an outbreak began.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)

    Multilocus sequence typing, abbreviated MLST, is a way of labelling bacterial strains by reading the sequence of a small set of housekeeping genes, usually around seven. Each distinct sequence of a gene gets a number, and the combination of numbers defines a sequence type. Because the labels are just numbers, laboratories anywhere can compare strains without exchanging the sequences themselves.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Multiple testing correction

    Multiple testing correction adjusts statistical results when many hypotheses are tested at once. If a threshold meant for a single test is applied to a million of them, a large number of apparently significant findings will arise purely by chance. Correction either raises the bar for significance or reinterprets the results so that the expected number of false findings is controlled.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Multiplexing and barcoding

    Multiplexing means sequencing many samples together in one run. Each sample is given its own short identifying sequence, called a barcode or index, during library preparation. After the run, software sorts the reads back to the sample they came from by reading those barcodes, a step called demultiplexing. This shares the cost of a run across many samples.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Mutation

    A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence compared with what was there before. Mutations arise from errors during DNA copying, from chemical damage, or from exposure to agents such as radiation. They may be inherited from a parent or acquired during a person's lifetime. Most have no noticeable effect, some are harmful, and a few are advantageous.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Mutational signature

    A mutational signature is a characteristic pattern of DNA changes left behind by a particular process. Different causes of damage, and different failures of repair, favour different kinds of change in different sequence contexts. Reading the pattern across a tumour genome can suggest which processes have been at work in that tumour's history.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • N50

    N50 is a summary statistic describing how contiguous a genome assembly is. Sort the assembled pieces from longest to shortest and add their lengths until half the total assembly length is reached; the length of the piece that takes the running total past that halfway point is the N50. A larger N50 means the assembly is built from longer pieces.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Nagoya Protocol

    The Nagoya Protocol is an international agreement under the Convention on Biological Diversity covering access to genetic resources and the sharing of benefits arising from their use. Countries that have joined it require users to obtain permission before collecting biological material and to agree terms for sharing benefits. Many research projects involving samples collected abroad must comply.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Nanopore sequencing

    Nanopore sequencing reads DNA or RNA by pulling a single strand through a tiny protein pore in a membrane. As each part of the strand passes through, it changes an electrical current in a characteristic way, and software converts that changing signal into a sequence of bases. The devices are small and produce data as the run proceeds.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Natural selection

    Natural selection is the process by which inherited differences that affect survival or reproduction become more or less common across generations. If carrying a particular allele means leaving more descendants in a given environment, that allele's frequency rises. Selection acts only on variation that already exists, and what counts as advantageous depends entirely on the environment at the time.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Newborn screening

    Newborn screening is a public health programme that tests babies shortly after birth for a small number of serious conditions that can be treated effectively if they are found early. It usually uses a few drops of blood taken from the heel. Screening does not diagnose; a result outside the expected range leads to confirmatory testing.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Next-generation sequencing (NGS)

    Next-generation sequencing, abbreviated NGS, is a family of methods that read millions of DNA fragments at the same time instead of one at a time. The sample is broken into pieces, prepared into a sequencing library and loaded onto an instrument that reads every fragment in parallel. Computers then assemble or map those reads to work out the original sequence.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Normalisation

    Normalisation adjusts measurements so that samples can be compared fairly. In sequencing, one sample may simply have produced more reads than another, and longer genes attract more reads than shorter ones. Normalisation removes such technical differences in scale, so that remaining differences between samples are more likely to reflect biology rather than how much data each sample yielded.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Nucleic acid extraction

    Nucleic acid extraction is the process of getting DNA or RNA out of a sample and into a clean solution that later methods can use. Cells are broken open, proteins and other material are removed, and the nucleic acid is captured, washed and released. How well this is done sets a ceiling on everything that follows.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Nucleotide

    A nucleotide is the basic building block of DNA and RNA. Each one has three parts: a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base. Nucleotides join end to end to form long chains, and the order of their bases carries genetic information. DNA uses the bases adenine, cytosine, guanine and thymine, while RNA uses uracil in place of thymine.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Odds ratio (OR)

    An odds ratio compares the odds of an outcome between two groups. In genomic association studies it usually compares the odds of a trait in people carrying one allele with the odds in those carrying another. A value of one means no difference, above one means higher odds in the first group, and below one means lower odds. Both directions matter.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Human and population genomics Published

  • Oncogene

    An oncogene is a gene whose overactivity can drive a cell to divide when it should not. Most oncogenes are altered forms of normal genes, called proto-oncogenes, that control growth signals under tight regulation. A change that locks such a gene into a permanently switched-on state can contribute to the development of a tumour.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • One Health

    One Health is an approach that treats the health of people, animals and the environment as connected, and organises surveillance and response across all three. Many infections and most antimicrobial resistance problems move between these domains, so looking at only one gives an incomplete picture. In practice it means human, veterinary and environmental services sharing data and planning together.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Open reading frame (ORF)

    An open reading frame is a stretch of sequence that could be read as a continuous set of three-letter codons without hitting a stop signal. Because sequence can be read in several frames and on either strand, software looks for such stretches when predicting where protein-coding genes might lie. An open reading frame is a candidate for coding, not proof of it.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Outbreak investigation

    An outbreak investigation is the organised effort to find out why more cases of an infection than expected are occurring, who is affected, and how to stop further spread. It combines case finding, interviews, laboratory testing and, increasingly, sequencing of the pathogen. The aim is practical: identify the source, interrupt transmission and prevent the same thing happening again.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Overfitting

    Overfitting happens when a model learns the particular noise of the data it was trained on instead of the pattern that generalises. Such a model performs impressively on its training data and poorly on anything new. It is a constant hazard in genomics, where datasets typically contain far more measured features than samples.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • P value

    A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as those collected, if the hypothesis of no effect were true and the statistical model were correct. A small p-value means the data would be surprising under that assumption. It does not give the probability that the hypothesis is true, nor does it measure how large or important an effect is.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Paired-end sequencing

    Paired-end sequencing reads each DNA fragment from both of its ends rather than from one end only. The two reads come from the same molecule and are a roughly known distance apart, which helps software place them correctly and reveals when that distance is unexpected. Reading from one end only is called single-end sequencing.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Pangenome

    A pangenome is a representation of a species' DNA that holds many genomes at once, instead of a single linear sequence. For humans it is assembled from people of diverse ancestries, so that sequence present in some people but missing from the traditional reference is included. It is usually stored as a graph, with shared paths and alternative branches where genomes differ.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Pango lineage

    A Pango lineage is a label from a naming system used to describe fine-grained groups of SARS-CoV-2 genomes that share recent common ancestry. Names such as B.1.1.7 or BA.2 use letters and dots to show how groups branch from one another. The system was designed so that newly spreading groups can be named quickly during an epidemic.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Passenger mutation

    A passenger mutation is a change in a tumour's DNA that happened along the way but does not itself help the tumour grow. Cells accumulate such changes continually, and when one cell gives rise to a tumour, all the changes it had already collected are carried along. Passengers greatly outnumber the changes that actually drive the cancer.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Pathogen genomics

    Pathogen genomics is the study of the complete genetic material of organisms that cause disease, including viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites. Reading a pathogen's genome shows which species or strain is present, how closely one sample resembles another, and which genetic changes it carries. Public health laboratories use these details to follow outbreaks, detect resistance to medicines and judge whether control measures are working.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Pathogenic variant

    A pathogenic variant is a DNA change that the available evidence shows is capable of causing or strongly predisposing to a disease. It is a judgement about evidence, not a separate kind of molecule: the same sequence change is simply better supported as harmful than the many variants that make no difference. Classifications can be revised when new evidence arrives.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Penetrance

    Penetrance is the proportion of people carrying a particular genotype who actually show the associated trait or condition. If every carrier is affected, penetrance is complete; if only some are, it is reduced or incomplete. Penetrance is a property of a variant in a population and a context, not a prediction about any single person.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Pharmacogenomics (PGx)

    Pharmacogenomics studies how differences in a person's DNA affect the way their body handles medicines and how those medicines act. Inherited differences can change how quickly a drug is broken down, how strongly it works, or how likely a severe reaction is. The abbreviation PGx is used for both pharmacogenomics and the closely related term pharmacogenetics.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Phenotype

    A phenotype is any observable or measurable characteristic of an organism, from eye colour and height to blood test values, disease status and how someone responds to a medicine. Phenotypes arise from the combined action of genetic variation, developmental processes and environment. Because a phenotype has to be measured, how it is defined and recorded affects what any study can conclude.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Phred quality score

    A Phred quality score tells you how confident a sequencing instrument is about a single base it has called. The scale is logarithmic: a score of twenty means roughly a one in a hundred chance the base is wrong, thirty means one in a thousand, and forty one in ten thousand. Every base in a read carries one.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Phylodynamics

    Phylodynamics is the study of how the shape of a pathogen's evolutionary tree is produced by the way the infection spreads. Because each transmission event leaves a trace in the genomes, the pattern of branching can be used to estimate things epidemiologists care about, such as whether an epidemic is growing, when it started and how populations are connected.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Phylogenetic tree

    A phylogenetic tree is a diagram that shows how sequences are related by descent from common ancestors. Each tip is a sampled sequence, each internal branching point is an inferred ancestor, and branch lengths usually represent the amount of genetic change or elapsed time. Trees are the main way outbreak analysts visualise which pathogen samples belong together.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Plasmid

    A plasmid is a small piece of DNA, usually circular, that sits inside a bacterial cell separately from the chromosome and copies itself independently. Plasmids are not essential for basic survival, but they often carry genes that are useful in particular conditions, such as resistance to antibiotics or heavy metals. Many can be passed from one bacterium to another.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Polygenic risk score (PRS)

    A polygenic risk score, or PRS, adds up the small contributions of many genetic variants to give a single number summarising how a person's genome compares with others for a trait. It is a relative ranking within a reference group, not a diagnosis or a prediction of what will happen. For most common traits, non-genetic factors account for a large share of the variation between people.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

    The polymerase chain reaction, abbreviated PCR, is a laboratory method that makes many copies of a chosen piece of DNA. Short synthetic primers mark the start and end of the region, and repeated cycles of heating and cooling let an enzyme copy it again and again. A small amount of starting material can become enough to detect or sequence.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Population structure

    Population structure means that the people in a dataset are not one randomly mixing group: allele frequencies differ systematically between subgroups because of geography, history, migration and patterns of partnership. If this patterning is ignored, it can create apparent links between a variant and a trait that actually reflect which subgroup a person belongs to rather than any biological effect.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Positive predictive value (PPV)

    Positive predictive value, abbreviated PPV, is the proportion of positive test results that are correct. It answers the question a person actually asks: given that my result is positive, how likely is it that the condition is really present? Unlike sensitivity and specificity, it depends strongly on how common the condition is in the group being tested.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Pre-emptive pharmacogenomic testing

    Pre-emptive pharmacogenomic testing means testing a person's relevant genes before any particular medicine is being considered, and storing the result for future use. Because inherited genotypes do not change, one test can inform prescribing decisions for years. The alternative, reactive testing, happens only when a specific medicine is about to be prescribed.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Promoter

    A promoter is a stretch of DNA just before a gene where the machinery that makes RNA first assembles. It acts as a starting signal, setting where transcription begins and influencing how often it happens. Promoters contain short sequence patterns recognised by proteins, and their activity can differ between tissues and between conditions within the same organism.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

    Quantitative PCR, abbreviated qPCR, measures how much of a target sequence is present while the copying reaction is running, rather than only looking at the end product. A fluorescent signal rises as copies accumulate, and the cycle at which it becomes detectable indicates how much target was there at the start. More starting material gives an earlier signal.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq)

    RNA sequencing, abbreviated RNA-Seq, reads the RNA molecules present in a sample to show which genes are being expressed and at what relative level. The RNA is converted to DNA, prepared as a sequencing library and read, and the resulting reads are counted against genes or transcripts. It captures a snapshot of activity at the moment of collection.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • RNA splicing

    RNA splicing is the editing step in which a cell removes the intervening sections of a freshly made RNA molecule and joins the remaining pieces together. This produces a continuous sequence ready to be read for protein synthesis. Many genes can be spliced in more than one way, so a single gene can give rise to several different RNA molecules and proteins.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Read

    A read is one stretch of sequence produced by a sequencing instrument from a single fragment of DNA or RNA. Each read is a string of bases together with a quality value for every base, indicating how confident the instrument is in that call. A sequencing run produces millions of reads, which analysis software then maps or assembles.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Read depth

    Read depth is the number of sequencing reads that cover a particular position in the genome. A position covered by thirty reads is said to have thirty-fold depth. Greater depth gives more confidence that a difference seen at that position is real rather than an error, and it is usually reported as an average across the target region.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Read mapping

    Read mapping is the process of finding, for each short sequencing read, the place in a reference genome it most likely came from. Millions of reads are placed in this way, producing a pile of overlapping reads at every covered position. Everything that follows, including variant calling and expression counting, depends on those placements being broadly correct.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Recessive inheritance

    Recessive inheritance describes a pattern in which both copies of a gene must be altered before a trait or condition appears. Someone with one altered copy and one unaltered copy is usually unaffected and is described as a carrier. When both parents are carriers, each child has about a one in four chance of inheriting both altered copies.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Reference genome

    A reference genome is an agreed, assembled DNA sequence used as a shared coordinate system, so that laboratories everywhere can describe positions and variants in the same way. The current human reference is a mosaic built from a small number of donors. It is a convenient yardstick rather than a model of a healthy or typical person, and it does not contain all human variation.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Reproduction number (R0)

    The reproduction number is the average number of new infections caused by one infected person. The basic reproduction number, written R0, applies to a fully susceptible population, while the effective reproduction number applies to the population as it actually is, with immunity and control measures in place. A value above one means the epidemic is growing.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Research ethics approval

    Research ethics approval is formal permission from an independent committee to carry out a study involving people, their samples or their data. The committee examines the scientific justification, the risks and benefits, how participants will be recruited and consented, and how data will be protected. Work should not begin before approval is granted.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Return of results

    Return of results means giving participants information that came out of research on their samples or data. This can be the main finding they were tested for, or an unrelated finding noticed along the way. Studies differ in what they offer, and the plan should be explained during consent rather than decided after a finding appears.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Ribonucleic acid (RNA)

    Ribonucleic acid, or RNA, is a nucleic acid closely related to DNA that cells use to carry and act on genetic information. It is usually single-stranded and uses the base uracil where DNA uses thymine. Some RNA molecules carry the instructions for making a protein, while others help build proteins, regulate which genes are active, or form part of the cell's machinery.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • SAM and BAM formats

    SAM and BAM are the standard formats for storing sequencing reads after they have been mapped to a reference genome. SAM is human-readable text; BAM is the same information compressed into a binary file that software can read quickly. Each record says where a read aligned, how well it aligned, and how its bases correspond to the reference at that position.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Sample quality control

    Sample quality control is the set of checks that confirm material is good enough to proceed at each stage of a laboratory workflow. Typical checks measure how much nucleic acid is present, how pure it is, how intact it is, and whether the library has the expected fragment sizes. Failing samples are repeated or reported as uninformative.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Sanger sequencing

    Sanger sequencing is a laboratory method for reading the order of bases along one short stretch of DNA, typically a few hundred letters at a time. Copying of the stretch is deliberately stopped at random positions by special building blocks, and the resulting fragments are sorted by length so that the base at each position can be read off in turn.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Secondary findings

    Secondary findings are results that a laboratory looks for deliberately, but that are unrelated to the reason the test was requested. When a person has their exome or genome sequenced for one condition, a defined list of other genes may also be examined because finding a variant in them could prevent serious harm. Consent is sought beforehand.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Sensitivity and specificity

    Sensitivity is the proportion of people or samples that truly have a condition and are correctly identified by a test. Specificity is the proportion of those without the condition that the test correctly calls negative. Both are properties of the test measured against a reference standard, and they describe performance in two different groups, so they must be reported together.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Sequence alignment

    Sequence alignment is the arrangement of two or more sequences so that corresponding positions sit beneath one another, with gaps inserted where one sequence has material the other lacks. It is the basic operation for comparing sequences, and the resulting pattern of matches, mismatches and gaps is the evidence used to infer relatedness, detect variants or identify an unknown sequence.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Sequencing adapter

    A sequencing adapter is a short, known piece of DNA attached to each end of a fragment during library preparation. It is not part of the sample. Adapters give the instrument somewhere to bind the fragment and start reading, and they usually carry the barcode that identifies the sample. Adapter sequence is removed before analysis.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Sequencing by synthesis

    Sequencing by synthesis works out a DNA sequence by watching a new strand being built against it. The instrument adds labelled building blocks one cycle at a time, records which base was added at each position by its colour or by another signal, and repeats. Doing this for millions of fragments at once is what makes modern sequencing fast.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Short-read sequencing

    Short-read sequencing reads DNA in small pieces, usually between about fifty and three hundred bases each, but reads enormous numbers of those pieces at once. Because each piece is short, software must work out where it came from by matching it against a reference sequence. The approach is accurate per base and inexpensive per sample.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq)

    Single-cell RNA sequencing, abbreviated scRNA-Seq, measures gene activity in individual cells rather than averaging across a whole tissue. Cells are separated, each one's RNA is tagged with a unique label, and everything is sequenced together. The labels let software reconstruct which reads came from which cell, revealing the different cell types present in a sample.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)

    A single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP, is a position in the genome where people commonly differ by one DNA letter. One person may carry a C where another carries a T, and each version is called an allele. SNPs are the commonest form of human genetic variation. Most of them lie outside genes and have no known effect on health or appearance.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Single-nucleotide variant (SNV)

    A single-nucleotide variant, abbreviated to SNV, is a change of one DNA letter at one position compared with a reference sequence. It is the most common kind of difference between individuals and also a frequent change acquired by cells over time. Whether a single-nucleotide variant matters depends on where it falls and what it does, not on its size.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Somatic variant

    A somatic variant is a change in DNA that appears in a body cell during a person's lifetime rather than being inherited. It is found only in that cell and the cells descended from it, such as a tumour, and it cannot be passed to children. Somatic variants accumulate steadily with age and with exposure to agents that damage DNA.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Star allele nomenclature

    Star allele nomenclature is the naming system used for versions of genes that affect how medicines are processed. Each named version, written with an asterisk and a number such as star two, stands for a defined combination of variants inherited together on one chromosome. It names a haplotype rather than a single DNA change.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Structural variant (SV)

    A structural variant, or SV, is a difference in DNA involving a sizeable chunk of a chromosome rather than one or a few letters. Deletions, duplications, insertions, inversions and translocations all count. The usual working threshold is around fifty base pairs. Structural variants are far less numerous than single-letter differences, yet together they affect much more total DNA in any genome.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • TPMT and NUDT15

    TPMT and NUDT15 are two genes whose protein products help the body handle thiopurine medicines, used in some leukaemias, inflammatory bowel disease and immune conditions. People with reduced activity of either protein are at much higher risk of severe suppression of blood cell production. Testing before treatment is established practice in many health systems.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Targeted panel sequencing

    Targeted panel sequencing reads only a chosen set of genes or regions instead of the whole genome. The regions are captured or amplified before sequencing, so most of the data come from the targets. Concentrating the reading effort on a small target allows much greater depth for the same cost, which matters when looking for rare or low-level variants.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Telomere

    A telomere is the protective cap at each end of a chromosome, made of a short DNA sequence repeated many times together with bound proteins. Telomeres stop the ends of chromosomes from being treated as broken DNA and prevent them from fusing together. They shorten each time most cells divide, which limits how many divisions a cell can undergo.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Transcription

    Transcription is the process by which a cell copies a stretch of DNA into RNA. An enzyme called RNA polymerase binds near the start of a gene, unwinds the two DNA strands and builds an RNA molecule that matches one of them. The resulting RNA may carry instructions for a protein or may itself do a job in the cell.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Translation

    Translation is the process by which a cell builds a protein using the instructions carried in a messenger RNA. Ribosomes move along the messenger RNA reading its bases three at a time, and transfer RNA molecules deliver the matching amino acids. The amino acids are joined into a chain that folds into a working protein. Translation stops at a stop signal.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Transmission cluster

    A transmission cluster is a group of infections whose pathogen genomes are so similar that the cases are likely to be linked by recent spread rather than by chance. Clusters are identified by counting genetic differences between sequences or by their position on a phylogenetic tree. Finding a cluster tells investigators where to look for a shared setting, contact or source.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Tumour heterogeneity

    Tumour heterogeneity means that the cells within one tumour are not all the same. Different regions can carry different mutations, behave differently and respond differently to treatment, and secondary deposits elsewhere in the body may differ again from the original site. A single small biopsy samples only part of this variation.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Tumour mutational burden (TMB)

    Tumour mutational burden, abbreviated TMB, counts how many somatic changes a tumour carries, usually expressed per million bases of DNA examined. Tumours with a high burden may display more abnormal proteins on their surface, which can make them more visible to the immune system. It is used as a marker alongside other tests, not as a diagnosis.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Tumour suppressor gene

    A tumour suppressor gene normally restrains cell growth, repairs damaged DNA or triggers the death of a damaged cell. Losing the function of such a gene removes a brake, making it easier for a tumour to develop. Because cells carry two copies of most genes, both usually have to be disabled before the effect is felt.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Turnaround time

    Turnaround time is how long it takes from a sample arriving, or being collected, to a result being issued. In genomics it covers extraction, library preparation, sequencing, analysis, interpretation and reporting. Different parts of that chain dominate for different tests, and a result that arrives too late to inform a decision has limited practical value.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Variant

    A variant is a difference between the DNA sequence observed in a sample and the sequence of the reference used for comparison. Variants are ordinary: every person carries millions of them. A variant is simply a difference, and saying that one exists tells you nothing about whether it affects health. That judgement requires separate evidence and a formal classification process.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Variant Call Format (VCF)

    Variant Call Format, usually shortened to VCF, is the standard text format for recording differences between sequenced samples and a reference genome. Each line describes one position: where it sits, what the reference carries, what alternative alleles were seen, how confident the caller is, and the genotype of every sample in the file. Annotations and filters are added as extra fields.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Variant annotation

    Variant annotation adds meaning to a list of genomic positions. For each variant it records which genes and transcripts it falls in, what effect it is predicted to have on the protein, how often the allele is seen in population databases, and what clinical databases already say about it. Annotation organises the evidence; it does not by itself decide significance.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Variant calling

    Variant calling is the step that decides, from the reads piled up at each position of a genome, whether a sample differs from the reference and what its genotype is. The caller weighs the number of reads supporting each allele, their base and mapping qualities, and the expected behaviour of the sequencing technology, then emits the sites it judges to be real.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS)

    A variant of uncertain significance, usually shortened to VUS, is a DNA change for which there is not yet enough evidence to say whether it affects health. It sits between benign and pathogenic on the classification scale. A VUS is an honest statement about incomplete knowledge rather than a weak positive result, and it may be reclassified later in either direction.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Version control

    Version control is a system that records the history of a set of files, who changed what, when and why. Each saved state can be recovered, differences between states can be inspected, and parallel lines of work can be developed and then merged. In genomics it is applied to analysis code, workflow descriptions, configuration and documentation.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Warfarin pharmacogenomics

    Warfarin thins the blood and has a narrow margin between too little effect and too much. How much a person needs varies widely, and part of that variation is inherited. Variants in a gene that processes the drug and in a gene encoding its target together explain a meaningful share of the differences in dose requirement.

    Pharmacogenomics Published

  • Wastewater surveillance

    Wastewater surveillance means testing sewage for traces of pathogens shed by the people who use the connected toilets. Because samples pool whole communities, they can show that an infection is circulating without anyone needing to be tested individually. Laboratories measure how much pathogen material is present over time, and may sequence it to identify which variants are there.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Whole-exome sequencing (WES)

    Whole-exome sequencing, abbreviated WES, reads the protein-coding portions of the genome, known collectively as the exome. These make up a small fraction of the total DNA but contain a large share of the variants currently known to cause inherited disease. Sequencing only this fraction is cheaper than sequencing everything and produces a smaller, more tractable dataset.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Whole-genome sequencing (WGS)

    Whole-genome sequencing, abbreviated WGS, reads essentially the entire genome of an organism rather than selected parts of it. For a human sample that means both the coding regions and the much larger non-coding fraction; for a bacterium or virus it usually means the complete genome. It provides the broadest view a sequencing experiment can give.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Workflow manager

    A workflow manager is software that describes an analysis as a set of steps with declared inputs and outputs, then runs those steps in the right order, in parallel where possible, on a laptop, a cluster or in the cloud. It keeps track of what has already completed, so an interrupted run can resume rather than start again from the beginning.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • X-linked inheritance

    X-linked inheritance describes patterns produced by genes on the X chromosome. Because people with one X chromosome have only a single copy of those genes, an altered copy is expressed directly, whereas people with two X chromosomes have a second copy that may compensate. This produces family patterns that differ from those of genes on the other chromosomes.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Y-chromosome haplogroup

    A Y-chromosome haplogroup is a branch of the family tree built from the male-specific part of the Y chromosome, which passes from father to son largely unchanged. Men who share the same set of defining variants are placed on the same branch. Haplogroups summarise a single paternal line of descent and are labelled with letters and numbers under an international naming convention.

    Human and population genomics Published

  • Zoonosis

    A zoonosis is an infection that can pass from animals to people. Rabies, brucellosis, Lassa fever and many others are zoonoses. Some spread only from animals to individual people, while others, once introduced, can then spread between people. Genomic methods help identify the animal source, estimate when the jump happened and distinguish repeated spillovers from sustained human transmission.

    Pathogen genomics Published
