Mendelian inheritance

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Mendelian inheritance describes traits and conditions determined largely by variants in a single gene, passed on in predictable patterns first described by Gregor Mendel. The main patterns are dominant, recessive and those linked to the X chromosome. Many rare conditions follow these patterns, while most common traits and diseases involve many genes together with environmental influences.

Technical explanation

Mendelian conditions arise from variants in a single gene with a large effect on phenotype, and their pedigrees follow recognisable segregation patterns. They are individually rare but collectively substantial, and sequencing has greatly expanded the catalogue of genes implicated. Diagnostic analysis proceeds by filtering variants on rarity in population databases, predicted consequence, inheritance model and gene-phenotype match, then weighing evidence under consensus classification frameworks. Diagnostic yield remains well short of complete, reflecting non-coding causes, structural and repeat variants missed by standard assays, genes not yet linked to disease, and phenotypes that are not in fact monogenic. Mendelian expectation is complicated by incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, imprinting, mosaicism, de novo variation and digenic contributions, and by polygenic background that modifies severity even in single-gene conditions. Reference data under-representing many populations reduce the reliability of rarity filters outside well-sampled groups, which is a recognised source of both missed diagnoses and over-calling.

Practical example

A diagnostic team filtering a child's exome retains variants that are rare in population databases and fit a recessive model. One candidate is rare in the main reference datasets but turns out to be relatively common in a population poorly represented there, so the team seeks additional ancestry-matched frequency data before drawing conclusions.

Common misunderstanding

Mendelian is often taken to mean that one gene straightforwardly determines one outcome. Even in single-gene conditions, severity and age of onset vary, and some carriers remain unaffected. The converse error is to expect Mendelian patterns for common diseases, where risk is distributed across many variants of small effect plus environment.
