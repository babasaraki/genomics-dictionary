Sequencing by synthesis

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Sequencing by synthesis works out a DNA sequence by watching a new strand being built against it. The instrument adds labelled building blocks one cycle at a time, records which base was added at each position by its colour or by another signal, and repeats. Doing this for millions of fragments at once is what makes modern sequencing fast.

Technical explanation

Sequencing by synthesis is the family of chemistries in which base identity is read out as a polymerase extends a primer along a template. In the cyclic reversible-termination form used by most short-read instruments, each fragment is amplified into a local cluster of identical copies, and each cycle adds one labelled, reversibly blocked nucleotide per template. The instrument images the flow cell, then cleaves the label and the blocking group so the next cycle can proceed. Base calls accumulate cycle by cycle, with a quality score reflecting the confidence of each call. Single-molecule real-time sequencing is also synthesis-based but observes incorporation continuously in a single immobilised polymerase rather than in cycles. Characteristic limitations follow from the cycling itself: phasing errors, where molecules within a cluster fall out of step, accumulate with cycle number and cause quality to decline towards the end of a read; signal from neighbouring clusters can bleed across; and because clusters originate from amplification, duplicate fragments and composition bias enter before any base is read.

Practical example

A core facility reviews the quality report from a completed run and sees per-base quality falling steadily across the final cycles of each read, in every sample on the flow cell. Recognising this as the expected behaviour of cycle-by-cycle chemistry rather than a fault with any one library, the team trims the low-quality read ends before mapping.

Common misunderstanding

The term is often used as though it meant one manufacturer's instrument. It describes a principle, shared by several chemistries that differ in how the incorporation signal is detected and whether reading proceeds in discrete cycles. Equating sequencing by synthesis with a single platform obscures those differences, which determine read length and error behaviour.
