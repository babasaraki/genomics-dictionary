Genetic recombination

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Genetic recombination is the exchange of segments of DNA between two chromosomes or DNA molecules. In people it happens mainly during the formation of eggs and sperm, when matching chromosomes swap sections. The result is that the chromosome a child inherits is a mixture of the two copies carried by that parent, so each child receives a different combination.

Technical explanation

In meiosis, programmed double-strand breaks are repaired using the homologous chromosome as a template, producing crossovers that exchange segments, alongside non-crossover gene conversion events that transfer short tracts. Crossovers are not uniformly distributed; in many species they cluster in hotspots, and rates differ between the sexes and among individuals. The resulting shuffling breaks down associations between nearby variants over generations, generating the pattern of linkage disequilibrium that underpins tagging in genotyping arrays, haplotype phasing and imputation. Recombination between repeated sequences that are not true alleles, termed non-allelic homologous recombination, can delete, duplicate or invert intervening segments and explains several recurrent copy-number syndromes. In microorganisms, recombination also occurs through horizontal transfer, conjugation and phage-mediated exchange, which complicates phylogenetic inference because a single tree may not describe the whole genome. Analysts therefore test for recombination before interpreting pathogen trees, as unrecognised recombination can distort branch lengths and inflate apparent evolutionary rates.

Practical example

A team building a phylogeny for a bacterial outbreak screens the alignment for recombinant regions before tree building. Several dense clusters of differences are attributed to imported sequence rather than to point change, and masking those regions substantially alters the inferred relationships between isolates and the conclusions drawn about transmission.

Common misunderstanding

Recombination is often pictured as a rare event affecting a small part of the genome. Every meiosis produces crossovers on most chromosomes, and over generations the effect is pervasive. In microbial genomics a different error is to assume a single tree describes the genome; recombination means different regions can have different histories.
