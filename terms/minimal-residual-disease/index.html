Measurable residual disease

MRD

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Measurable residual disease, abbreviated MRD, refers to the small number of cancer cells that can remain after treatment when standard scans and ordinary microscopy no longer detect anything. Sensitive molecular or cellular methods can sometimes find this remaining disease. The term was previously rendered as minimal residual disease, and both forms are still encountered.

Technical explanation

MRD assessment uses assays with far greater sensitivity than conventional imaging or morphology. In haematological malignancies the established methods are multiparameter flow cytometry, quantitative polymerase chain reaction for fusion transcripts or immunoglobulin and T-cell receptor rearrangements, and high-throughput sequencing of those rearrangements. In solid tumours the dominant approach is circulating tumour DNA, usually with a tumour-informed panel built from variants identified in the patient's own tumour so that very low allele fractions can be tracked. Results are reported with an explicit limit of detection, because a negative result means only that disease was below that threshold in the sample examined. Standardisation across laboratories, choice of time point and choice of sample material all influence what a result means, and bone marrow and blood can disagree. The evidence that MRD status predicts relapse is strong in several diseases; evidence that acting on MRD changes outcomes is established in some settings and still being generated in others, so its use varies by disease.

Practical example

After curative-intent surgery for a solid tumour, a laboratory builds a personalised panel from variants found in the resected specimen and tests plasma at intervals. Detection of tumour DNA at a very low fraction some months later, before any imaging change, is recorded as molecular evidence of residual disease and discussed at the multidisciplinary meeting alongside the assay's stated detection limit.

Common misunderstanding

A negative result is often taken to mean that no cancer cells remain. It means only that none were detected at the assay's sensitivity in the material sampled. Terminology also causes confusion: measurable residual disease is now the preferred expansion of the acronym, replacing minimal residual disease, with no change in meaning.
