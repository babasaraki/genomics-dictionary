Pangenome

Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

A pangenome is a representation of a species' DNA that holds many genomes at once, instead of a single linear sequence. For humans it is assembled from people of diverse ancestries, so that sequence present in some people but missing from the traditional reference is included. It is usually stored as a graph, with shared paths and alternative branches where genomes differ.

Technical explanation

A human pangenome reference is a graph in which nodes hold sequence and paths through the graph correspond to observed haplotypes, allowing reads to be aligned against many alternative alleles simultaneously. It is constructed from high-quality, typically long-read assemblies of individuals chosen to span diverse ancestries, and it captures megabases of sequence absent from the linear reference together with structural variation that linear mapping handles badly. Mapping to a graph reduces reference bias, improves genotyping of structural variants, insertions and segmental duplications, and increases variant discovery in regions such as the major histocompatibility complex. The costs are practical: graph coordinates are harder to communicate than linear positions, a great deal of existing software and annotation assumes a linear reference, and the tooling is less mature. Current panels remain limited in the number of individuals included and in global coverage, so broadening representation, particularly from African populations that carry the greatest diversity, is an active priority rather than a solved problem.

Practical example

A team characterising a structurally complex locus finds that short reads from several participants fail to align confidently to the linear reference, leaving a gap in the call set. Re-mapping the same reads to a pangenome graph recovers alignments along an alternative haplotype path and resolves an insertion that the linear analysis had missed completely.

Common misunderstanding

A pangenome is sometimes described as a bigger and better reference genome that simply replaces the old one. It is a different data structure rather than a longer sequence, and it coexists with the linear coordinates that most clinical reporting still uses. Nor does it contain all human variation: it represents the haplotypes of the individuals assembled into it.
