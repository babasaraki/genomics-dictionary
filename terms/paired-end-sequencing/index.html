Paired-end sequencing

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Paired-end sequencing reads each DNA fragment from both of its ends rather than from one end only. The two reads come from the same molecule and are a roughly known distance apart, which helps software place them correctly and reveals when that distance is unexpected. Reading from one end only is called single-end sequencing.

Technical explanation

In paired-end sequencing the instrument reads the first strand of a library fragment, regenerates the template, and reads the opposite end, producing two reads of known orientation separated by the unsequenced middle of the insert. The pair provides two kinds of extra information. First, placement: a read that is individually ambiguous can often be anchored by its mate, improving effective mapping in repetitive regions. Second, structural evidence: because the library has a characteristic insert size distribution, pairs that map too far apart, too close together, in the wrong orientation, or to different chromosomes are candidate signatures of deletions, insertions, inversions and translocations. Paired reads also allow overlapping mates from short inserts to be merged into a single more accurate consensus read, which is useful in amplicon work. The advantages depend entirely on the insert size being appropriate and well controlled, so poor size selection removes most of the benefit. Discordant pairs are evidence, not proof, since mismapping and reference assembly errors generate the same patterns.

Practical example

A laboratory analysing a tumour sample observes a cluster of read pairs whose two ends map to different chromosomes, all pointing to the same two positions. Taken together these discordant pairs suggest a translocation, which is then confirmed by an orthogonal assay before the finding is included in any report.

Common misunderstanding

Paired-end data are sometimes described as doubling the read length. They do not: the middle of the fragment is never read. What a pair provides is two separated observations from one molecule with a known expected distance between them, which aids placement and reveals structural changes, not a single continuous longer sequence.
