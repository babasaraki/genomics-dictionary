Outbreak investigation

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

An outbreak investigation is the organised effort to find out why more cases of an infection than expected are occurring, who is affected, and how to stop further spread. It combines case finding, interviews, laboratory testing and, increasingly, sequencing of the pathogen. The aim is practical: identify the source, interrupt transmission and prevent the same thing happening again.

Technical explanation

Outbreak investigation follows a recognised sequence of steps: confirm the diagnosis and the existence of an excess of cases, define a case, describe cases by person, place and time, generate hypotheses about the source, test those hypotheses analytically, implement control measures and communicate findings. Genomics contributes mainly at the hypothesis-testing stage. Sequencing isolates from cases, and where possible from food, water, animals or the hospital environment, shows whether they are genetically close enough to be plausibly part of one chain of transmission. Typing schemes such as core genome multilocus sequence typing or single nucleotide variant distances provide a comparable measure across laboratories. Thresholds for relatedness are organism-specific and depend on the mutation rate and the time between sampling, so they are guides rather than rules. Two important limitations apply: control measures usually cannot wait for sequencing results, and genomic similarity is necessary but not sufficient evidence of a transmission link, because a widespread endemic clone may produce near-identical isolates in unconnected settings.

Practical example

A hospital notices four cases of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae on one ward within a month. Isolates are sequenced and three are nearly identical while the fourth differs substantially. The infection prevention team concentrates on shared equipment and staffing patterns linking the three patients, and reviews whether the fourth case was acquired before admission rather than on the ward.

Common misunderstanding

People often expect an investigation to end with a single proven source. Many outbreaks close without one. The honest output is a ranked set of hypotheses with the evidence for each, plus the control measures taken. Declaring a source on genomic similarity alone, without an epidemiological link, risks blaming a facility, a food producer or a community incorrectly.
