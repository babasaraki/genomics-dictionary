Phenotype

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A phenotype is any observable or measurable characteristic of an organism, from eye colour and height to blood test values, disease status and how someone responds to a medicine. Phenotypes arise from the combined action of genetic variation, developmental processes and environment. Because a phenotype has to be measured, how it is defined and recorded affects what any study can conclude.

Technical explanation

In genomic research the phenotype is the dependent variable, and its definition usually limits a study more than the genetic data do. Phenotypes may be binary, such as the presence of a diagnosis; quantitative, such as a laboratory measurement; or composite, derived from coded health records. Harmonising phenotypes across cohorts is difficult because diagnostic criteria, coding practice and access to care differ between sites and over time, and misclassification dilutes association signals. Structured vocabularies such as the Human Phenotype Ontology support consistent description of clinical features, which is important in rare disease where matching patients on precise features drives diagnosis. Deep phenotyping, meaning detailed longitudinal measurement, increases power but raises cost and privacy considerations. Phenotypes also vary in heritability, and a measurable family resemblance does not identify which genes are involved or imply that environment is unimportant. Expressivity and penetrance describe how the same genotype can produce phenotypes of different severity, or none at all.

Practical example

A rare disease team recording a child's features uses standardised ontology terms rather than free text. When those coded features are matched against other centres, a second child with overlapping terms and a variant in the same gene is identified, which strengthens the evidence that the gene is relevant and would have been missed had the descriptions remained in narrative form.

Common misunderstanding

It is tempting to assume that a strong genetic contribution to a phenotype means it is fixed. Heritability is a population statistic describing variance in a particular setting, not a statement about any individual, and it can change when the environment changes. Equally, an absent phenotype does not prove the absence of a relevant variant, because penetrance is often incomplete.
