Population structure

Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

Population structure means that the people in a dataset are not one randomly mixing group: allele frequencies differ systematically between subgroups because of geography, history, migration and patterns of partnership. If this patterning is ignored, it can create apparent links between a variant and a trait that actually reflect which subgroup a person belongs to rather than any biological effect.

Technical explanation

Population structure arises whenever mating is not random across a sample, producing correlated allele frequency differences between subgroups. It is detected by principal component analysis of genotype data, by model-based ancestry estimation, or by measuring differentiation with fixation indices. In association testing, structure is a classic confounder: when both allele frequency and trait prevalence differ between subgroups, unadjusted analysis yields associations that track ancestry rather than biology. Standard mitigations include fitting the leading principal components as covariates, using linear mixed models that absorb relatedness and structure through a genetic relationship matrix, or restricting analysis to a genetically homogeneous subset at the cost of generalisability. None is complete, because fine-scale structure within a region, assortative mating and environmental gradients that mirror ancestry can survive adjustment. Structure is also informative in its own right, since it records demographic history, and it determines which reference panels are appropriate for imputation and for frequency comparison.

Practical example

A multi-site study recruiting across several African countries plots the first two principal components of its genotype data and sees samples separating clearly by recruitment site. The team adds the leading components as covariates and refits the association model. Several of the strongest initial signals disappear, indicating that they tracked site-level ancestry differences rather than the trait under study.

Common misunderstanding

Structure is often dismissed as a nuisance that a few principal components remove. Adjustment reduces confounding but does not abolish it, particularly when exposures vary along the same geographical gradients. The opposite error is treating the clusters that emerge from such analyses as discrete biological races; they are summaries of continuous variation shaped by history and by sampling choices.
