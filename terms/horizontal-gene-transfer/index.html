Horizontal gene transfer

HGT

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Horizontal gene transfer, abbreviated HGT, is the movement of genetic material between organisms other than from parent to offspring. Bacteria can take up free DNA from their surroundings, receive DNA through direct contact with another cell, or be injected with it by a virus. This is why a resistance gene can appear in a species that never had it before.

Technical explanation

Horizontal gene transfer proceeds by three classical routes. In transformation, competent cells take up free DNA from the environment and recombine it into their genome. In conjugation, a donor cell transfers a plasmid or integrative conjugative element through a pilus and mating pore. In transduction, a bacteriophage packages host DNA and delivers it to a new cell. Additional routes include membrane vesicles and gene transfer agents. Transfer is constrained by sequence similarity for homologous recombination, by restriction-modification and CRISPR-Cas defences, and by the fitness cost of the acquired DNA, so the observed gene flow is far narrower than the physical opportunities. The consequences for genomics are large. Acquired regions break the assumption of vertical inheritance underlying phylogenetic inference, so recombination detection and masking are routine before tree building in bacteria. Pangenome analyses, which separate core from accessory genes, are the usual framework for describing the resulting variation. Horizontal transfer also explains how resistance and virulence determinants cross species boundaries quickly, making the mobile element rather than the host lineage the relevant unit for some surveillance questions.

Practical example

A surveillance programme finds the same carbapenemase gene in Klebsiella, Enterobacter and Escherichia coli isolates from one hospital network. The chromosomal backbones are unrelated, but the gene sits in a near-identical genetic context in all three. The pattern is consistent with transfer of a mobile element between species rather than spread of a single bacterial clone.

Common misunderstanding

Horizontal transfer is sometimes imagined as a free exchange in which any gene can move anywhere. Barriers are substantial: defence systems destroy incoming DNA, sequence divergence blocks recombination, and most acquisitions are costly and quickly lost. Observed transfers are the survivors of strong filtering, which is why some gene families move widely while most never leave their lineage.
