Karyotype

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A karyotype is the complete set of chromosomes in a cell, and also the laboratory image in which those chromosomes are arranged in order by size and banding pattern. Examining a karyotype shows how many chromosomes are present and whether any large pieces are missing, duplicated or rearranged. It is one of the oldest techniques in genetics and remains useful.

Technical explanation

Conventional karyotyping requires dividing cells, which are arrested in metaphase, spread, stained to produce reproducible banding and then examined microscopically. Results are described in standardised cytogenetic nomenclature stating chromosome number, sex chromosome constitution and any structural findings. Resolution is limited to imbalances of roughly five to ten megabases, so chromosomal microarray and sequencing-based copy-number analysis are used when smaller imbalances are suspected. Karyotyping retains advantages those methods lack: it detects balanced translocations and inversions, which leave copy number unchanged, and it reveals mosaicism and marker chromosomes directly. It also requires viable, culturable cells, which limits its use on some sample types and lengthens turnaround compared with molecular methods. In haemato-oncology, karyotyping of bone marrow remains part of standard characterisation because recurrent structural rearrangements carry prognostic meaning, often complemented by fluorescence in situ hybridisation for specific targets.

Practical example

A haematology laboratory characterising a new leukaemia diagnosis cultures marrow cells and examines banded chromosomes alongside targeted fluorescence in situ hybridisation. The karyotype reveals a balanced rearrangement that a copy-number array would not have detected, and this structural finding becomes part of the disease classification discussed at the multidisciplinary meeting.

Common misunderstanding

Karyotyping is often assumed to be obsolete now that sequencing exists. Sequencing and arrays outperform it for small imbalances but can miss balanced rearrangements and give a less direct view of mosaicism. The opposite error is to treat a normal karyotype as excluding a genetic cause, when its resolution is measured in megabases.
