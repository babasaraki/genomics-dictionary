Tumour heterogeneity

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Tumour heterogeneity means that the cells within one tumour are not all the same. Different regions can carry different mutations, behave differently and respond differently to treatment, and secondary deposits elsewhere in the body may differ again from the original site. A single small biopsy samples only part of this variation.

Technical explanation

Heterogeneity is described along two axes. Spatial heterogeneity refers to differences between regions of a primary tumour and between primary and metastatic sites; temporal heterogeneity refers to change across the course of disease, including under treatment. It is generated by clonal evolution and by non-genetic sources, including epigenetic state, differentiation programme, position within the tumour and interaction with the microenvironment, which together mean that genetically identical cells can behave differently. Measurement approaches differ in what they can see: multi-region bulk sequencing resolves spatial structure but averages within each region, single-cell sequencing resolves individual cells at the cost of sparser data per cell, spatial transcriptomics retains tissue context, and circulating tumour DNA samples shedding sites across the body. The practical consequence is sampling bias: a biomarker call from one biopsy may not represent the whole tumour, and a targetable alteration confined to a subclone predicts a partial response. Reports should therefore state what was sampled and when.

Practical example

A team takes several spatially separated samples from one resected kidney tumour. Some alterations are present in every region, while others appear in only one or two. A biomarker that would have been reported as present from one biopsy is absent from the others, illustrating why a single sample can misrepresent the tumour as a whole.

Common misunderstanding

A tumour is often pictured as a uniform mass of identical cells, so one biopsy is assumed to describe it completely. Regions commonly differ in genotype and behaviour, and metastatic sites can differ from the primary. A negative biomarker result from one sample constrains but does not settle what is present elsewhere.
