Newborn screening

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Newborn screening is a public health programme that tests babies shortly after birth for a small number of serious conditions that can be treated effectively if they are found early. It usually uses a few drops of blood taken from the heel. Screening does not diagnose; a result outside the expected range leads to confirmatory testing.

Technical explanation

Newborn screening programmes are population-wide services built around conditions that meet established screening criteria: the condition is serious, its natural history is understood, an acceptable test exists, and early treatment materially changes outcome. Most programmes rest on biochemical analytes measured by tandem mass spectrometry and immunoassay, with molecular testing used as a second tier or for selected conditions such as severe combined immunodeficiency and some haemoglobin disorders. Panels differ between countries, reflecting local disease burden, treatment availability and health system capacity rather than disagreement about biology; sickle cell disease, for example, is a high priority in many African and Caribbean programmes. Thresholds are deliberately set to favour sensitivity, which means a proportion of screen-positive results will not be confirmed and families need prompt, clear follow-up. Genomic sequencing of newborns is under active study, but it raises distinct questions about conditions without effective early treatment, about findings whose onset is in adulthood, and about consent given on a child's behalf.

Practical example

A maternity unit collects a dried blood spot from a baby at around day five of life. The laboratory flags a haemoglobin pattern consistent with sickle cell disease, and the family is contacted for confirmatory testing within days. Early identification allows preventive care to begin before complications arise, which is the reason the condition is included in the programme.

Common misunderstanding

A screen-positive result is often heard as a diagnosis, which causes avoidable distress. Screening tests are deliberately tuned to miss as few affected babies as possible, so some babies who screen positive turn out to be unaffected once confirmatory testing is complete. Equally, a negative screen covers only the conditions on the panel.
