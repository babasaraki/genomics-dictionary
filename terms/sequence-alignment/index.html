Sequence alignment

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

Sequence alignment is the arrangement of two or more sequences so that corresponding positions sit beneath one another, with gaps inserted where one sequence has material the other lacks. It is the basic operation for comparing sequences, and the resulting pattern of matches, mismatches and gaps is the evidence used to infer relatedness, detect variants or identify an unknown sequence.

Technical explanation

Alignment algorithms optimise a score built from substitution rewards and penalties and gap penalties. Global alignment, in the Needleman-Wunsch sense, aligns sequences end to end and suits comparison of complete, similar sequences; local alignment, in the Smith-Waterman sense, finds the best-scoring subregion and suits searching a short query against a large database. Exact dynamic programming is quadratic in sequence length, so practical tools use heuristics: seed-and-extend searching, suffix arrays or the Burrows-Wheeler transform for read mapping, and anchor-based chaining for whole-genome comparison. Multiple sequence alignment extends the problem to many sequences at once and is normally approximated progressively, because the exact solution is computationally intractable; the resulting alignment is the input to phylogenetic inference, so alignment error propagates directly into tree topology. The central limitation is that an alignment is a hypothesis about homology, not an observation of it. Repetitive sequence, large insertions, rearrangements and divergent regions admit several nearly equal-scoring arrangements, and the one reported depends on the scoring parameters chosen.

Practical example

A surveillance team aligns the genomes of an outbreak cluster before building a phylogeny. A region near the end of each genome contains long gaps where coverage was poor, and these gap-rich columns inflate apparent differences between samples. The team masks the unreliable positions from the alignment and rebuilds the tree, which then resolves the cluster sensibly.

Common misunderstanding

An alignment is often taken as a fact about the sequences rather than a result produced by a chosen algorithm and parameters. Change the gap penalty or the substitution model and the arrangement of an indel-rich region can change too. Where downstream conclusions depend on a few columns, the alignment itself should be inspected.
