Return of results

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Return of results means giving participants information that came out of research on their samples or data. This can be the main finding they were tested for, or an unrelated finding noticed along the way. Studies differ in what they offer, and the plan should be explained during consent rather than decided after a finding appears.

Technical explanation

Policies on returning results distinguish primary findings, secondary or additional findings deliberately sought from a defined gene list, and incidental findings encountered by chance. The usual criteria for offering a result are analytical validity, clinical validity, actionability and the participant's expressed preference. Professional bodies publish lists of genes for which laboratories performing clinical exome or genome sequencing are advised to report findings, and such lists are revised as evidence accumulates. Research settings differ from clinical ones: research pipelines are not always accredited, so a finding typically requires confirmation in a clinical laboratory before it is acted upon, and the infrastructure for counselling may not exist. Returning results has resource implications and raises questions about findings relevant to relatives, about participants who asked not to be told, about children, and about results whose meaning later changes, since a variant of uncertain significance may be reclassified. In settings where clinical genetics services are limited, offering results responsibly requires a referral pathway that actually exists; promising return without one is not defensible.

Practical example

A research study sequencing genomes for a study of infection susceptibility states in its consent materials that it will not search for unrelated findings, and explains why. When an analyst nonetheless notices a well-established cancer susceptibility variant, the protocol directs the case to a review panel with clinical genetics input, which considers the consent terms and the availability of a referral pathway before any contact.

Common misunderstanding

Participants often assume that any health-relevant finding will reach them automatically. Many studies do not look for additional findings and are not equipped to confirm or explain them. The opposite assumption, that research results are ready to guide care, is equally mistaken: research-grade variant calls usually require confirmation in an accredited laboratory, and interpretation can change over time.
