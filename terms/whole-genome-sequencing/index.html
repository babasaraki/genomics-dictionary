Whole-genome sequencing

WGS

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Whole-genome sequencing, abbreviated WGS, reads essentially the entire genome of an organism rather than selected parts of it. For a human sample that means both the coding regions and the much larger non-coding fraction; for a bacterium or virus it usually means the complete genome. It provides the broadest view a sequencing experiment can give.

Technical explanation

Whole-genome sequencing generates reads across the genome without target selection, so coverage depends on library chemistry and mappability rather than on a capture design. Human applications are typically run at a modest uniform depth sufficient to genotype germline variants, while microbial genomes are sequenced to much higher relative depth because the genomes are small. Compared with exome or panel approaches, whole-genome data cover regulatory and intronic sequence, give more even coverage because no hybridisation step is involved, and support detection of structural and copy-number variation as well as mitochondrial and, in pathogens, plasmid content. The constraints are analytical and interpretive rather than technical. Most variants found lie outside coding sequence, where evidence for functional consequence is sparse, so the yield of interpretable findings does not rise in proportion to the number of variants detected. Short-read whole-genome data still miss repetitive and segmentally duplicated regions, and comparison depends on a reference whose composition shapes which variants are even representable, a limitation that pangenome references are intended to reduce.

Practical example

A national public health laboratory sequences complete genomes of bacterial isolates from several hospitals. Because the whole genome is available, investigators can compare isolates at single-base resolution, type them by their core genes, and screen the same data for resistance determinants and plasmid sequences, without running a separate assay for each question.

Common misunderstanding

Whole-genome sequencing is often assumed to detect every variant in a sample. It does not. Coverage is incomplete in repetitive and difficult regions, short reads resolve some structural changes poorly, and variants absent from the reference framework may not be represented at all. Breadth of sequencing should not be confused with completeness of detection or of interpretation.
