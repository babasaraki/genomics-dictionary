Y-chromosome haplogroup

Human and population genomics Published

Definition

A Y-chromosome haplogroup is a branch of the family tree built from the male-specific part of the Y chromosome, which passes from father to son largely unchanged. Men who share the same set of defining variants are placed on the same branch. Haplogroups summarise a single paternal line of descent and are labelled with letters and numbers under an international naming convention.

Technical explanation

Most of the Y chromosome does not recombine, so variants accumulate in a fixed order along paternal lineages, allowing a single bifurcating phylogeny to be reconstructed from single-nucleotide variants; short tandem repeats on the Y mutate faster and support finer-scale and forensic comparison. Nomenclature is hierarchical, with a capital letter denoting a major clade and subclades defined by downstream markers, and names are revised as new branches are discovered, so a haplogroup label is meaningful only alongside the version of the tree used. Sequencing-based calling has largely replaced panels of pre-selected markers and has increased resolution considerably. Like mitochondrial DNA, the Y traces one lineage among a rapidly growing number of ancestral lines and can be entirely unrepresentative of a person's autosomal ancestry. The effective population size of the Y is roughly a quarter that of the autosomes, so drift is strong and lineage extinction common, which shapes observed haplogroup distributions independently of any selective or cultural process.

Practical example

A population genomics course asks students to compare a participant's Y haplogroup, mitochondrial haplogroup and autosomal ancestry estimate. The three point to different source regions. The exercise makes concrete that uniparental markers each follow a single line of descent, whereas autosomal data integrate information across thousands of ancestors in the same pedigree.

Common misunderstanding

Y haplogroups are frequently read as a statement of a man's nationality, ethnicity or overall ancestry. The Y traces one line among many, it is not evidence of membership of any social group, and haplogroup distributions reflect demographic chance as much as migration history. Branch names also change as the phylogeny is revised.
