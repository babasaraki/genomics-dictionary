Plasmid

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

A plasmid is a small piece of DNA, usually circular, that sits inside a bacterial cell separately from the chromosome and copies itself independently. Plasmids are not essential for basic survival, but they often carry genes that are useful in particular conditions, such as resistance to antibiotics or heavy metals. Many can be passed from one bacterium to another.

Technical explanation

Plasmids are extrachromosomal replicons that maintain themselves in bacterial populations through their own replication and partitioning systems. They are classified by incompatibility group or by replicon typing, which reflects the replication machinery they use, and they range from a few kilobases to several hundred kilobases. Conjugative plasmids encode the apparatus to transfer a copy to a recipient cell, while mobilisable plasmids exploit the machinery of others, making plasmids a principal route for the spread of antimicrobial resistance and virulence determinants across species boundaries. Plasmids also carry addiction systems that kill cells losing them, and carrying them usually imposes a metabolic cost that compensatory mutation can reduce. In the laboratory, plasmids are the standard vectors for cloning and recombinant protein expression. Reconstructing plasmids from short-read data is difficult because repeated sequences such as insertion elements prevent closure, so a resistance gene may be assembled on a contig that cannot be confidently assigned to a plasmid or the chromosome; long-read or hybrid assembly is generally needed to resolve complete plasmid structures.

Practical example

An infection prevention team investigating carbapenem resistance across two hospitals sequences isolates with long reads to close the plasmid structures. Several isolates of different species share a nearly identical plasmid carrying the same carbapenemase gene. The investigation therefore turns from tracing one bacterial strain to understanding how a mobile element is moving between species in the clinical environment.

Common misunderstanding

Plasmids are often pictured as permanent features of a strain. They are accessory and dynamic: a plasmid can be lost in culture, gained from an unrelated organism and rearranged. Reporting a resistance gene as plasmid-borne also needs evidence, because short-read assemblies frequently cannot place a gene on a plasmid rather than the chromosome.
