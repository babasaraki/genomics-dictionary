Multiplexing and barcoding

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Multiplexing means sequencing many samples together in one run. Each sample is given its own short identifying sequence, called a barcode or index, during library preparation. After the run, software sorts the reads back to the sample they came from by reading those barcodes, a step called demultiplexing. This shares the cost of a run across many samples.

Technical explanation

Multiplexing adds a sample-specific index sequence to every fragment of a library so that pooled libraries can be separated computationally after sequencing. Indices sit within or adjacent to the adapters, and modern designs place a distinct index at each end, the dual-index arrangement, because a single index is vulnerable to misassignment. Index hopping, in which free adapters or index primers transfer between libraries on a patterned flow cell, moves a small fraction of reads into the wrong sample; unique dual indices allow such reads to be identified and discarded rather than silently counted. Index sets are also chosen for sequence diversity and for an edit distance that permits error correction. Practical design involves balancing the number of samples against the depth each requires, since the run yield is divided among the pool, and ensuring the pool is quantified accurately so that libraries are represented evenly. The consequences of failure are specific: uneven pooling produces samples with insufficient depth, while misassignment can introduce low-level contaminating reads that mimic genuine low-fraction variants.

Practical example

A surveillance laboratory pools ninety-six barcoded pathogen libraries onto one flow cell. After demultiplexing, three samples have far fewer reads than the rest. Reviewing the pooling records shows those three libraries were quantified before a repeat amplification step, so they entered the pool at a lower concentration and are repeated on the next run.

Common misunderstanding

A natural assumption is that demultiplexing returns every read to its true sample. A small proportion is misassigned, through index hopping or sequencing error in the index itself. This matters most when looking for rare variants or for pathogen reads at low abundance, where a handful of misassigned reads can be mistaken for a genuine finding.
