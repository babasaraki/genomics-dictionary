Single-cell RNA sequencing

scRNA-Seq

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Single-cell RNA sequencing, abbreviated scRNA-Seq, measures gene activity in individual cells rather than averaging across a whole tissue. Cells are separated, each one's RNA is tagged with a unique label, and everything is sequenced together. The labels let software reconstruct which reads came from which cell, revealing the different cell types present in a sample.

Technical explanation

Single-cell RNA sequencing resolves transcription at the level of individual cells by isolating them, most commonly in droplets, and attaching a cell barcode and a unique molecular identifier to each captured transcript before amplification. Unique molecular identifiers allow duplicate reads from amplification to be collapsed into molecule counts. The resulting matrix of cells by genes is sparse, because only a fraction of transcripts in each cell is captured, and analysis proceeds through quality filtering, normalisation, dimensionality reduction, clustering and annotation of clusters against marker genes. Applications include defining cell-type composition, identifying rare populations, and studying heterogeneity within a tumour. Several limitations shape interpretation. Tissue dissociation stresses cells and destroys fragile types selectively, so the recovered composition is not necessarily the composition in the tissue. Droplets containing two cells produce artefactual mixed profiles, ambient RNA contaminates all barcodes, and cluster boundaries depend on analytical parameters rather than on a natural discreteness of cell states. Single-nucleus protocols address some dissociation problems, at the cost of losing cytoplasmic transcripts.

Practical example

A group studying a tumour biopsy dissociates the tissue and profiles thousands of individual cells, finding clusters corresponding to malignant cells, fibroblasts and several immune populations. The proportion of each type is interpreted cautiously, because cells differ in how well they survive dissociation, and the group checks key populations against stained tissue sections.

Common misunderstanding

Clusters produced by single-cell analysis are easily mistaken for discrete, naturally defined cell types. They are groupings produced by a chosen set of analytical parameters, applied to cells that survived dissociation. Changing resolution settings changes the number of clusters, and the cells present in the data are not necessarily a faithful sample of those in the tissue.
