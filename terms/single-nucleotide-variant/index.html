Single-nucleotide variant

SNV

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A single-nucleotide variant, abbreviated to SNV, is a change of one DNA letter at one position compared with a reference sequence. It is the most common kind of difference between individuals and also a frequent change acquired by cells over time. Whether a single-nucleotide variant matters depends on where it falls and what it does, not on its size.

Technical explanation

A single-nucleotide variant is a substitution at one position, distinguished from a single-nucleotide polymorphism chiefly by convention: polymorphism historically implied a frequency above about one per cent in a population, while variant makes no claim about frequency and is preferred in clinical reporting. Calling single-nucleotide variants from short reads involves alignment, local realignment or assembly, and a probabilistic model of base quality and read support, with filters tuned to balance sensitivity against false positives. Germline calling assumes expected allele fractions near one half or one, whereas somatic calling must detect low fractions in impure, subclonal tumour samples and therefore uses matched normal samples and error models designed for low-frequency signal. Consequences range from synonymous changes to missense, nonsense and splice-affecting changes, and non-coding substitutions may alter regulatory elements. Context-specific artefacts, including oxidative damage introduced during library preparation and deamination in formalin-fixed material, produce characteristic false substitutions, which is why strand bias and mutational context are inspected before reporting.

Practical example

An analyst reviewing a tumour panel sees many low-frequency changes of one base type in a formalin-fixed sample. The pattern is consistent with a known artefact of fixation rather than with real somatic change, so the laboratory requires orthogonal confirmation before any such call is reported to the oncology team.

Common misunderstanding

A single-nucleotide variant is often assumed to be minor because only one base changes. Single-base changes include some of the best characterised disease-associated variants, and conversely most are without consequence. Equating single-nucleotide variant with single-nucleotide polymorphism is a second error, since the latter carries an implication about population frequency.
