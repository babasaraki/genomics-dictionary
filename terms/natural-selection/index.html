Natural selection

Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Natural selection is the process by which inherited differences that affect survival or reproduction become more or less common across generations. If carrying a particular allele means leaving more descendants in a given environment, that allele's frequency rises. Selection acts only on variation that already exists, and what counts as advantageous depends entirely on the environment at the time.

Technical explanation

Selection changes allele frequencies in proportion to differences in relative fitness. It takes several forms: directional selection shifts a trait in one direction, stabilising selection favours intermediate values, balancing selection maintains multiple alleles, and purifying selection removes deleterious variants, which is why constrained genes show a deficit of loss-of-function variation in large population databases. The best-characterised human example is the sickle haemoglobin allele HbS, where heterozygotes have substantially reduced risk of severe falciparum malaria while homozygotes develop sickle cell disease; the allele reaches appreciable frequencies across much of sub-Saharan Africa where malaria transmission is or has been intense. Lactase persistence alleles in pastoralist populations and variants affecting high-altitude physiology are further well-supported cases. Selection leaves detectable genomic signatures, including extended haplotype homozygosity, elevated frequency differentiation between populations, and distorted allele frequency spectra. Interpretation demands care, because bottlenecks and expansions produce similar patterns, and a statistical signal does not identify which variant was the target or which trait was selected.

Practical example

A class is asked why an allele associated with a serious inherited condition remains common in parts of Africa, Asia and the Mediterranean. Working through the malaria example, students see that fitness is a property of a genotype in a particular environment, and that an advantage to heterozygotes can maintain an allele whose homozygous form causes severe disease.

Common misunderstanding

Selection is often imagined as progress towards a better or more advanced genome. There is no such endpoint. An allele favoured under one set of conditions can become disadvantageous when the environment changes, and much of the genome is not under strong selection at all. Selection also does not act for the good of a species; it operates through differential reproduction of individuals.
