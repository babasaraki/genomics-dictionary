Exon

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

An exon is a part of a gene that is kept in the finished RNA molecule after the gene has been copied and edited. Most human genes are split into several exons separated by sequences that are removed. Exons may contain the instructions for part of a protein, or they may carry untranslated sequence at either end of the RNA.

Technical explanation

Exons are the segments retained in a mature transcript after splicing. They include coding sequence and the untranslated regions at the five-prime and three-prime ends, so exon and coding sequence are not synonymous. Exon boundaries are defined by splice donor and acceptor sites, and exon numbering is transcript-specific, which is why clinical reports quote a transcript identifier alongside any exon reference. Exome sequencing targets exonic sequence with capture probes, concentrating effort on the small fraction of the genome where most currently interpretable disease-associated variants lie, but capture is uneven: regions of extreme base composition, recent duplications and some first exons are covered poorly, and non-coding regulatory variants are missed entirely by design. Alternative splicing means that an exon included in one transcript may be skipped in another, so describing a variant as exonic is incomplete without naming the transcript. Exon-level analysis of RNA sequencing data can reveal splicing changes that gene-level summaries conceal.

Practical example

A diagnostic laboratory reports a variant in exon eleven of a gene. A colleague comparing it with an older publication that numbered exons according to a different transcript finds the positions appear to disagree, and the discrepancy resolves once both are mapped to the same reference transcript.

Common misunderstanding

Exons are often equated with protein-coding sequence. Untranslated regions are exonic too, and they carry elements that affect transcript stability and translation. A second misunderstanding is that exome sequencing covers all exons; capture efficiency varies, and a region reported as negative may simply have been sequenced too shallowly to call.
