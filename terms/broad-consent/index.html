Broad consent

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Broad consent is agreement to the future use of samples and data for a described range of research, rather than for one named study only. Participants are told the kinds of research that may be done, the governance that will decide on access, and how they can withdraw. It is not blanket permission for anything whatsoever.

Technical explanation

Broad consent responds to a practical problem: biobanks and genomic datasets are built to support studies that are not yet designed, and re-consenting every participant for each new analysis is often impossible. Its legitimacy rests on governance rather than on specificity. Typical safeguards include an access committee that reviews each proposed use against the terms participants accepted, a public register of approved projects, ethics committee oversight, restrictions on certain categories of research, and ongoing engagement so that communities can raise concerns. Some frameworks add dynamic consent, where participants manage preferences through a portal and are notified of new uses. Criticism focuses on whether consent can be informed when the research is unspecified, on commercial reuse, and on the position of populations with limited influence over the institutions holding their data. Legal regimes differ in whether broad consent is permitted, and in what counts as adequate specification of purposes. A broad consent framework should state explicitly whether data may leave the country of collection and whether commercial entities may obtain access.

Practical example

A national biobank asks participants to agree to health-related research generally, with an access committee including lay members reviewing applications. A later proposal to study a stigmatising condition falls outside what participants were told, so the committee refers it back and the study team undertakes specific engagement and a separate consent process before proceeding.

Common misunderstanding

Broad consent is sometimes described as consent to anything. The scope is bounded by what participants were told and by the governance that enforces it. Conversely, critics sometimes treat it as inherently invalid. Whether it is adequate depends on the quality of the oversight, transparency about who obtains access, and the ability of participants and communities to object.
