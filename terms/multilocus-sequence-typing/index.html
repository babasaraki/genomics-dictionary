Multilocus sequence typing

MLST

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Multilocus sequence typing, abbreviated MLST, is a way of labelling bacterial strains by reading the sequence of a small set of housekeeping genes, usually around seven. Each distinct sequence of a gene gets a number, and the combination of numbers defines a sequence type. Because the labels are just numbers, laboratories anywhere can compare strains without exchanging the sequences themselves.

Technical explanation

Multilocus sequence typing characterises isolates by the alleles present at several conserved housekeeping loci chosen to be under weak selection and distributed around the chromosome. Each unique allele sequence receives an arbitrary integer in a central database, and the ordered set of allele numbers constitutes the sequence type. Related sequence types sharing most alleles are grouped into clonal complexes. The scheme was designed for portability, giving an unambiguous, database-backed nomenclature that is comparable between laboratories and over decades, which makes it valuable for long-term population biology and for international comparison. Its resolution is deliberately coarse: because only a few kilobases are examined, isolates within one outbreak usually share a sequence type, so MLST can exclude relatedness but rarely confirms a transmission link. Recombination in some species means that single allele differences do not correspond to single mutations, so allele-based distances are not substitution counts. Modern practice extracts sequence types in silico from whole-genome data, preserving continuity with historical collections while allowing higher-resolution analyses on the same sequences.

Practical example

A student working on meningococcal isolates archived over twenty years derives sequence types computationally from newly generated genome data. Comparing them with the historical records shows that one clonal complex has persisted throughout, while another appears only after a particular decade. The genome data then support higher-resolution analysis within the persistent complex, which the seven-locus scheme cannot resolve.

Common misunderstanding

MLST is sometimes expected to prove that two patients were part of the same outbreak. Sharing a sequence type means two isolates are of the same broad strain type, and common sequence types are carried by very many unrelated isolates worldwide. Identity at seven loci is weak evidence of recent transmission; differing sequence types, by contrast, argue strongly against it.
