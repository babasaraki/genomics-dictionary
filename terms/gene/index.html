Gene

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for making a product the cell uses, most often a protein but sometimes a functional RNA molecule. Each gene sits at a particular place on a chromosome and usually includes signals that control when and where it is switched on. People carry two copies of most genes, one inherited from each parent.

Technical explanation

A gene is conventionally defined as a genomic interval that is transcribed and gives rise to one or more functional products. In practice the boundaries are set by annotation: a gene model in a database such as Ensembl lists exons, introns, transcription start sites and alternative transcripts, and these models are revised as evidence accumulates. Protein-coding genes are a minority of annotated human genes; the catalogue also includes RNA genes and pseudogenes. Alternative splicing, alternative promoters and alternative polyadenylation mean that one locus can yield several transcripts with different coding potential, so a gene name alone is an imprecise description of what is actually expressed. Clinically this matters because variant interpretation depends on which transcript is treated as canonical, and laboratories therefore quote a specific transcript identifier alongside nomenclature for any reported variant. Gene names also change: approved symbols are curated and historical synonyms persist in older literature. A further limitation is that assigning function to a gene usually rests on experiments in particular cell types or model organisms, so inferences about its role in human tissue should be stated as inference rather than fact.

Practical example

A diagnostic laboratory reporting a variant in a cardiomyopathy panel writes the gene symbol, the reference transcript and the variant in standard nomenclature. A referring clinician who sees only the gene symbol cannot tell which transcript was used, and because exon numbering differs between transcripts, the transcript identifier is what makes the report reproducible in another laboratory.

Common misunderstanding

Many readers picture one gene producing one protein and one trait. Most genes produce several transcripts, most traits involve many genes together with environment, and a single gene can contribute to several unrelated phenotypes. Describing a gene as the gene for a disease is similarly misleading, since the gene exists in everyone and only particular variants within it are associated with disease.
