Antimicrobial resistance

AMR

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Antimicrobial resistance, abbreviated AMR, is the ability of bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites to survive medicines that would normally stop them. The microbe becomes resistant, not the person or animal being treated. Resistance arises through genetic change or through genes acquired from other microbes, and it spreads when resistant organisms are passed on or when resistance genes move between them.

Technical explanation

Antimicrobial resistance arises by several mechanisms: chromosomal mutation altering a drug target, acquisition of genes encoding drug-modifying enzymes such as beta-lactamases, efflux pumps that expel the drug, reduced permeability, and target protection or bypass. Selection pressure from antimicrobial use in human medicine, agriculture and aquaculture favours resistant organisms, while horizontal gene transfer on plasmids, transposons and integrons spreads determinants across species. Genomic methods complement phenotypic susceptibility testing by identifying known resistance genes and mutations from a sequenced isolate, often within a day of culture, and by tracking the spread of resistant lineages between hospitals, communities and countries. Genotype-to-phenotype prediction is good for some organism and drug combinations and poor for others, because expression level, gene copy number, regulatory changes and unknown mechanisms all modify the result. Absence of a known determinant therefore does not establish susceptibility, and laboratory susceptibility does not guarantee clinical response. Resistance is also not a static property of a species, so surveillance must be continuous and locally grounded; patterns differ markedly between settings and cannot be imported from elsewhere.

Practical example

A district hospital laboratory sequences bloodstream isolates of Escherichia coli collected over a year. Most carry an extended-spectrum beta-lactamase gene on a similar plasmid, and the isolates belong to several unrelated lineages. This pattern suggests that the plasmid is spreading between lineages rather than one resistant clone spreading between patients, which points prescribing and infection prevention efforts in different directions.

Common misunderstanding

A widespread error is to say that a person has become resistant to an antibiotic. Resistance is a property of the microbial population, not of the patient. A second error is to treat resistance genes found by sequencing as proof of treatment failure; prediction from genotype is imperfect and must be interpreted with phenotypic testing and the clinical picture.
