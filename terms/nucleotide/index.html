Nucleotide

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A nucleotide is the basic building block of DNA and RNA. Each one has three parts: a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base. Nucleotides join end to end to form long chains, and the order of their bases carries genetic information. DNA uses the bases adenine, cytosine, guanine and thymine, while RNA uses uracil in place of thymine.

Technical explanation

Nucleotides consist of a pentose sugar, deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA, linked to a phosphate and to a purine or pyrimidine base. Adenine and guanine are purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines. Polymerisation proceeds through phosphodiester bonds between the three-prime hydroxyl of one sugar and the five-prime phosphate of the next, giving each strand a defined directionality that governs how polymerases and nucleases act. Free nucleoside triphosphates serve both as substrates for synthesis and as energy carriers in the cell. Sequencing chemistries exploit nucleotide chemistry directly: reversible terminators halt extension after each incorporation, while chain-terminating dideoxynucleotides underpin Sanger sequencing, and nanopore methods instead read the ionic current perturbation caused by passing bases. Nucleotide counts provide the standard units for genome description, with base pairs for double-stranded DNA and bases for single-stranded molecules. Chemical modification of bases, such as cytosine methylation or oxidative damage, changes behaviour without changing the nominal sequence and can be misread as variation if not handled deliberately.

Practical example

A technician preparing a sequencing library checks fragment size and the concentration of input DNA in nanograms. Too little input forces extra amplification cycles, which increases duplicate reads and can introduce errors that resemble low-frequency variants, so the laboratory sets a minimum input threshold rather than processing every sample regardless of quantity.

Common misunderstanding

Nucleotide, nucleoside and base are often used interchangeably. A nucleoside is sugar plus base; adding one or more phosphates makes a nucleotide; the base alone is only the nitrogenous ring system. Confusing them matters when reading reagent specifications, where dideoxynucleotides, deoxynucleotides and nucleoside analogues behave very differently in a reaction.
