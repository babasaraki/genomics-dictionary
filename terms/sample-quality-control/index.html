Sample quality control

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Sample quality control is the set of checks that confirm material is good enough to proceed at each stage of a laboratory workflow. Typical checks measure how much nucleic acid is present, how pure it is, how intact it is, and whether the library has the expected fragment sizes. Failing samples are repeated or reported as uninformative.

Technical explanation

Quality control in a sequencing workflow is layered. At the sample stage, fluorometric quantification measures nucleic acid specifically, whereas absorbance measures anything that absorbs at the same wavelength and is therefore a purity indicator rather than a reliable quantity; integrity is assessed by automated electrophoresis, giving metrics such as an RNA integrity number or a DNA integrity score. At the library stage, fragment size distribution and molar concentration determine loading and reveal adapter dimers or over-amplification. After sequencing, read-level metrics cover base quality by cycle, adapter content, duplicate rate, base composition, mapping rate, depth and breadth against the target, and for human samples, checks of sex concordance and of identity against an independent genotype. Thresholds must be set for the assay rather than copied between assays, since acceptable input for a high-depth panel differs from that for shallow whole-genome sequencing. The essential discipline is that failing checks lead to repetition or to an explicit uninformative result; silently reporting a sample that fell below threshold converts a known limitation into an unrecognised error.

Practical example

A laboratory establishing an RNA sequencing service sets a minimum integrity score for accepted samples. Early batches from a collaborating clinic fail consistently, and reviewing collection practice shows a delay before stabilisation of the specimens. Changing the collection protocol, rather than lowering the threshold, resolves the problem for subsequent batches.

Common misunderstanding

Quality control is sometimes seen as a hurdle to be cleared so that analysis can start. Its purpose is to decide which results can be trusted and which cannot. A sample that passes every check can still produce poor data in a difficult region, and a sample that fails one metric may still be informative for a narrower question, provided the limitation is stated.
