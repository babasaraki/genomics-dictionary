Phylodynamics

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Phylodynamics is the study of how the shape of a pathogen's evolutionary tree is produced by the way the infection spreads. Because each transmission event leaves a trace in the genomes, the pattern of branching can be used to estimate things epidemiologists care about, such as whether an epidemic is growing, when it started and how populations are connected.

Technical explanation

Phylodynamic inference links genealogies estimated from time-stamped sequences to epidemiological models. Coalescent approaches treat the tree as the outcome of a population process and estimate effective population size through time, which under assumptions tracks the number of infections. Birth-death approaches model transmission, recovery and sampling directly and can estimate a reproduction number and sampling proportion. Structured models add discrete locations or host types, allowing estimation of migration rates between regions or spillover between animal and human hosts. Inference is usually Bayesian and computationally intensive, and results are conditional on the model: unmodelled sampling bias, population structure, superspreading or recombination can bias estimates substantially. Dense sampling in one setting and sparse sampling elsewhere is the most common practical problem, and it tends to make well-sampled regions look like sources. Estimates of effective population size are not head counts of infected people; they are a genetic quantity sensitive to variance in the number of onward infections. Phylodynamic outputs are most useful when presented with credible intervals and compared with independent surveillance indicators.

Practical example

A research group analyses rabies virus sequences from dogs and from wildlife collected across several districts. A structured model estimates migration rates between host groups and between districts. The results suggest that most human exposure risk is sustained by circulation within the dog population, supporting an argument for maintaining dog vaccination coverage rather than focusing on wildlife control.

Common misunderstanding

Effective population size from a coalescent analysis is frequently reported as the number of infected people. It is not a case count. It is a measure of genetic diversity that depends on the variance in onward transmission, so an epidemic dominated by superspreading can show a small effective size while infecting many people. Case data remain necessary.
