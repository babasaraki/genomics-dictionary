Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium

HWE

Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium describes the simple relationship expected between how often each allele occurs and how often each genotype occurs, in a large population that mates at random and in which nothing is disturbing the alleles. If the two alleles have frequencies p and q, the expected genotype proportions are p squared, two pq and q squared. Real populations only approximate this, so it serves as a baseline for comparison.

Technical explanation

The Hardy-Weinberg principle states that in an idealised population, infinitely large, randomly mating, closed to migration and free of selection and new mutation at the locus, allele frequencies remain constant across generations and genotype frequencies follow p squared, two pq and q squared. No human population satisfies those conditions exactly, which is precisely what makes the model useful: departures flag something worth investigating. In practice a chi-squared or exact test of Hardy-Weinberg proportions is applied to every variant as a routine quality-control step in array and sequencing studies. A marked deficit of heterozygotes usually reflects a technical fault such as poor probe performance, allele dropout, sample contamination or the merging of data generated on different platforms, rather than biology. Genuine biological causes include population structure, close kinship within the sample and strong selection. Two cautions apply: the test is normally run in controls only, because disease-associated loci can depart legitimately in cases, and with millions of variants some will fail by chance, so thresholds must allow for multiple testing.

Practical example

A team genotyping a case-control cohort tests every variant for Hardy-Weinberg proportions among the control samples. A cluster of failures maps to a single genotyping plate. Checking the laboratory records reveals a DNA extraction batch of poor quality. Those samples are re-extracted and re-genotyped, after which the failures disappear and the overall variant pass rate returns to the expected level.

Common misunderstanding

People often treat a failed Hardy-Weinberg test as proof that a locus is under selection. In genotyping data the usual explanation is technical: a badly performing probe, contaminated DNA, or related individuals in the sample. Equally, passing the test does not certify that genotype calls are correct, because systematic errors can preserve the expected proportions.
