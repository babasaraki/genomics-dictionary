Genome-wide association study

GWAS

Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

A genome-wide association study, or GWAS, tests hundreds of thousands to millions of positions across the genome to see whether any version of a position occurs more often in people with a trait than in people without it. It scans the genome without a prior hypothesis about which gene matters, and it reports statistical associations rather than proven causes.

Technical explanation

A GWAS regresses a trait on genotype at each variant, adjusting for covariates such as age, sex and principal components capturing ancestry, then compares results with a stringent significance threshold that accounts for the effective number of independent tests. Contemporary analyses use linear or logistic mixed models to handle relatedness and residual structure, and combine cohorts by meta-analysis of summary statistics. Quality control is substantial: call-rate filters, Hardy-Weinberg checks in controls, frequency filters, sex and relatedness checks, and inspection of genomic inflation. Most signals for common traits fall in non-coding sequence, have small individual effect sizes, and tag an interval through linkage disequilibrium rather than pinpointing a causal base, so fine-mapping and functional work are needed to move from locus to mechanism. The most consequential limitation is representation: participant cohorts have been dominated by people of European ancestry, which restricts discovery of variation common elsewhere and degrades the portability of findings and derived scores to other populations.

Practical example

A consortium studying a common disease meta-analyses several cohorts and reports twenty loci passing the genome-wide threshold. One falls in a gene desert with no obvious candidate nearby. Rather than naming the closest gene as causal, the team reports the associated interval and prioritises chromatin interaction and expression data to work out which gene the region actually regulates.

Common misunderstanding

A GWAS hit is frequently reported as the gene for a trait. The output is an associated region, usually non-coding, usually of small effect, and usually correlated with many neighbouring variants. Naming the nearest gene is a labelling convention, not evidence of mechanism, and an association observed across a cohort does not establish causation in any individual.
