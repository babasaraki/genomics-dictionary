Genetic ancestry

Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Genetic ancestry describes where in the past the segments of a person's DNA came from, reconstructed by comparing their genome with samples from many populations. It is a statement about lines of descent, estimated with uncertainty. It is not the same as race, nationality, ethnicity or the culture someone was raised in, all of which are social categories that people define and claim for themselves.

Technical explanation

Genetic ancestry is inferred from patterns of allele sharing using principal component analysis, model-based clustering, or local ancestry inference that assigns each chromosomal segment to a probable source population. Every estimate is relative to the reference panels supplied: proportions shift when panels change, and source populations absent from the panel cannot be detected. Ancestry is continuous and segmental rather than categorical, since one genome carries segments with different histories, and continental labels are coarse summaries imposed on gradients. In research and diagnostic genomics, ancestry estimates are used to control for population structure in association testing, to select appropriate allele frequency references, and to judge whether a polygenic score or a variant classification transfers to the person being tested. Treating self-reported race or nationality as a proxy for genetic ancestry is a documented source of error in both directions. Communication of ancestry results also carries a long history of misuse, which is why entries on scientific racism remain relevant to how such findings are presented.

Practical example

A diagnostic laboratory interpreting a variant needs an appropriate allele frequency reference. Rather than relying on the nationality written on the request form, the team estimates genetic ancestry from the sequencing data itself and selects the reference subset accordingly, recording in the report that the estimate is a statistical inference with uncertainty, not a label applied to the person.

Common misunderstanding

Ancestry percentages are often read as a precise statement of identity, or as membership of a biologically distinct group. They are model outputs that depend on which reference populations were included and how those were sampled. Human genetic variation is largely continuous, and far more variation exists within any labelled group than between such groups.
