Positive predictive value

PPV

Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Positive predictive value, abbreviated PPV, is the proportion of positive test results that are correct. It answers the question a person actually asks: given that my result is positive, how likely is it that the condition is really present? Unlike sensitivity and specificity, it depends strongly on how common the condition is in the group being tested.

Technical explanation

Positive predictive value is derived from sensitivity, specificity and prevalence, and the dependence on prevalence is the crucial feature. A test with excellent characteristics applied to a condition that is rare in the tested group will generate many more false positives than true positives, because the small number of affected individuals is swamped by the much larger number of unaffected ones, each with a small chance of a false positive result. This is why screening and diagnostic contexts are not interchangeable, and why predictive value must be quoted for a defined population rather than as an intrinsic property of an assay. Negative predictive value is the mirror quantity. The same arithmetic governs genomic applications: cell-free DNA screening in pregnancy, population screening for rare conditions, incidental findings from broad sequencing, and the prioritisation of rare variants in an exome all show high false positive fractions when the prior probability is low. Predictive value is also sensitive to how the tested group was selected, so performance measured in a high-risk referral cohort will not carry over to unselected testing.

Practical example

A screening test with high sensitivity and specificity is applied to a condition that is uncommon in the population screened. Of a hundred positive results, only a minority correspond to genuinely affected pregnancies. The programme therefore describes the result as a screening indication that requires confirmation by a diagnostic test, not as a diagnosis.

Common misunderstanding

A test described as ninety-nine per cent accurate is widely assumed to mean a positive result is almost certainly correct. For a rare condition the opposite can hold, with most positives being false. Accuracy figures quoted without the prevalence of the tested population cannot tell anyone what their own positive result means.
