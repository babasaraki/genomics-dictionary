False discovery rate

FDR

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

The false discovery rate, abbreviated FDR, is the expected proportion of mistakes among the results a study declares positive. If a list of one hundred differentially expressed genes is reported at a five per cent false discovery rate, about five of them are expected to be false findings. It describes the quality of a list rather than the status of any single item.

Technical explanation

The false discovery rate reframes multiple testing: instead of avoiding any false positive, it accepts a controlled fraction of them in exchange for much greater power. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure ranks the p-values, compares each with a threshold that increases with rank, and declares significant all tests up to the largest one that passes. Adjusted values produced this way are often called q-values, and tools for expression analysis report them routinely. Methods that estimate the proportion of true null hypotheses can increase power further. Three points are commonly missed in practice. The guarantee is an average over the whole list, so it says nothing about whether a particular gene is a true finding, and the gene at the threshold is much more likely to be a false positive than the top-ranked one. The procedure assumes p-values are correctly calibrated, which fails when the underlying model is misspecified or batch structure is ignored, in which case the nominal rate is meaningless. And filtering or selecting tests after inspecting the data invalidates the control.

Practical example

An analysis of a bacterial transcriptomics experiment yields two hundred genes at a five per cent false discovery rate. The team treats this as a screening list of which roughly ten entries are expected to be spurious, and designs confirmatory assays for the handful of candidates that are both strongly ranked and biologically plausible, rather than claiming all two hundred.

Common misunderstanding

An adjusted value of 0.05 is often read as a five per cent chance that this particular gene is a false positive. It is not a per-gene probability; it is a property of the whole list at that threshold. Items near the cut-off are much less reliable than items far above it, even though they share the same label.
