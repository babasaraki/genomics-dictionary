Zoonosis

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

A zoonosis is an infection that can pass from animals to people. Rabies, brucellosis, Lassa fever and many others are zoonoses. Some spread only from animals to individual people, while others, once introduced, can then spread between people. Genomic methods help identify the animal source, estimate when the jump happened and distinguish repeated spillovers from sustained human transmission.

Technical explanation

Zoonotic infections originate in a non-human animal reservoir and reach humans through direct contact, bites, vectors, food or contaminated environments. Spillover depends on pathogen characteristics such as receptor usage and host range, on reservoir ecology and population density, and on human activities including land conversion, livestock intensification, hunting and wildlife trade. Genomic approaches contribute in several ways: sequencing isolates from humans and from candidate reservoir or intermediate hosts to test relatedness, dating common ancestors with molecular clock methods, and applying structured phylodynamic models to estimate cross-species transmission rates. Metagenomic sequencing supports discovery of previously uncharacterised agents in both humans and animals. Inference is constrained by reservoir sampling, which is usually far sparser than human sampling, so an apparent host jump may be mediated by an unsampled species, and genetic similarity between viruses in two hosts does not by itself establish the direction of transfer. Attribution of a spillover to a specific animal population or trade practice therefore requires ecological and epidemiological evidence alongside sequence data, and should avoid stigmatising communities or livelihoods.

Practical example

A series of human Lassa fever cases is investigated in a rural district. Rodents trapped in and around affected households are screened, and the viral sequences from several rodents cluster with the human sequences but differ from one another. The pattern is more consistent with repeated independent spillovers from the local rodent population than with person-to-person spread.

Common misunderstanding

Zoonotic origin is sometimes taken to mean that each human case came from an animal. Once a pathogen is established in people, most cases arise from human-to-human transmission even though the reservoir is animal. The reverse error is also common: a shared sequence in an animal does not prove that animal was the source, as both may have acquired it from a third host.
