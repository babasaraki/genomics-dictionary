Heritability

Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

Heritability is the share of the differences between people in a trait, within a particular population and set of environments, that can be attributed to differences in their genes. It is a property of a group rather than of an individual, and it changes when the population or the environment changes. A high value does not mean a trait is fixed or beyond modification.

Technical explanation

Narrow-sense heritability is the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by additive genetic variance; broad-sense heritability also includes dominance and interaction effects. Classical estimates come from twin and family designs that partition observed resemblance, while SNP-based heritability is estimated among unrelated individuals using genomic relationship matrices, or from GWAS summary statistics by LD score regression. These estimators answer related but distinct questions, and SNP-based values are generally lower than twin-based ones, the so-called missing heritability gap, because common genotyped variants do not capture rare variation or every source of familial resemblance. Every estimate is conditional on the population sampled and the range of environments present: narrow the environmental variation and heritability rises even though the underlying biology is unchanged. Estimates can also be inflated by shared environment, assortative mating and residual population structure. Crucially, heritability places no limit on how much an environmental intervention can shift a trait, and heritability within groups says nothing about the causes of differences between groups.

Practical example

A seminar compares a twin-based heritability estimate for a common trait with an estimate derived from genotype data in unrelated adults. The genomic value is considerably lower. Rather than treating one as wrong, the group examines what each design captures and why cohorts recruited under different conditions can yield different values for the same trait.

Common misunderstanding

A heritability of seventy per cent is often read as meaning that seventy per cent of a person's trait is caused by their genes. It is a statement about variance between people in a sampled population, not a split within any individual. It carries no implication that differences between groups are genetic, nor that intervention would be futile.
