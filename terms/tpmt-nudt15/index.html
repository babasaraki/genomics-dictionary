TPMT and NUDT15

Pharmacogenomics Published

Definition

TPMT and NUDT15 are two genes whose protein products help the body handle thiopurine medicines, used in some leukaemias, inflammatory bowel disease and immune conditions. People with reduced activity of either protein are at much higher risk of severe suppression of blood cell production. Testing before treatment is established practice in many health systems.

Technical explanation

Thiopurines are prodrugs converted into active nucleotides that incorporate into DNA. TPMT methylates thiopurine metabolites and diverts them from activation, while NUDT15 hydrolyses the active nucleotide before incorporation; reduced function in either raises active metabolite levels and the risk of myelosuppression. The two genes are complementary rather than redundant in clinical terms, and their allele frequencies differ by genetic ancestry: reduced-function TPMT alleles are relatively more frequent in European and African populations, whereas the principal reduced-function NUDT15 allele reaches appreciable frequency in East Asian and some Latin American populations. Testing only one gene therefore leaves a predictable gap for part of the population, which is why current guidance addresses both. Assays may be genotype-based or, for TPMT, enzyme activity-based; activity assays can be confounded by recent transfusion, while genotyping misses untested alleles. Genetic testing reduces but does not remove the need for the blood count monitoring that remains standard during thiopurine treatment.

Practical example

A haematology service introduces testing of both genes before thiopurine treatment rather than TPMT alone, after finding that single-gene testing left patients with the principal reduced-function allele of the second gene unidentified. The laboratory documents which alleles each assay covers, and routine blood count monitoring continues for all patients regardless of the genotype result.

Common misunderstanding

Testing is sometimes believed to remove the risk of toxicity altogether. It identifies an important inherited contribution, but untested alleles, non-genetic factors and interacting medicines also affect outcome, so monitoring remains necessary. Testing only one of the two genes is also inadequate, because the relevant alleles differ between populations.
