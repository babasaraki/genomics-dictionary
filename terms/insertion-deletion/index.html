Insertion or deletion

indel

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

An insertion or deletion, often shortened to indel, is a variant in which extra DNA letters are added or existing letters are removed compared with a reference sequence. Indels range from a single letter to many. Within protein-coding sequence, an indel whose length is not a multiple of three shifts the reading frame and usually disrupts the protein that follows.

Technical explanation

Indels are the second most common class of small variant after substitutions. In coding sequence, in-frame indels add or remove whole codons, whereas frameshifting indels change every downstream codon and typically introduce a premature termination codon, with consequences that depend on whether nonsense-mediated decay is triggered. Detection is harder than for substitutions: reads must be aligned across the gap, and short reads in repetitive or homopolymeric sequence frequently produce ambiguous placements, so haplotype-based callers that locally reassemble reads outperform simple position-based approaches. Representation is also ambiguous, since the same indel can be written at different positions within a repeat, and left-alignment and normalisation are required before variant sets can be compared or merged. Homopolymer-length errors are a known weakness of some sequencing chemistries, including early nanopore data, which has improved substantially but still warrants care. Larger insertions and deletions grade into structural variants, with no universally agreed size boundary, so pipelines state their own thresholds.

Practical example

A bioinformatician merging variant calls from two cohorts finds apparent disagreement at a short repeat. Normalising both call sets to a common left-aligned representation shows that the two laboratories had reported the identical indel in different but equivalent notation, and the apparent discrepancy disappears without any re-sequencing.

Common misunderstanding

Indels are often assumed to be small and therefore unimportant. A single-base insertion in coding sequence can abolish a protein, while a large in-frame deletion may be tolerated, so length alone predicts little. A second error is comparing indel calls between datasets without normalisation, which creates false differences from notation rather than biology.
