One Health

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

One Health is an approach that treats the health of people, animals and the environment as connected, and organises surveillance and response across all three. Many infections and most antimicrobial resistance problems move between these domains, so looking at only one gives an incomplete picture. In practice it means human, veterinary and environmental services sharing data and planning together.

Technical explanation

One Health frames human, animal, plant and ecosystem health as interdependent and calls for coordinated action across sectors that are usually funded and governed separately. In genomics, the approach means generating comparable sequence data from clinical, veterinary, food and environmental sources and analysing them together, which requires shared typing schemes, common metadata standards and agreed data governance. Typical applications include tracing foodborne outbreaks from farm to patient, following resistance determinants between livestock, abattoir workers and hospital patients, and monitoring vector-borne and zoonotic pathogens in wildlife and domestic animals. The main obstacles are practical rather than conceptual: sequencing capacity is unevenly distributed between human and animal health laboratories, sampling frames differ, metadata are often incompatible, and legal mandates for data sharing between ministries may be absent. Attribution across sectors is also easily overstated, because a resistance gene found in both farm animals and patients demonstrates a shared determinant, not the direction of flow, which needs denser sampling and careful analysis. Equitable arrangements covering data ownership and benefit sharing are part of workable cross-sector surveillance.

Practical example

A national programme links sequence data from human Salmonella infections, from poultry sampled at slaughter and from retail meat. Isolates from all three sources are typed with the same scheme and compared in one database. When a cluster spanning the sources appears, the human and veterinary agencies investigate jointly rather than each pursuing a separate enquiry.

Common misunderstanding

One Health is often read as a slogan for better communication between sectors. Its operational content is more specific: comparable methods, shared nomenclature, joint analysis and an agreed governance framework. Without these, sequence data generated by human and animal health laboratories cannot be compared, and cross-sector claims about sources of infection rest on assumption rather than evidence.
