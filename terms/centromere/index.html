Centromere

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A centromere is the constricted region of a chromosome where the two copies produced by DNA replication are held together and where the cell attaches the machinery that pulls them apart during division. Its position gives each chromosome a characteristic shape under the microscope. Centromeres contain long stretches of highly repetitive DNA that are difficult to sequence.

Technical explanation

The centromere is the chromosomal locus at which the kinetochore assembles and spindle microtubules attach, ensuring accurate segregation. In humans, centromeric regions comprise megabases of alpha satellite higher-order repeat arrays, and centromere identity is specified epigenetically by nucleosomes containing the histone variant CENP-A rather than by sequence alone, as shown by neocentromeres that form at non-satellite positions. These repeat arrays defeated short-read assembly for decades and appeared as gaps in reference assemblies until long-read methods produced complete representations. Errors in centromere function cause missegregation and contribute to aneuploidy in germ cells and to chromosomal instability in tumours. Analytically, repetitive centromeric sequence attracts mismapped reads and spurious variant calls, so pipelines routinely exclude these regions using blacklists, with the consequence that variation within them has been systematically understudied rather than shown to be absent.

Practical example

An analyst inspecting apparent variant clusters in a sequencing dataset finds many calls concentrated in satellite repeat regions near centromeres with unusual read depth and mapping quality. Applying the pipeline's region exclusion list removes these artefacts, and the analyst records that variation in those regions is unassessed rather than reported as absent.

Common misunderstanding

Centromeres are often assumed to be defined by their DNA sequence and to sit at the middle of every chromosome. Position varies, including near one end in acrocentric chromosomes, and centromere identity is maintained epigenetically. Treating these regions as uninformative is a further error; they were excluded for technical reasons that newer sequencing methods are now addressing.
