Epigenetics

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Epigenetics is the study of changes that affect how genes are used without altering the underlying DNA sequence. These include chemical marks added to DNA or to the proteins that package it, which influence whether a gene is switched on or off. Such marks differ between tissues and can change over time and with environment, and some persist through cell division.

Technical explanation

Epigenetic mechanisms include DNA methylation, covalent histone modification, chromatin remodelling, non-coding RNA activity and higher-order genome organisation. Together they establish and maintain cell-type-specific gene expression patterns from a single genome, which is why liver and neuron differ despite identical sequence. Epigenetic marks are mitotically heritable in somatic lineages; transmission across generations in humans is largely reset during gametogenesis and early development and remains contested, with imprinted loci as the clearest exception. Measurement techniques target specific layers: bisulphite-based sequencing and arrays for methylation, chromatin immunoprecipitation for histone marks, accessibility assays for open chromatin. Interpretation is constrained by tissue specificity, since blood-derived measurements may not reflect the tissue of interest, and by cell composition, because a mixed sample yields an average that can shift simply because the cell mixture changed. Causality is also hard to establish: an epigenetic difference associated with a disease may be a consequence of it rather than a driver.

Practical example

A study comparing methylation in blood between people with and without a condition finds many differing sites. Because blood cell proportions also differ between the groups, the analysts adjust for estimated cell composition, and a large part of the apparent signal resolves, illustrating why composition is modelled rather than ignored.

Common misunderstanding

Epigenetics is often presented as showing that lifestyle rewrites inheritance. Somatic epigenetic change is well established, but robust evidence for transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans is limited, since most marks are reset in the germ line. A further error is reading an epigenetic association as a cause when it may be a downstream consequence.
