Material transfer agreement

MTA

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

A material transfer agreement, abbreviated MTA, is a contract between institutions setting out the terms on which biological material is sent from one to another. It records what is being transferred, what the recipient may do with it, what happens to anything derived from it, and who may publish or own resulting intellectual property.

Technical explanation

A material transfer agreement is the legal instrument that accompanies physical transfer of samples such as isolates, cell lines, nucleic acid extracts or tissue, and it should be consistent with the consent given by participants, with ethics approval and, where applicable, with access and benefit-sharing permits. Typical clauses cover permitted uses and prohibited uses, onward transfer to third parties, retention and destruction, handling of derivatives and modifications, publication and authorship, intellectual property and commercialisation, liability and biosafety, and governing law. In genomics a further question arises: data generated from the material may outlive the material itself, so the agreement should state whether sequence data may be deposited in public archives and under what access conditions, or the physical and data governance arrangements will conflict. Negotiation often becomes the slowest step in multi-country studies, which is why standardised templates and master agreements between institutions are widely used. Agreements between a well-resourced recipient and a provider with little legal support can be one-sided, so review by the provider's own research office and attention to benefit sharing are important safeguards.

Practical example

A national reference laboratory sends bacterial isolates to a collaborating centre for long-read sequencing. The agreement limits use to the agreed project, requires that the resulting assemblies be returned to the sending laboratory, specifies joint authorship, and states that public deposition will happen only with the sender's agreement and with agreed metadata.

Common misunderstanding

Transfer agreements are often treated as paperwork that lawyers resolve after the science is planned. The terms determine whether data can later be shared, who may publish and who owns derivatives, so settling them late can block publication or deposition. It is also mistaken to assume an agreement covering material automatically covers the data produced from it.
