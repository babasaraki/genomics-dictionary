Cell-free DNA screening

cfDNA screening

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Cell-free DNA screening analyses short fragments of DNA circulating in a person's blood. In pregnancy, a sample from the pregnant person contains fragments from the placenta as well as from their own cells, and the mixture can be analysed to estimate the chance of certain chromosomal conditions in the pregnancy. It is a screening test, not a diagnosis.

Technical explanation

Cell-free DNA fragments are released into plasma as cells die, and in pregnancy a measurable fraction derives from placental trophoblast. Assays either sequence fragments across the genome and compare the counts mapping to each chromosome against expectation, or target selected loci and model allelic ratios. Performance depends strongly on fetal fraction: when it is low, as can occur early in gestation or at higher body weight, the test may fail to produce a result or lose sensitivity. The test performs best for trisomy 21 and less well for other autosomal trisomies, sex chromosome aneuploidies and microdeletions, where positive predictive value falls because the conditions are rarer. Because the signal is placental rather than fetal, confined placental mosaicism, a vanishing twin, maternal copy-number variation and, occasionally, an undiagnosed maternal malignancy can all produce discordant results. A positive screen therefore requires diagnostic confirmation by invasive testing before any irreversible decision, and the same cell-free DNA principle underpins circulating tumour DNA testing in oncology.

Practical example

A prenatal service reports a high-chance cell-free DNA result for trisomy 21. The counsellor explains that the number reported is a probability conditioned on the prior chance at that age and gestation, not a diagnosis, and that confirmation requires chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis. The couple is given time and balanced information before deciding whether to proceed with diagnostic testing.

Common misunderstanding

Marketing language such as non-invasive prenatal testing leads many to treat the result as definitive. It is a screen whose positive predictive value varies with the rarity of the condition, so for uncommon findings most positive results are not confirmed. The placenta, not the fetus, is the source of the signal, which explains part of the discordance.
