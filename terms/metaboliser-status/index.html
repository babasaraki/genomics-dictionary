Metaboliser status

Pharmacogenomics Published

Definition

Metaboliser status is a summary of how quickly a person is predicted to process a particular medicine, based on the pair of gene versions they carry. The usual categories run from poor, through intermediate and normal, to rapid and ultrarapid. It is a prediction from genotype, not a measurement of the drug in the body.

Technical explanation

Metaboliser status is derived by assigning an activity value to each allele in a person's diplotype, summing the values and mapping the total onto a phenotype category through consensus translation tables. Standardised terms replaced older labels such as extensive metaboliser to improve consistency between laboratories. The clinical meaning depends on whether the medicine is active as administered or is a prodrug requiring conversion: reduced metabolism raises exposure to an active drug but lowers the formation of an active metabolite from a prodrug, so the same genotype can imply opposite directions of effect for different medicines. Predicted status is not fixed in practice. Phenoconversion occurs when interacting medicines inhibit or induce the enzyme, or when inflammation, liver disease or age alters its activity, so an observed phenotype may differ from the genotype-predicted one. Genotype also cannot capture alleles the assay did not test, and activity scores are revised as evidence accumulates, so reports should name the assay, the translation table and the version used.

Practical example

A laboratory report gives a predicted intermediate metaboliser phenotype for an enzyme. The patient is also taking a medicine that strongly inhibits the same enzyme. A pharmacist reviewing the record notes that the observed metabolic capacity is likely lower than the genotype alone predicts, and documents this phenoconversion alongside the genotype-based prediction rather than replacing it.

Common misunderstanding

Poor metaboliser is often heard as a defect, and ultrarapid as an advantage. Neither is true in general: whether faster or slower processing raises or lowers drug exposure depends on whether the medicine is active already or must be converted. These are population-frequency categories, not statements that a genome is abnormal.
