Warfarin pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenomics Published

Definition

Warfarin thins the blood and has a narrow margin between too little effect and too much. How much a person needs varies widely, and part of that variation is inherited. Variants in a gene that processes the drug and in a gene encoding its target together explain a meaningful share of the differences in dose requirement.

Technical explanation

Warfarin dose requirement is influenced by CYP2C9, which clears the more active enantiomer, by VKORC1, which encodes the vitamin K epoxide reductase target and whose promoter variant alters expression, and by CYP4F2, which affects vitamin K recycling. Published dosing algorithms combine these genotypes with age, weight, height and interacting medicines, and guidelines describe how genotype may be incorporated into initial dosing. An important caveat concerns genetic ancestry: the variants that predict requirement in populations of European and Asian ancestry explain much less of the variance in populations of African ancestry, where additional CYP2C9 alleles contribute and were absent from early algorithms. Applying an algorithm developed in one population to another can therefore mislead, which is an argument for population-appropriate allele panels rather than for abandoning the approach. Clinically, genotype informs starting dose only; subsequent dosing is guided by coagulation monitoring, and diet, adherence, liver function and interacting medicines remain major determinants of stability.

Practical example

An anticoagulation service reviews a dosing algorithm that uses genotype together with clinical factors, and finds that it was derived mainly in populations of European ancestry. Before adopting it locally, the service checks that the allele panel includes the CYP2C9 alleles that are frequent in the population it serves, and records that coagulation monitoring continues to govern ongoing dosing regardless of genotype.

Common misunderstanding

Genotype-guided dosing is sometimes expected to replace blood monitoring. It informs the starting dose and narrows early uncertainty, while monitoring still governs maintenance. A second error is to assume an algorithm transfers between populations: allele frequencies and the explained variance differ, so panels and algorithms need checking against the population in which they are used.
