Nucleic acid extraction

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Nucleic acid extraction is the process of getting DNA or RNA out of a sample and into a clean solution that later methods can use. Cells are broken open, proteins and other material are removed, and the nucleic acid is captured, washed and released. How well this is done sets a ceiling on everything that follows.

Technical explanation

Extraction releases nucleic acid from cells or virions and separates it from proteins, lipids, polysaccharides and inhibitory compounds. Common formats include silica membrane columns, magnetic beads suited to automation, and organic separation methods. The chemistry chosen determines what is recovered: methods optimised for total nucleic acid recover both DNA and RNA, column methods can deplete very short fragments such as cell-free DNA, and vigorous bead beating needed for tough-walled organisms shears high-molecular-weight DNA that long-read sequencing requires. RNA work adds the constraint that ribonucleases are ubiquitous and active, so cold chain, stabilising reagents and clean technique matter from the moment of collection. Outputs are assessed for yield, purity by absorbance ratios, and integrity by electrophoretic or microfluidic sizing. Known limitations include differential lysis, which skews microbial community composition in metagenomic studies; carry-over of inhibitors such as haem or humic substances that suppress amplification; and loss of material at low input, where the physical adsorption of molecules to plastic becomes significant.

Practical example

A laboratory preparing samples for long-read sequencing finds read lengths much shorter than expected. The extraction protocol in use involved vigorous vortexing and narrow pipette tips, both of which shear long molecules. Switching to a gentle, wide-bore protocol designed for high-molecular-weight DNA increases read length without changing the sequencing chemistry.

Common misunderstanding

Extraction is often treated as a neutral preparatory step that simply makes the sample usable. The method actively shapes the result: it decides which fragment sizes survive, which organisms in a mixture are lysed efficiently, and whether inhibitors remain. A difference between two datasets can originate entirely in the extraction protocol rather than in the biology.
