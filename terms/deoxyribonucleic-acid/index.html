Deoxyribonucleic acid

DNA

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA, is the chemical that carries the inherited instructions used by almost every living organism. It is built from four chemical letters strung along two strands that wind around each other in a double helix. The order of those letters stores information that cells read to build proteins and to decide when each gene is switched on. Cells copy their DNA before dividing, so the instructions pass to the next generation of cells.

Technical explanation

DNA is a polymer of deoxyribonucleotides, each made of a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine, cytosine, guanine and thymine. Sugars and phosphates form the backbone; the bases project inwards and pair specifically, adenine with thymine and cytosine with guanine, so the two antiparallel strands are complementary. That complementarity underlies both semi-conservative replication and every laboratory method that depends on hybridisation, including polymerase chain reaction, probe-based assays and sequencing by synthesis. In eukaryotes most DNA sits in the nucleus, packaged with histones into chromatin and condensed into chromosomes, with a much smaller circular genome in mitochondria. Only a minority of human DNA encodes protein; the remainder includes regulatory elements, non-coding RNA genes, repetitive sequence and segments whose function is still being characterised. A practical limitation is that sequence alone does not reveal function: identifying a stretch of DNA tells you its letters, not whether it is transcribed in a given tissue, nor what the consequence of altering it would be. Chemical modifications such as methylation and the surrounding chromatin state also shape how the same sequence behaves in different cells.

Practical example

A laboratory extracting DNA from whole blood lyses the cells, digests protein, and binds the nucleic acid to a silica column before eluting it in buffer. The scientist then measures absorbance ratios and fragment size to judge purity and integrity, because degraded or protein-contaminated DNA performs poorly in library preparation. Only material passing those checks goes forward to sequencing.

Common misunderstanding

A common belief is that DNA is a fixed blueprint that dictates outcomes directly. Sequence constrains what a cell can make, but gene expression, epigenetic modification, developmental timing and environment all shape the result. It is also wrong to speak of a single normal human DNA sequence: variation between individuals and populations is ordinary, and reference sequences are conventions rather than ideals.
