DNA methylation

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

DNA methylation is the addition of a small chemical group to a DNA base, most often a cytosine followed by a guanine. The sequence itself is unchanged, but the mark influences whether nearby genes are switched on. Patterns of methylation differ between tissues, are copied when cells divide, and change during development, with age and in disease.

Technical explanation

In mammals, methylation occurs predominantly at cytosines in CpG dinucleotides, established by DNA methyltransferases and maintained through replication. Dense methylation of CpG island promoters is generally associated with transcriptional silencing, whereas gene-body methylation shows a different and more complex relationship with expression. Imprinted loci carry parent-of-origin-specific methylation, and X inactivation involves extensive methylation changes. In cancer, promoter hypermethylation can silence tumour suppressor genes while the genome overall becomes hypomethylated, and methylation profiling is used in tumour classification, including for central nervous system tumours. Measurement relies on bisulphite conversion followed by sequencing or array interrogation, or on native detection by long-read platforms that distinguish modified bases directly. Analytical pitfalls include incomplete conversion, probe cross-hybridisation, confounding by cell composition in bulk tissue, and the fact that most reported associations are correlative, so a methylation difference does not establish that it causes the expression difference observed with it.

Practical example

A neuropathology service runs methylation array profiling on a brain tumour whose appearance under the microscope is ambiguous. The profile groups the tumour with a defined methylation class, and this classification is used alongside histology and molecular findings rather than replacing them in the diagnostic discussion.

Common misunderstanding

Methylation is often described simply as switching genes off. The relationship depends on context: promoter methylation is usually repressive, while gene-body methylation is often found in actively transcribed genes. It is also mistaken to treat measured methylation as a change to the genetic code, since the DNA sequence itself is unaltered.
