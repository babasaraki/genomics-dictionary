Batch effect

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

A batch effect is a systematic technical difference between groups of samples that were processed separately. Samples extracted on different days, sequenced on different instruments, or prepared by different staff can differ measurably for reasons that have nothing to do with biology. If those technical groupings line up with the comparison of interest, the artefact is indistinguishable from the biological signal.

Technical explanation

Batch effects arise from reagent lots, extraction protocols, library preparation, flow cells, lane position, storage time and operator. They typically affect many features at once, so they appear as dominant structure in principal component analysis or clustering, often larger than the effect under study. The decisive question is confounding: if all cases were processed in one batch and all controls in another, no statistical method can separate batch from condition, and the study cannot answer its question. Prevention is therefore design, by randomising or balancing samples across batches and recording processing metadata as rigorously as clinical metadata. Where batch is partly confounded, it can be included as a covariate in the model, or removed with dedicated correction methods, and surrogate variable approaches can estimate unmeasured sources of variation. Correction carries its own risk: aggressive removal can delete genuine biological signal that happens to correlate with batch, and applying correction before cross-validation leaks information and inflates apparent performance. Batch effects also afflict single-cell and proteomic data, where they are routinely confused with cell type or subgroup structure.

Practical example

A transcriptomic study of tumour and adjacent tissue finds thousands of differentially expressed genes. Plotting the samples shows they separate first by sequencing run, and every tumour sample was run in the first batch. The analysis cannot be rescued statistically, so the team re-sequences with tumour and adjacent samples interleaved across runs.

Common misunderstanding

Batch effects are often thought to be removable after the fact by a correction method. Correction helps only when batch and the biological comparison are not fully confounded. If every case was processed separately from every control, the two are mathematically inseparable, and applying a correction merely hides the problem behind plausible-looking output.
