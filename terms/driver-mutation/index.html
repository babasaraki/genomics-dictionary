Driver mutation

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A driver mutation is a change in a tumour's DNA that gives the cell a growth advantage and so contributes to the cancer developing or progressing. Only a small number of the changes found in a tumour are drivers. The rest are bystanders that accumulated along the way without helping the tumour grow.

Technical explanation

Drivers are identified statistically and functionally rather than by inspection. Statistical approaches look for genes mutated more often than the local background rate predicts, for clustering of variants at specific codons or domains, for recurrent fusions and for copy-number changes recurring across many tumours; functional evidence comes from cell and animal models and from curated knowledge bases. The driver label is context-dependent, because an alteration that drives one tumour type may be a passenger in another, and it applies to the alteration in a given tumour rather than to the gene in general. Drivers may be clonal, present in all tumour cells and therefore likely to have occurred early, or subclonal, present in part of the tumour and arising later; this distinction matters for therapy, since targeting a subclonal driver leaves other clones unaffected. Limitations include poor power for rare drivers, difficulty distinguishing drivers in regions of high mutation rate, and the fact that non-coding and epigenetic drivers are systematically under-detected by coding-focused analyses.

Practical example

A molecular pathology team analyses a colorectal tumour and finds dozens of somatic variants. Only two fall in genes recurrently altered at the same codons across large tumour series, and one of them is present at a clonal allele fraction. The report highlights these as candidate drivers and lists the remainder as variants without established oncogenic significance.

Common misunderstanding

Because tumours carry many variants, each one is sometimes assumed to contribute to the cancer. Most are passengers with no effect on growth. Equally, driver status is not a property of a gene in isolation: the same alteration can drive one tumour type and mean nothing in another, so context is part of the claim.
