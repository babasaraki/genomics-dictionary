Confidence interval

CI

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

A confidence interval is a range of values, calculated from the data, that expresses how precisely a quantity has been estimated. A ninety-five per cent interval is constructed by a procedure that, repeated across many studies, would contain the true value in ninety-five per cent of them. A wide interval signals an imprecise estimate; a narrow one signals a precise estimate.

Technical explanation

The interval is built around a point estimate using its standard error and a distributional assumption, or by resampling methods where that assumption is doubtful. Its width is driven by sample size and by the variability of the data, and for ratio measures such as odds ratios or hazard ratios it is computed on the logarithmic scale and is therefore asymmetric around the estimate. Reporting intervals rather than p-values alone changes what a reader can judge, because the interval shows the range of effect sizes compatible with the data: an interval spanning no effect but extending to a substantial effect means the study was inconclusive, not that the effect is absent. The strict frequentist interpretation concerns the long-run behaviour of the procedure, not the probability that this particular interval contains the value, which is what a Bayesian credible interval provides. Intervals also inherit every assumption of the model that generated them and make no allowance for bias: confounding, selection effects, batch structure and measurement error shift the estimate without widening the interval.

Practical example

A study reports an odds ratio of 1.4 with a ninety-five per cent interval from 0.9 to 2.1. A summary describing this as no association would be misleading, since the data are equally compatible with a doubling of odds. The authors instead describe the estimate as imprecise and recommend a larger sample before drawing conclusions.

Common misunderstanding

An interval that crosses one, or zero for a difference, is often read as proving there is no effect. It means the data cannot distinguish between no effect and effects of the sizes the interval spans, which may include clinically meaningful ones. A narrow interval around no effect and a wide one are very different results.
