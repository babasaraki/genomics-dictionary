Gene expression

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make a product, usually a protein. It is also the term for how much of that product a cell makes. Expression varies between tissues, between individuals and over time, and it responds to signals, so the same genome produces very different cells.

Technical explanation

Gene expression encompasses transcription, RNA processing, export, translation and the degradation steps that set steady-state abundance. It is most often measured as transcript abundance by RNA sequencing, which involves library preparation, alignment or pseudoalignment to a transcriptome, and quantification, followed by normalisation for sequencing depth and library composition. Differential expression testing uses count-based models with moderated dispersion estimates, and multiple testing correction is essential given the number of genes examined. Design matters more than depth for most questions: biological replication, randomisation of samples across batches and recording of covariates such as collection time, tissue composition and sex protect against confounding, and batch effects can otherwise dominate the signal. Bulk measurements average over cell types, so apparent expression changes may reflect shifts in composition, which single-cell methods can resolve at the cost of sparsity and additional technical noise. Expression differences are correlative; demonstrating that a change in expression causes a phenotype requires perturbation experiments.

Practical example

A group comparing tumour and adjacent tissue processes all tumour samples in one sequencing run and all adjacent samples in another. Because batch is now perfectly confounded with the comparison of interest, no statistical adjustment can separate them, and the study is redesigned with samples randomised across runs before data are generated.

Common misunderstanding

Gene expression results are frequently read as showing that a gene causes a difference. Expression analysis identifies association, and changes may be downstream consequences. A second error is to treat transcript abundance as protein abundance, when the two correlate only moderately because translation and protein turnover are regulated independently.
