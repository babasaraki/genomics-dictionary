Genome assembly

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

Genome assembly is the process of reconstructing the long chromosomes of an organism from the many short or medium-length reads a sequencer produces. Overlapping reads are joined into longer stretches called contigs, which may then be ordered and oriented into scaffolds. The output is a set of sequences that approximates the genome, together with statistics describing how complete and contiguous it is.

Technical explanation

Assembly algorithms fall into two families: overlap-layout-consensus, which compares reads pairwise and suits long reads, and de Bruijn graph methods, which break reads into fixed-length k-mers and suit large numbers of short reads. Both must resolve repeats, and repeats longer than the read length are the fundamental obstacle, since the graph cannot distinguish the possible paths through them. Long reads, linked reads and chromosome conformation data are therefore used to span repetitive structure, and a telomere-to-telomere human assembly became possible only once reads were long and accurate enough to traverse satellite arrays. Assemblies are assessed by contiguity statistics, by the proportion of expected single-copy genes recovered and by agreement with independent data, and polished by mapping reads back to correct residual errors. Limitations are real: haplotypes may be collapsed into a single consensus in a diploid organism, contamination can be incorporated as genuine sequence, and an assembly from a single individual cannot represent the variation of a population, which is the motivation for pangenome references.

Practical example

A national laboratory assembles a fungal pathogen genome using long reads. Early drafts break at several ribosomal repeat arrays, so the team increases read length by adjusting extraction to preserve high-molecular-weight DNA. The new assembly spans the repeats, and the completeness check recovers nearly all expected conserved genes.

Common misunderstanding

A published assembly is easily mistaken for the organism's finished genome. Every assembly is a model containing collapsed repeats, gaps, residual base errors and sometimes contaminant sequence, and it reflects one or a few individuals. Describing an assembly as the genome of a species obscures both its technical limits and the diversity it cannot capture.
