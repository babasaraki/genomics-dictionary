Molecular clock

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

A molecular clock is the idea that genetic changes accumulate in a population at a roughly steady average rate, so the number of differences between two sequences can be used to estimate how long ago they shared an ancestor. In outbreak work, clocks let analysts put dates on the branching points of a tree and estimate when an outbreak began.

Technical explanation

Molecular clock models convert genetic distance into time by estimating a substitution rate, usually expressed as substitutions per site per year. A strict clock assumes one rate across the whole tree; relaxed clocks allow the rate to vary among branches, drawn from a distribution, which fits real data better for most pathogens. For time-stamped pathogen sequences, rates and dates are co-estimated from sampling dates, either by root-to-tip regression as a quick diagnostic or jointly within a Bayesian framework. Reliable estimates require measurable evolution across the sampling window: if too little change has accumulated, dates are driven by the prior rather than the data. Rate estimates also depend on timescale, because short-term rates measured within an outbreak typically exceed long-term substitution rates once slightly deleterious changes have been removed by purifying selection. Other complications include recombination, variable selection pressure, within-host diversity and sampling bias. Credible intervals around estimated dates are often wide and should be reported rather than quoting a single date.

Practical example

A team investigating an unexpected cluster of hepatitis cases estimates the date of the most recent common ancestor of the sequences. The estimate falls several months before the first reported case, with a wide credible interval. This prompts a search for earlier unrecognised infections in stored diagnostic samples, rather than an assumption that transmission began when surveillance first noticed it.

Common misunderstanding

Clock dates are often quoted as if they were observed facts, such as the exact week a virus entered a country. They are model-based estimates with uncertainty, and they date the common ancestor of the sampled sequences, not the true first infection. Unsampled earlier cases always push the real introduction back before the estimated node.
