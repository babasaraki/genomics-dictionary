Tumour suppressor gene

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A tumour suppressor gene normally restrains cell growth, repairs damaged DNA or triggers the death of a damaged cell. Losing the function of such a gene removes a brake, making it easier for a tumour to develop. Because cells carry two copies of most genes, both usually have to be disabled before the effect is felt.

Technical explanation

Tumour suppressor genes act through loss of function, and the classical two-hit model holds that both alleles must be inactivated for the restraining function to be lost. In hereditary cancer predisposition the first hit is a germline pathogenic variant present in every cell, and the second is a somatic event in the tissue concerned, frequently loss of heterozygosity, a somatic variant or promoter hypermethylation. This explains why carriers develop tumours earlier and more often than non-carriers while still requiring a further event. The model is a simplification: haploinsufficiency, in which one functional copy is not enough, and dominant-negative alleles both occur, and TP53 provides well-described examples of missense alleles that behave differently from simple null alleles. Functional categories include DNA damage response genes, cell cycle checkpoint regulators and genes controlling apoptosis. Therapeutically, lost function cannot be inhibited directly, so strategies instead exploit the resulting vulnerability, as with synthetic lethal approaches in tumours deficient in homologous recombination repair.

Practical example

A person carries a germline pathogenic variant in one copy of a DNA repair gene. Sequencing of a tumour that later develops shows that the remaining copy has been lost in the tumour tissue, while blood retains both the variant and the intact allele. The tumour therefore lacks the repair function entirely, whereas the person's other tissues do not.

Common misunderstanding

The idea that a tumour suppressor variant means cancer is inevitable is common but mistaken. A germline variant supplies only the first hit; a second, somatic event is usually required in a specific cell, and whether and when that happens varies. Carriers face raised risk and are offered surveillance, not a certain diagnosis.
