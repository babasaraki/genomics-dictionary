Mutational signature

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A mutational signature is a characteristic pattern of DNA changes left behind by a particular process. Different causes of damage, and different failures of repair, favour different kinds of change in different sequence contexts. Reading the pattern across a tumour genome can suggest which processes have been at work in that tumour's history.

Technical explanation

Signature analysis classifies somatic mutations into categories, most commonly the ninety-six classes defined by each single base substitution together with its immediate flanking bases, and decomposes the observed counts into a combination of reference signatures catalogued from large tumour series. Parallel catalogues exist for doublet substitutions, small insertions and deletions and structural rearrangements, and copy-number signatures have been proposed. Some signatures have well-supported aetiologies, including ultraviolet exposure, tobacco smoke, defective mismatch repair, polymerase proofreading defects and homologous recombination deficiency, while many remain of unknown cause. Clinically, signatures can point towards an underlying repair defect and so complement targeted biomarker assays. The method has real limitations: decomposition is an ill-posed problem in which several combinations can fit the same data, signatures with similar profiles are hard to separate, accuracy depends on having enough mutations, which makes small panels unsuitable, and attribution of a signature to an exposure is an inference about process rather than proof of what an individual encountered.

Practical example

A laboratory analysing whole-genome data from a tumour finds a substitution pattern dominated by a signature associated with defective mismatch repair, consistent with the high mutation count and the instability result obtained separately. The finding is reported as supporting evidence for the repair defect rather than as an independent diagnosis, and the number of mutations available for fitting is stated.

Common misunderstanding

Signatures are sometimes read as a record of what a person was exposed to, so a smoking-associated signature is taken as proof of smoking history. Signatures identify mutational processes, and several processes can produce overlapping patterns. They describe the biochemistry that generated the mutations, not a verified account of an individual's exposures.
