Haplotype

Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A haplotype is a set of DNA variants that sit on the same copy of a chromosome and are usually inherited together as a unit. Because we carry two copies of most chromosomes, one from each parent, every person has two haplotypes across any region. Knowing which variants travel together often tells you more than knowing the list of variants alone.

Technical explanation

A haplotype is a specific combination of alleles along a single chromosome copy. Routine short-read genotyping returns unphased genotypes: it records that a person is heterozygous at two positions without stating whether the two alternative alleles lie on the same chromosome copy or on opposite ones. Resolving that arrangement is phasing, performed statistically against a reference panel of known haplotypes, or directly from long reads, linked reads or family trios. Haplotypes matter because function often depends on configuration. Two loss-of-function variants in the same gene disable both copies when they lie on opposite chromosomes but leave one intact copy when they lie on the same one. Haplotype definitions also underpin pharmacogenomic star alleles, HLA typing and imputation, which works by matching an observed genotype pattern to panel haplotypes. The limiting factor is the panel itself: statistical phasing is least accurate for rare variants and for individuals whose ancestry is poorly represented among the reference haplotypes.

Practical example

A pharmacogenomics laboratory reports a CYP2C19 result. Two variants are detected, and the interpretation depends entirely on phase: on the same chromosome copy they define a single star allele, on opposite copies they imply two different alleles and a different predicted metaboliser status. The laboratory resolves the configuration with a long-read assay before issuing the report.

Common misunderstanding

Haplotype is often used as a synonym for genotype. A genotype lists which alleles a person carries at a position; a haplotype states which chromosome copy those alleles sit on. Haplotype is also confused with haplogroup, which is a broader label for a branch of the mitochondrial or Y-chromosome tree grouping related haplotypes together.
