Secondary findings

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Secondary findings are results that a laboratory looks for deliberately, but that are unrelated to the reason the test was requested. When a person has their exome or genome sequenced for one condition, a defined list of other genes may also be examined because finding a variant in them could prevent serious harm. Consent is sought beforehand.

Technical explanation

Secondary findings are distinguished from incidental findings by intent: they come from a predefined gene list that the laboratory actively interrogates, whereas incidental findings surface unexpectedly. Professional bodies maintain such lists, selected mainly on actionability, so that most entries concern inherited cancer predisposition, cardiovascular conditions including cardiomyopathies and arrhythmia syndromes, and a small number of metabolic disorders. Only variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic are reported; uncertain variants in these genes are not returned, because the balance of benefit and harm shifts once certainty falls. Interpretation is complicated by ascertainment: penetrance estimates for these genes come largely from affected families, so a variant found in someone sequenced for an unrelated reason may carry lower risk than those figures imply. Returning such results has resource consequences for confirmatory testing, surveillance and cascade testing in relatives, which many health systems are not funded to absorb. Consent frameworks should allow a person to decline, and arrangements for children require particular care where onset is in adulthood.

Practical example

A child is sequenced to investigate a developmental disorder, and the parents have consented to analysis of the secondary findings gene list. A pathogenic variant in a cardiomyopathy gene is identified in one parent during trio analysis. The service arranges cardiology assessment for that parent and discusses testing with adult relatives, separately from the original diagnostic question.

Common misunderstanding

Secondary findings are often described as accidental discoveries. They are the planned output of a defined gene list that the laboratory chose to examine, which is why consent is taken in advance and why a person may decline them. They are also not a general health check: the list is small and deliberately restricted to actionable conditions.
