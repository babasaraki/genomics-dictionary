Benefit sharing

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Benefit sharing means that the people and communities who contribute samples, data or effort to research receive a fair share of what the research produces. Benefits can be financial, but more often they are practical: training, equipment, authorship, local analysis capacity, access to results, or affordable access to any product that is developed.

Technical explanation

Benefit sharing is a requirement in international instruments on access to genetic resources and a recurring theme in research ethics guidance. In genomics it addresses a structural imbalance: samples and sequence data frequently originate in low- and middle-income settings while analysis, publication, intellectual property and commercial exploitation occur elsewhere. Concrete mechanisms include co-design of research questions, meaningful co-authorship rather than acknowledgement, placement of data in repositories that local investigators can use, transfer of sequencing and bioinformatics capacity, support for local ethics and governance infrastructure, provision for continued access to any intervention shown to work, and terms written into material transfer and data access agreements rather than left to goodwill. Benefits should be negotiated before samples move, with the communities concerned rather than only with national authorities, and documented. Difficulties include defining the relevant community, avoiding inducements that undermine voluntary consent, valuing contributions that are not easily priced, and sustaining commitments after a grant ends. Evaluation of whether promised benefits materialised is rarely built into projects but is necessary for accountability.

Practical example

A consortium studying drug-resistant tuberculosis in several African countries agrees at the outset that sequencing will be performed in national laboratories rather than shipped abroad, that analysts from each site will be trained and included as authors, and that national programmes will receive the resistance profiles for their own isolates in a usable format.

Common misunderstanding

Benefit sharing is often reduced to a payment or a line in a budget. Most of what participating institutions value is non-monetary: capacity, authorship, data access and a say in the research agenda. Treating it as a transaction completed at recruitment also misses the point, since the main benefits accrue during analysis, publication and any later commercial development.
