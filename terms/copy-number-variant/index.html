Copy number variant

CNV

Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A copy number variant, or CNV, is a stretch of DNA present in a different number of copies in one person than in another, either deleted or duplicated one or more times. Copy number differences are an ordinary part of human variation. Some change how much of a gene product is made, and a subset is associated with developmental conditions or disease.

Technical explanation

Copy number variants are unbalanced structural variants, conventionally from about one kilobase upwards, although current methods detect smaller events. Detection approaches include chromosomal microarray, which measures hybridisation intensity and, with SNP probes, allele ratios; read-depth analysis of sequencing data normalised for GC content and capture efficiency; and long-read or optical methods that resolve breakpoints directly. Each has blind spots: arrays cannot see balanced events and have limited resolution in repeat-rich regions, exome-based depth calling is noisy across capture boundaries, and depth methods struggle to distinguish three copies from four at higher copy number. Functionally, copy number variants act through gene dosage, disruption of a gene at a breakpoint, unmasking of a recessive allele on the remaining copy, or altered regulatory architecture. Recurrent events often arise by non-allelic homologous recombination between flanking segmental duplications, which is why particular genomic disorders recur at the same intervals. Interpretation weighs population frequency alongside gene content, and many such variants remain of uncertain significance.

Practical example

A cytogenetics laboratory running chromosomal microarray detects a deletion of a few hundred kilobases. The interval contains two genes with no established disease association, and the deletion appears in population copy number databases at low but non-trivial frequency. The laboratory classifies it as of uncertain significance and documents the evidence considered in reaching that classification.

Common misunderstanding

Copy number variation is often imagined as inherently pathological, with any deletion assumed to cause disease. Human genomes contain many copy number differences, and some genes vary widely in copy number across populations. Detecting such a variant is also not the same as sizing it accurately, since array and read-depth estimates of breakpoints are approximate.
