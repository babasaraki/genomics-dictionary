Polygenic risk score

PRS

Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A polygenic risk score, or PRS, adds up the small contributions of many genetic variants to give a single number summarising how a person's genome compares with others for a trait. It is a relative ranking within a reference group, not a diagnosis or a prediction of what will happen. For most common traits, non-genetic factors account for a large share of the variation between people.

Technical explanation

A polygenic score is typically a weighted sum of allele counts, with weights taken from GWAS summary statistics and adjusted for linkage disequilibrium by clumping and thresholding or by Bayesian shrinkage. Performance is reported as variance explained, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, or odds ratio per standard deviation, always relative to the distribution in a specified population. The raw score is dimensionless and interpretable only after standardisation against that reference. Portability is the central problem: scores derived in European-ancestry cohorts lose accuracy when applied to people of other ancestries, because tagging variants sit in different linkage disequilibrium with causal variants, allele frequencies differ, and effect estimates may not transfer. Deploying such scores without ancestry-appropriate derivation and validation risks widening rather than narrowing health inequalities. Further limitations include ascertainment and environmental confounding in the discovery cohort, poor calibration at the extremes of the distribution, and the fact that a score describes a population-level risk gradient rather than an individual trajectory.

Practical example

A biobank analysis reports that participants in the highest few per cent of a polygenic score distribution have appreciably greater relative risk of a common condition than those at the median. When the same score is applied, without re-derivation, to a cohort of different genetic ancestry, its ability to discriminate falls substantially, illustrating why ancestry-matched validation is required before any applied use.

Common misunderstanding

Polygenic scores are often read as personal verdicts, with a high score meaning disease is certain and a low score meaning safety. A score places someone on a distribution relative to a reference population. Most people in a high-scoring band never develop the condition, many who do have unremarkable scores, and the score identifies no individual causal variant.
