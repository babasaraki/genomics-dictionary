Mutation

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence compared with what was there before. Mutations arise from errors during DNA copying, from chemical damage, or from exposure to agents such as radiation. They may be inherited from a parent or acquired during a person's lifetime. Most have no noticeable effect, some are harmful, and a few are advantageous.

Technical explanation

Mutation denotes the process or event by which sequence changes, and historically the word was also used for the resulting sequence difference. Current clinical practice prefers variant for the observed difference, with pathogenicity stated separately, because mutation carries an unintended implication of harm and because the same change may be common and benign in some populations. Mutations are classified by mechanism and scale: substitutions, small insertions and deletions, structural rearrangements and copy-number changes. Germline mutations occur in the gametes or early embryo and can be transmitted; somatic mutations arise in body cells and contribute to cancer and to mosaicism without being passed to offspring. Mutation rates vary with sequence context, replication timing, parental age and exposure, and characteristic patterns of somatic change form mutational signatures that can indicate underlying processes such as ultraviolet damage or defective mismatch repair. A key limitation is that detecting a sequence change says nothing by itself about consequence, which requires separate evidence.

Practical example

A laboratory analysing a tumour sample alongside matched blood classifies which changes are present only in the tumour. Changes shared with blood are germline and are handled under consent provisions for inherited findings, while tumour-only changes are somatic, and conflating the two would misdirect both treatment discussion and family follow-up.

Common misunderstanding

Mutation is widely assumed to mean something harmful or abnormal. Most sequence changes are neutral, variation between individuals is ordinary, and harm depends on the specific change and context. The term also blurs an important distinction: a change in a tumour is not inherited, whereas a germline change may be, and the implications for relatives differ entirely.
