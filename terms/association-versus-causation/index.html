Association versus causation

Bioinformatics and statistics Human and population genomics Published

Definition

An association means two things vary together: a genetic variant is seen more often in people with a trait, or a gene's expression tracks a disease state. A causal relationship means that changing one would change the other. Association is observed; causation is inferred, and the inference requires evidence beyond the observation that the two travel together.

Technical explanation

Associations arise from several mechanisms besides causation. Confounding occurs when a third factor influences both, and in genomics population structure is the classic case: if allele frequencies and trait prevalence both differ between groups in a sample, spurious associations follow unless structure is modelled, which is why association studies adjust for principal components or use mixed models. Linkage disequilibrium means an associated variant is usually a marker for a correlated set of nearby variants rather than the functional one, so fine-mapping and functional work are needed to identify the actual effector. Selection and ascertainment in how participants were recruited can generate associations that exist only in the sampled group. Reverse causation is possible where the trait alters the measurement, a frequent concern for expression and methylation studies. Approaches that strengthen causal inference include replication in independent cohorts, Mendelian randomisation using germline variants fixed at conception, and experimental perturbation. None is sufficient alone, and each rests on assumptions that should be stated and tested.

Practical example

An association study links a variant to a dietary trait. Adjusting for genetic ancestry removes most of the signal, showing that the original result reflected population structure in the sample rather than a biological effect. The remaining association is small and is taken forward for replication in an independent cohort before any mechanistic claim is made.

Common misunderstanding

A statistically robust association is often reported as though the variant causes the trait. Association establishes that the two co-occur more than expected, which is compatible with confounding, with linkage to the real effector, with reverse causation, or with how participants were selected. The strength of a p-value says nothing about which of these applies.
