Codon

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A codon is a group of three consecutive bases in a messenger RNA that specifies one amino acid during protein synthesis, or signals that synthesis should stop. Reading a coding sequence three bases at a time gives the order of amino acids in the protein. There are sixty-four possible codons, and most amino acids are specified by more than one of them.

Technical explanation

Codons are read sequentially by the ribosome within a reading frame established at the initiation codon, usually AUG, which also specifies methionine. Sixty-one codons specify amino acids and three act as termination signals. The code is degenerate: synonymous codons encode the same amino acid, commonly differing at the third position, which is why many substitutions at that position do not change the protein sequence. Synonymous changes are nonetheless not always neutral, since codon usage influences translation speed, messenger RNA stability and splicing regulatory elements, and a synonymous variant can disrupt a splice site. Variant nomenclature distinguishes changes described at the complementary DNA level from their predicted protein consequence, and classification frameworks treat predicted protein effect as one line of evidence among several rather than as proof of pathogenicity. Codon usage also differs between organisms, which matters when expressing a human gene in bacteria or designing synthetic constructs. A practical limitation is that predicting consequence from codon change alone ignores context such as transcript choice and tissue-specific splicing.

Practical example

A bioinformatician annotating a variant sees a synonymous change at the third position of a codon and would normally deprioritise it. Because the position lies at the edge of an exon, the analyst checks splice predictions, which suggest the change weakens the donor site, and the variant is kept for further assessment rather than filtered out.

Common misunderstanding

It is widely assumed that synonymous codon changes are harmless and that a change of amino acid is harmful. Neither holds reliably: some synonymous changes disrupt splicing or translation, while many amino acid substitutions are tolerated. Predicted protein consequence is evidence to be weighed, not a classification in itself.
