Genomic data protection

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Genomic data protection is the set of legal and practical measures that govern how genetic data about people are stored, used and shared. Because a genome is permanent, identifiable and informative about relatives, most data protection laws treat it as a special category requiring stronger safeguards than ordinary personal information.

Technical explanation

Genomic data have features that complicate conventional data protection: they are unchangeable, so a breach cannot be remedied by issuing new credentials; they identify individuals even without names; they carry information about biological relatives who never consented; and their interpretation changes as knowledge grows, so data collected today may reveal more in future. Legal frameworks commonly classify genetic data as sensitive or special category, requiring an explicit lawful basis, risk assessment, security measures and limits on international transfer. Separate instruments restrict use in insurance and employment. Practical controls include access committees, trusted research environments, encryption at rest and in transit, key separation for pseudonymised identifiers, audit logging, retention schedules and incident response plans. National requirements differ substantially, and some countries restrict export of human genetic data entirely, which shapes how multi-country studies are designed. Compliance is not only technical: lawful processing depends on consent terms, ethics approval and transfer agreements being consistent with one another, and inconsistencies between them are a common source of avoidable breaches when data move between institutions.

Practical example

A multi-country study designs its architecture so that individual-level genotype data remain on servers in each country. Analyses are run locally according to a shared protocol and only summary statistics are combined centrally. This satisfies national restrictions on transferring human genetic data while still allowing the pooled analysis the study was designed to perform.

Common misunderstanding

A common assumption is that encryption and password protection are sufficient. Security controls address unauthorised access but not lawful use; processing still needs a valid basis, consistent consent terms and approved transfers. It is also wrong to think protection ends with the individual, since genomic data reveal information about relatives who were never asked.
