DPYD

Pharmacogenomics Published

Definition

DPYD encodes the enzyme that breaks down fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy drugs such as fluorouracil and capecitabine. People who carry variants reducing this enzyme's activity clear the drug more slowly and face a much greater risk of severe toxicity. Testing for a small set of these variants before treatment is recommended in several health systems.

Technical explanation

Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase catalyses the first and rate-limiting step of fluoropyrimidine catabolism, so reduced activity prolongs exposure to the active drug and increases the risk of severe mucositis, diarrhoea, myelosuppression and neurotoxicity. Clinical testing conventionally covers a small number of well-characterised variants with established effects, including a splice-disrupting variant, and each is assigned an activity value that is summed into a gene activity score used to guide dosing guidance. The approach has clear limits. The tested variants explain only part of deficiency, so a negative result reduces but does not eliminate risk, and the common variants were characterised largely in European populations, meaning that deficiency alleles more frequent elsewhere, including in African populations, are under-represented in standard panels and in the supporting evidence. Phenotypic approaches such as measuring uracil concentration in plasma offer a complementary route that does not depend on variant catalogues. Deficiency is a continuum rather than a binary state, and partial activity is more common than complete absence.

Practical example

An oncology pharmacy introduces genotyping of a defined set of variants in this gene before fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy. The laboratory reports a gene activity score alongside the alleles tested. The service documents that a result showing none of the tested variants does not exclude reduced enzyme activity, and clinical monitoring for early toxicity continues for every patient.

Common misunderstanding

A negative test is often taken as a guarantee that chemotherapy will be tolerated. Standard panels cover a few well-studied variants and miss others, particularly alleles under-represented in the populations from which the evidence was generated. Reduced activity varies continuously, so partial deficiency can occur without any of the tested variants being present.
