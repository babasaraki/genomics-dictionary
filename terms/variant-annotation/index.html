Variant annotation

Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Variant annotation adds meaning to a list of genomic positions. For each variant it records which genes and transcripts it falls in, what effect it is predicted to have on the protein, how often the allele is seen in population databases, and what clinical databases already say about it. Annotation organises the evidence; it does not by itself decide significance.

Technical explanation

An annotation tool maps each variant onto a chosen gene model and reports the consequence for every affected transcript, using standard sequence ontology terms such as missense, frameshift, splice acceptor or intron variant, together with nomenclature describing the change at the coding and protein level. It then joins external evidence: allele frequencies from population reference datasets, constraint metrics, conservation scores, in-silico pathogenicity predictors and assertions deposited in variant archives. The output is the raw material for filtering a genome down to a handful of candidates. Several limitations are important. Consequences are transcript-dependent, so the same variant may be missense in one isoform and non-coding in another, and the choice of canonical transcript affects what is reported. Population frequency databases under-represent many populations, so a variant that is common in an under-sampled group can appear spuriously rare and therefore spuriously interesting. In-silico predictors are correlated with one another and are weak evidence on their own. Finally, database assertions vary in quality and are revised, so annotation must be dated and repeatable.

Practical example

A research team filtering an exome for a rare recessive condition finds an apparently novel variant absent from the frequency database in use. Checking a dataset with broader population coverage shows the allele is present at appreciable frequency in individuals of similar genetic ancestry, which makes a disease-causing role far less likely and redirects the search.

Common misunderstanding

Annotation is often read as a verdict, so that a variant labelled deleterious by a predictor is taken to cause disease. Predictors estimate whether a change disrupts a protein, not whether it causes a condition in a person. Classification requires structured assessment of several independent evidence types, of which prediction is one weak strand.
