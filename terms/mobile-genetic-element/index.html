Mobile genetic element

MGE

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

A mobile genetic element, abbreviated MGE, is a piece of DNA that can move, either within a genome or between organisms. Plasmids, transposons, insertion sequences, integrons and bacteriophages are all examples. Mobile elements matter in pathogen genomics because they carry resistance and virulence genes from one microbe to another, often across species.

Technical explanation

Mobile genetic elements comprise diverse entities united by the capacity for relocation. Insertion sequences carry only the transposase needed to move themselves and can disrupt or activate neighbouring genes; composite and unit transposons additionally carry cargo genes; integrons capture and express gene cassettes through a site-specific recombinase; integrative and conjugative elements combine chromosomal integration with transfer machinery; plasmids replicate autonomously; and bacteriophages package and deliver DNA between cells. Together they create a mobilome whose content varies widely even between closely related isolates, and which drives the rapid dissemination of antimicrobial resistance and virulence determinants. Bacterial defence systems, including restriction-modification and CRISPR-Cas adaptive immunity, limit uptake and shape which elements establish in a lineage. Analytically, mobile elements are the main reason short-read assemblies fragment, because repeated copies of the same insertion sequence cannot be resolved, and they complicate phylogenetics, since regions acquired horizontally violate the assumption of vertical inheritance and should normally be removed before tree building. Tracking elements rather than only host lineages is often necessary to explain resistance spread.

Practical example

Analysts comparing two hospital isolates find that one carries an extra resistance gene flanked on both sides by identical insertion sequences, in a configuration typical of a composite transposon. The surrounding sequence is otherwise shared. This suggests a recent acquisition into an existing lineage rather than the arrival of a different strain, so the control response focuses on the setting where the acquisition probably occurred.

Common misunderstanding

Phylogenies of bacteria are often built from whole alignments as though all sequence is inherited vertically. Regions acquired horizontally break this assumption and can make unrelated isolates appear closely related. Detecting and masking mobile and recombinant regions before phylogenetic inference is standard practice, and omitting it is a common source of misleading outbreak conclusions.
