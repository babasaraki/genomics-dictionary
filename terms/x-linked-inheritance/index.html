X-linked inheritance

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

X-linked inheritance describes patterns produced by genes on the X chromosome. Because people with one X chromosome have only a single copy of those genes, an altered copy is expressed directly, whereas people with two X chromosomes have a second copy that may compensate. This produces family patterns that differ from those of genes on the other chromosomes.

Technical explanation

Genes on the X chromosome are present in one copy in individuals with an XY karyotype, a hemizygous state in which a single altered copy is fully expressed. In individuals with two X chromosomes, random X inactivation silences one X in each cell, so the proportion of cells expressing the altered copy varies, which explains why carriers of X-linked recessive conditions may show mild or patchy features and why skewed inactivation can produce more substantial involvement. X-linked dominant conditions affect both sexes, sometimes with male lethality, and affected fathers transmit the variant to all daughters and no sons. Pseudoautosomal regions at the ends of the X and Y chromosomes recombine and behave autosomally. Analytically, X-chromosome data require ploidy-aware variant calling and sex-aware association analysis, and inferred chromosomal sex from sequencing data may differ from recorded sex, which must be handled with care and without assumptions about gender identity.

Practical example

An analyst notices that a sample produces heterozygous calls across the X chromosome although the record lists the participant as male. Rather than assuming an error in the record, the team treats the finding as a quality control flag, checks for sample mix-up and for sex-chromosome variation, and follows the study's agreed procedure.

Common misunderstanding

X-linked recessive conditions are often described as affecting only males and never carriers. Carriers can show features because X inactivation is random and sometimes skewed. A second error is to equate chromosomal sex inferred from sequencing with a person's gender; they are different concepts and should never be conflated in reporting.
