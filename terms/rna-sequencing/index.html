RNA sequencing

RNA-Seq

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

RNA sequencing, abbreviated RNA-Seq, reads the RNA molecules present in a sample to show which genes are being expressed and at what relative level. The RNA is converted to DNA, prepared as a sequencing library and read, and the resulting reads are counted against genes or transcripts. It captures a snapshot of activity at the moment of collection.

Technical explanation

RNA sequencing quantifies the transcriptome by converting RNA to complementary DNA, preparing a library and counting reads assigned to genes or transcripts. Library choice determines what is observed: selection of polyadenylated transcripts captures most messenger RNA but excludes many non-polyadenylated species, while ribosomal depletion retains a broader population, including unprocessed and non-coding transcripts, at the cost of more complex data. Reads are either aligned to a genome with a splice-aware aligner or assigned to transcripts by selective alignment, and counts are normalised for library size and composition before comparison. Differential expression is assessed with count-based models that account for the mean-variance relationship of such data. Beyond expression, the data support detection of splicing changes, fusion transcripts and expressed variants. Limitations are substantial and routinely understated. Counts are relative within a library, so a global shift in transcription is invisible without external spike-in controls; reverse transcription, amplification and fragment-length effects introduce bias; the reference annotation constrains what can be quantified; and expression differences describe association with condition, not a causal mechanism.

Practical example

A research group compares bladder tumour tissue with adjacent non-tumour tissue from the same resections, sequencing RNA from both. Genes showing consistent differences between the two tissue types across several pairs become candidates for follow-up, and the group confirms a subset by an independent assay before drawing conclusions about the pathway involved.

Common misunderstanding

Differences in RNA levels are often reported as if they explained a phenotype. An expression difference is an association measured at one moment in a particular tissue. It may be a cause, a consequence, or a correlate of something else entirely, and transcript level does not reliably predict protein level, so further evidence is required before any mechanistic claim.
