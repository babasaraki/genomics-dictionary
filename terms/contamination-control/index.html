Contamination control

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Contamination control covers the laboratory practices that stop unwanted DNA or RNA entering a reaction or moving between samples. Because amplification and sequencing can detect tiny amounts of material, even invisible traces matter. Measures include separating work areas, using negative controls, handling reagents carefully and watching for suspicious results in the data.

Technical explanation

Contamination in molecular workflows arises from several distinct sources, and the controls differ accordingly. Carry-over of amplified product from previous reactions is the most serious in amplification-based diagnostics and is addressed by unidirectional workflow through physically separate pre-amplification and post-amplification areas, dedicated equipment and, in some assays, enzymatic degradation of previous products. Sample-to-sample cross-contamination during extraction and plate handling is managed by layout design, filtered tips, automation and the deliberate placement of blank wells. Reagent-borne nucleic acid, sometimes called the kit microbiome, is a dominant problem in metagenomics and low-biomass work, where it can generate plausible but spurious taxa. Index misassignment during sequencing creates an analogous effect in the data rather than at the bench. Detection relies on negative and blank controls carried through the whole process, on monitoring the species or variants seen in controls, and on checks of sample identity using independent genotypes. Suspicion should rise when a result appears at very low abundance, when it matches a high-abundance neighbouring sample, or when it appears in controls.

Practical example

A metagenomics laboratory processing low-biomass respiratory specimens includes extraction blanks in every batch. Several bacterial genera appear in both the blanks and the specimens at similar read counts. Treating these as reagent-derived rather than clinical findings, the team removes them from the analysis and reports the limitation alongside the results.

Common misunderstanding

Contamination is often imagined as a visible failure that only careless laboratories experience. Most contamination is invisible and systematic: trace nucleic acid in reagents, aerosols from previous reactions, and index hopping between pooled libraries. Without controls carried through the full process, contamination is indistinguishable from a genuine low-level finding in the data.
