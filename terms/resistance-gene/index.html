Antimicrobial resistance gene

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

An antimicrobial resistance gene is a stretch of DNA whose product helps a microbe survive a drug, for example by destroying the drug, pumping it out or changing its target. Such genes may sit on the chromosome or on mobile pieces of DNA that move between microbes. Sequencing can detect known resistance genes directly from a genome.

Technical explanation

Acquired resistance genes are identified in genome data by comparing assembled contigs or reads against curated databases of reference alleles, with thresholds for identity and coverage. Curated resources catalogue determinants together with the drug classes and mechanisms they affect, and some also record resistance-conferring point mutations in chromosomal genes, which are missed by gene-presence searches alone. Reporting conventions matter: a hit should state the gene family, the allele, the percentage identity and whether the match is complete, because truncated or partial matches frequently encode no functional product. Context is as important as presence. A determinant on a plasmid alongside other resistance genes behaves differently from the same gene on the chromosome, as co-location allows co-selection by unrelated drugs. Expression is also variable; an intact gene may be silent, while a promoter change can raise expression of a gene already present. Databases lag behind biology, so novel determinants are invisible until characterised, and homologues in environmental organisms may be reported as resistance genes without evidence that they confer clinical resistance.

Practical example

Sequencing a Salmonella isolate from a food investigation returns several resistance gene hits. One is a complete gene for an aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme at full length and identity; another is a fragment covering only part of a beta-lactamase. The report lists the complete gene and flags the fragment as partial, since a truncated sequence cannot be assumed to confer a resistant phenotype.

Common misunderstanding

Finding a resistance gene is often taken as proof that the organism is resistant to the corresponding drug. Gene detection predicts a phenotype; it does not measure one. Partial matches, silent genes, regulation and unknown mechanisms all break the link in both directions, which is why genotypic results are reported alongside, and not instead of, susceptibility testing.
