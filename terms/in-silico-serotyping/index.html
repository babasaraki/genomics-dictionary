In silico serotyping

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

In silico serotyping means working out which serotype an organism belongs to by examining its genome sequence on a computer, instead of testing it with antibodies in the laboratory. The method looks for the genes that produce the surface structures the antibodies would have recognised. It gives results that can be compared with decades of older laboratory typing data.

Technical explanation

Classical serotyping classifies isolates by antigenic reactions of surface structures such as lipopolysaccharide O antigens, flagellar H antigens or pneumococcal capsular polysaccharides, using panels of antisera. In silico methods replace this with sequence analysis, detecting the biosynthetic gene clusters or marker alleles associated with each serotype and mapping them onto the established antigenic nomenclature. Advantages are substantial: antisera are expensive and perishable, some serotypes are difficult to resolve by agglutination, and a single genome supports serotype prediction alongside sequence typing and resistance gene detection from one laboratory process. Accuracy depends on the completeness of the underlying database and on assembly quality, since capsular and O-antigen clusters are repetitive and may be fragmented in short-read assemblies. Discordance arises where isolates carry a disrupted biosynthetic cluster and therefore do not express the predicted antigen, or where novel clusters fall outside the scheme and are reported as untypeable. Prediction should thus be reported as a genotypic inference, with phenotypic confirmation reserved for discrepant, novel or regulatory-critical results.

Practical example

A reference laboratory monitoring pneumococcal disease after vaccine introduction predicts capsular serotypes directly from genome data for all invasive isolates. Most are assigned confidently, but a handful of genomes carry capsule clusters that do not match the scheme. These isolates are referred for phenotypic work and for closer analysis of the capsule region rather than being reported as a known serotype.

Common misunderstanding

Predicted serotypes are sometimes treated as though an antibody test had been performed. A prediction states which genes are present, not which antigen is expressed. Insertions, deletions or regulatory changes can silence a cluster, so the organism may not react with the corresponding antiserum, and novel clusters may be absent from the database altogether.
