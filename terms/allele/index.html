Allele

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

An allele is one of the alternative versions of a sequence found at a particular place in the genome. Because people inherit one copy of each chromosome from each parent, an individual carries two alleles at most positions, which may be identical or different. Alleles can differ by a single letter or by a larger stretch of sequence.

Technical explanation

Formally an allele is a specific sequence state at a defined locus, whether that locus is a single base, a short tandem repeat, a structural segment or a whole haplotype block. Population genomics describes loci by allele frequency, and frequency is always relative to a sampled population rather than to humanity as a whole, so an allele that is common in one ancestry group may be rare in another. In diagnostic practice a laboratory reports the alleles observed and, where phasing is available, whether two variants lie on the same or opposite copies of a gene, a distinction that changes the interpretation of recessive conditions. Pharmacogenomics uses a star-allele system in which named haplotypes of genes such as CYP2D6 capture combinations of variants that together predict enzyme activity. Limitations arise from detection: short-read methods resolve some alleles poorly, especially in repetitive or paralogous regions, and an untyped allele is not the same as an absent one.

Practical example

A laboratory testing a family for a recessive condition finds two different variants in the same gene in the affected child. Only by testing the parents, or by using a method that reads both variants on one DNA molecule, can the team establish whether the variants sit on opposite chromosome copies, which is what the recessive interpretation requires.

Common misunderstanding

People often assume alleles come in a normal version and a mutant version. A reference sequence simply records what one set of donors carried, and the reference allele is not necessarily the more common or the healthier one. Allele frequencies also differ between populations for reasons of history and demography, which says nothing about the merit of any group.
