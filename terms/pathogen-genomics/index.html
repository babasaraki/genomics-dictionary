Pathogen genomics

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Pathogen genomics is the study of the complete genetic material of organisms that cause disease, including viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites. Reading a pathogen's genome shows which species or strain is present, how closely one sample resembles another, and which genetic changes it carries. Public health laboratories use these details to follow outbreaks, detect resistance to medicines and judge whether control measures are working.

Technical explanation

Pathogen genomics applies sequencing and comparative analysis to the genomes of infectious agents in order to answer epidemiological and clinical questions. A typical workflow extracts nucleic acid from a clinical, environmental or veterinary sample, prepares a library, sequences it on a short-read or nanopore platform, and then either assembles a genome de novo or maps reads to a reference to produce a consensus sequence. Downstream analyses include lineage assignment, phylogenetic reconstruction, detection of antimicrobial resistance determinants and typing schemes such as multilocus sequence typing. Because pathogen populations evolve on the timescale of an outbreak, genomic differences accumulate quickly enough to separate independent introductions from local spread. The main limitations are sampling and context. A phylogeny reflects only the isolates that were sequenced, so uneven surveillance can make one country or hospital appear to be a source merely because it sequenced more. Sequence data alone cannot establish who infected whom; they constrain plausible transmission routes and must be read alongside epidemiological information. Quality control over depth, contamination and amplicon dropout matters, because missing genome regions can mask or mimic informative variants.

Practical example

During a cholera outbreak, a national public health laboratory sequences Vibrio cholerae isolates from several districts. The genomes fall into one tight group plus two unrelated ones. The tight group, which also carries the same resistance genes, suggests a single spreading lineage, while the outliers point to separate sources. Field teams combine this with case interviews to focus water testing on the districts linked by the shared lineage.

Common misunderstanding

It is often assumed that sequencing a pathogen proves who infected whom. It does not. Genomes narrow the field of plausible transmission links and rule some out, but identical or nearly identical sequences can be shared by many people in a chain, or by cases that were never sampled. Genomic evidence supports epidemiological investigation; it does not replace it.
