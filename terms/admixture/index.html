Admixture

Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Admixture happens when people from populations that had been separated for a long time have children together, so their descendants carry chromosome segments of different ancestral origin. Over successive generations recombination cuts these segments into shorter pieces. Most human populations today show some degree of admixture, and it is a feature of a genome's history rather than a defect.

Technical explanation

In an admixed genome, ancestry varies along each chromosome, and local ancestry inference assigns individual segments to a probable source population using haplotype patterns from reference panels. Segment lengths carry timing information, because recombination breaks ancestry tracts down at a roughly predictable rate, so long tracts indicate recent admixture and short ones older events. Admixture complicates several routine analyses. Global ancestry proportions are too coarse for choosing an allele frequency reference when the locus of interest falls within a segment of different ancestry. Imputation accuracy depends on whether the panel contains haplotypes resembling both source populations. Polygenic scores derived in one population transfer poorly, because linkage disequilibrium and allele frequencies differ between the contributing ancestries. Admixture mapping exploits the structure directly by testing whether local ancestry at a locus associates with a trait, which can be powerful where trait prevalence differs between source populations. All such inferences remain panel-dependent, and the source populations used are modern proxies rather than the actual ancestral groups.

Practical example

A research team studying an admixed cohort finds that global ancestry proportions explain little variation in a trait, but one chromosomal region shows consistently elevated ancestry from a single source population among affected participants. The team treats the signal as a pointer to an interval worth fine-mapping rather than as a cause in itself, and follows up with dense genotyping of the region.

Common misunderstanding

Admixture proportions are sometimes treated as fixed labels applying uniformly across a genome, as though a person were an even blend at every position. Ancestry is segmental: one stretch of a chromosome may descend entirely from one source population while the next descends from another, which is exactly why local ancestry, not a global percentage, matters for interpretation.
