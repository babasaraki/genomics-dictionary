Structural variant

SV

Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A structural variant, or SV, is a difference in DNA involving a sizeable chunk of a chromosome rather than one or a few letters. Deletions, duplications, insertions, inversions and translocations all count. The usual working threshold is around fifty base pairs. Structural variants are far less numerous than single-letter differences, yet together they affect much more total DNA in any genome.

Technical explanation

Structural variants are described as balanced, such as inversions and reciprocal translocations that rearrange sequence without net gain or loss, or unbalanced, where copy number changes. Detection depends heavily on technology. Short-read calling infers structural variants indirectly from discordant read pairs, split reads, read depth and local assembly, and performs poorly in repetitive sequence, which is precisely where many such variants lie; false positive and false negative rates are high and callers disagree substantially. Long-read sequencing spans repeats and resolves breakpoints directly, raising sensitivity considerably, while optical mapping and karyotyping remain useful for very large events. Structural variants matter biologically because they can delete or duplicate entire genes, interrupt coding sequence at a breakpoint, fuse two genes, or reposition regulatory elements relative to their targets and so rewire expression. Germline events contribute to developmental disorders, and somatic events are a defining feature of many tumours. Reporting is hampered by inconsistent breakpoint representation across file formats and by population databases that remain far less complete than those for small variants.

Practical example

A diagnostic team with a negative exome result for a suspected developmental disorder revisits the case using long-read sequencing. The data reveal a balanced inversion whose breakpoint interrupts a gene of interest, an event invisible to exome capture and poorly resolved by short-read mapping across the repetitive sequence surrounding the breakpoint.

Common misunderstanding

Structural variants are often assumed to be rare and almost always harmful. Every human genome carries thousands of them, the great majority with no known consequence, including common polymorphic deletions and duplications. Conversely, a negative short-read analysis is weak evidence of absence, because detection remains unreliable in repetitive regions.
