Genetic drift

Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Genetic drift is the change in how common an allele is from one generation to the next, caused simply by chance in who reproduces and which copies are passed on. It occurs in every population but matters most in small ones, where frequencies can swing widely. Drift can make an allele common, or lose it altogether, without the allele doing anything useful or harmful.

Technical explanation

Drift is the stochastic component of allele frequency change, arising because each generation samples a finite number of gametes from the one before. Its strength scales inversely with effective population size: the variance in allele frequency per generation is approximately p times one minus p, divided by twice the effective size, so small populations drift quickly. Over time drift removes variation by driving alleles to loss or fixation, while mutation and migration replenish it. Drift and selection act simultaneously, and their relative weight depends on the product of effective population size and the selection coefficient. When that product is much smaller than one an allele behaves as effectively neutral regardless of its functional consequence, which is why mildly deleterious alleles can reach appreciable frequency in populations that have passed through bottlenecks. Drift is the null expectation against which claims of selection are tested. A practical limitation is that demographic history and selection leave overlapping signatures, so separating them requires explicit demographic modelling.

Practical example

A student compares allele frequencies between an island population and the mainland population it descends from, and finds several alleles far commoner on the island. Before invoking adaptation, the class works through the frequency spread that drift in a small founding group would generate over the same number of generations, and finds the observed differences sit comfortably within that expectation.

Common misunderstanding

Drift is frequently described as random mutation, which conflates two distinct processes. Mutation creates new alleles; drift changes the frequency of alleles that already exist. A related error is assuming that any allele reaching high frequency must be beneficial. In a small or recently bottlenecked population, chance alone is a sufficient explanation.
