Single-nucleotide polymorphism

SNP

Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP, is a position in the genome where people commonly differ by one DNA letter. One person may carry a C where another carries a T, and each version is called an allele. SNPs are the commonest form of human genetic variation. Most of them lie outside genes and have no known effect on health or appearance.

Technical explanation

A SNP is a single-base substitution that is present in a population at appreciable frequency, in contrast to a rare single-nucleotide variant seen in one family. The distinction is one of frequency rather than mechanism, and many laboratories now prefer the neutral word variant for anything observed in a sequenced sample, reserving pathogenic variant for the subset with evidence of clinical effect. SNPs carry stable identifiers in dbSNP and are the markers assayed by genotyping microarrays, which interrogate hundreds of thousands of chosen positions rather than reading every base. Because alleles at nearby positions are inherited together in blocks, a genotyped SNP often acts as a proxy for untyped variation around it, which is what makes array-based association studies and imputation workable. Two limitations matter. Array content has historically been selected from variation common in European-ancestry samples, so coverage of variation common in African, South Asian and admixed populations is often poorer. And a statistical association with a SNP identifies a correlated region, not a causal base or a gene.

Practical example

A surveillance laboratory genotypes a cohort on a commercial array and obtains calls at several hundred thousand SNPs. Before analysis the team checks call rates per sample and per marker, removes positions failing quality thresholds, and compares allele frequencies with a matched reference panel. A handful of markers with wildly discordant frequencies turn out to be strand-assignment errors introduced during file conversion rather than real biological differences.

Common misunderstanding

It is often assumed that a SNP is a mutation that causes disease. Most SNPs are simply common differences between people with no known effect on any trait. A minority alter protein sequence or gene regulation, and only a small subset of those has evidence of being pathogenic. Classifying a variant as pathogenic requires evidence, not merely the observation that it exists.
