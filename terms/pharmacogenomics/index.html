Pharmacogenomics

PGx

Pharmacogenomics Published

Definition

Pharmacogenomics studies how differences in a person's DNA affect the way their body handles medicines and how those medicines act. Inherited differences can change how quickly a drug is broken down, how strongly it works, or how likely a severe reaction is. The abbreviation PGx is used for both pharmacogenomics and the closely related term pharmacogenetics.

Technical explanation

Pharmacogenomics covers pharmacokinetic genes, which govern absorption, metabolism, transport and elimination, and pharmacodynamic genes, which govern the drug target and downstream response, as well as immunologically mediated reactions determined by HLA alleles. Historically, pharmacogenetics referred to single gene-drug pairs and pharmacogenomics to genome-wide approaches, but the terms are now used largely interchangeably and both are abbreviated PGx. Clinical testing is usually genotype-based, interrogating a defined set of variants and structural changes rather than sequencing the whole gene, which means the result depends on which alleles the assay was designed to detect. Allele frequencies differ substantially between genetic ancestry groups, so a panel designed around alleles common in one population can misclassify individuals from populations in which other alleles predominate; this is a limitation of panel design, not of the individuals tested. Evidence quality varies by gene-drug pair, and implementation also requires decision support, pharmacy workflow and clinician education, which is why laboratory capability alone does not deliver benefit.

Practical example

A hospital laboratory offers a pharmacogenomic panel covering several metabolism genes and an HLA allele. A prescriber reviewing results for a patient sees a predicted metaboliser phenotype for one gene and a negative HLA result for another, and uses published guidance to interpret both. The report states which alleles the panel detects and which it does not.

Common misunderstanding

Pharmacogenomics is often expected to say whether a drug will work for someone. It addresses a narrower question: how inherited variation shifts exposure or risk of specific reactions for particular gene-drug pairs. Adherence, interacting medicines, renal and hepatic function, age and diet also shape response, and most medicines have no established pharmacogenomic marker.
