Sanger sequencing

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Sanger sequencing is a laboratory method for reading the order of bases along one short stretch of DNA, typically a few hundred letters at a time. Copying of the stretch is deliberately stopped at random positions by special building blocks, and the resulting fragments are sorted by length so that the base at each position can be read off in turn.

Technical explanation

Sanger sequencing uses a DNA polymerase to copy a template from a defined primer in the presence of ordinary deoxynucleotides plus a small proportion of fluorescently labelled dideoxynucleotides, which lack the chemical group needed to extend the chain. Each incorporation of a dideoxynucleotide terminates that molecule, so the reaction generates a nested set of fragments differing by one base. Capillary electrophoresis separates the fragments by size and a detector records the colour of the terminating base, producing a chromatogram whose peaks are translated into a base sequence with an associated quality value. Usable read lengths of several hundred bases per reaction are routine. The method remains the reference standard for interrogating a single locus because its per-base accuracy is high and the trace can be inspected by eye. Its limitations are equally important: it reads one amplicon per reaction, so it scales poorly; it reports the mixture of molecules in the reaction, so a low-fraction somatic variant can be hidden in the background of the trace; and it cannot resolve which of two heterozygous positions lie on the same chromosome without further work.

Practical example

A diagnostic laboratory finds a candidate single-nucleotide variant in a gene panel run on a patient sample. Before the result is reported, staff design primers around the position, amplify that fragment from the stored DNA extract and read it by Sanger sequencing. Seeing the same base change in the chromatogram, from an independent amplification, confirms the finding is not an artefact of the panel chemistry.

Common misunderstanding

Many readers assume Sanger sequencing has been retired by newer platforms. It has not. It is still the practical choice for confirming a single position, checking a cloned insert or typing one short region, and many laboratories use it daily. What it cannot do is survey a whole genome affordably or reliably detect variants present in only a small fraction of the molecules sequenced.
