Nanopore sequencing

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Nanopore sequencing reads DNA or RNA by pulling a single strand through a tiny protein pore in a membrane. As each part of the strand passes through, it changes an electrical current in a characteristic way, and software converts that changing signal into a sequence of bases. The devices are small and produce data as the run proceeds.

Technical explanation

Nanopore sequencing measures the ionic current through a biological pore held in an electrically resistant membrane as a nucleic acid strand is ratcheted through by a motor protein. The current is modulated by the stretch of bases occupying the pore, and a neural network model converts the raw signal trace into base calls. Because the strand is read intact, read length is set by the input molecule rather than the chemistry, and reads of tens to hundreds of kilobases are achievable with careful extraction. Data stream in real time, which allows a run to be stopped once sufficient coverage is reached and supports adaptive sampling, where a target region is enriched by rejecting unwanted molecules at the pore. Native strands are read directly, so base modifications can be inferred from the signal. The instruments are portable and have been used for field pathogen surveillance. Limitations include higher raw per-read error than short-read platforms, systematic difficulty with homopolymer runs, dependence of accuracy on the basecalling model version, and sensitivity of yield to DNA purity and pore occupancy.

Practical example

During an outbreak response in a district laboratory with limited infrastructure, amplicons from patient samples are barcoded, loaded onto a portable nanopore device and sequenced on site. Consensus genomes are generated within the same working day and shared with the national reference laboratory, informing the investigation while transport of samples to a central facility would still have been in progress.

Common misunderstanding

Because nanopore devices are small and inexpensive, they are sometimes assumed to be lower-grade instruments. Portability is a design choice, not a measure of data quality. What genuinely differs is the error profile: errors are distributed differently from short-read platforms and depend on the basecalling model, so the analysis chosen must suit that profile rather than being transferred unchanged.
