P value

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as those collected, if the hypothesis of no effect were true and the statistical model were correct. A small p-value means the data would be surprising under that assumption. It does not give the probability that the hypothesis is true, nor does it measure how large or important an effect is.

Technical explanation

The p-value is computed from a test statistic under a specified null model, and its interpretation depends entirely on that model being appropriate and on the analysis having been specified in advance. Professional statistical bodies have set out the resulting cautions explicitly: a p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, a threshold such as 0.05 is a convention rather than a boundary between real and unreal effects, and conclusions should not rest on whether a value falls either side of it. Two further issues dominate genomic work. Sample size drives the p-value as much as effect size, so with very large cohorts negligible differences reach extreme significance while small studies miss substantial effects. And p-values are valid only for the tests that were planned; selecting variables, outcomes or subgroups after seeing the data, or running many tests and reporting the smallest value, invalidates the calculation. Good practice reports the effect estimate with its confidence interval alongside the p-value, and states how many tests were performed.

Practical example

A cohort analysis of a hundred thousand participants finds an association with a p-value below one in a million, but the effect corresponds to a fraction of a percentage point of difference in risk. The authors report the effect size and interval prominently, so readers can see that the association is precisely estimated and small, rather than large because it is highly significant.

Common misunderstanding

A p-value above 0.05 is routinely reported as showing there is no effect. It shows only that the data are compatible with no effect; a small or imprecise study is compatible with a wide range of effects, including important ones. Absence of significance is not evidence of absence, and the confidence interval usually makes this visible.
