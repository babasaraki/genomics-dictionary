Translation

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Translation is the process by which a cell builds a protein using the instructions carried in a messenger RNA. Ribosomes move along the messenger RNA reading its bases three at a time, and transfer RNA molecules deliver the matching amino acids. The amino acids are joined into a chain that folds into a working protein. Translation stops at a stop signal.

Technical explanation

Translation proceeds through initiation, elongation and termination. In eukaryotes the small ribosomal subunit is recruited to the capped five-prime end and scans to an initiation codon in a favourable sequence context, after which the large subunit joins and elongation begins. Aminoacyl transfer RNAs deliver amino acids matched to each codon, peptide bonds form in the ribosomal peptidyl transferase centre, and release factors act at stop codons. Rates of elongation vary with codon usage and transfer RNA availability, influencing co-translational folding. Quality control pathways, notably nonsense-mediated decay, degrade transcripts carrying premature termination codons, which is why a stop-gain variant often reduces protein quantity rather than producing a truncated product, though position within the transcript determines whether decay is triggered. Clinically this distinction matters, since loss-of-function interpretation depends on whether the mechanism of disease is haploinsufficiency. A limitation of transcript-level data is that it does not report translation, and ribosome profiling or proteomics is required to measure what is actually synthesised.

Practical example

A variant assessment team encounters a stop-gain variant in the final exon of a gene. Because premature termination there is unlikely to trigger nonsense-mediated decay, a truncated protein may be produced, so the team weighs loss-of-function evidence more cautiously than it would for an equivalent variant in an early exon.

Common misunderstanding

A common assumption is that any premature stop codon abolishes the protein. The cellular consequence depends on position and on whether the transcript is degraded, and some truncated proteins retain or gain activity. Assuming that abundant messenger RNA implies abundant protein is a related error, since translation efficiency and protein turnover vary considerably.
