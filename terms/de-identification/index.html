De-identification

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

De-identification is the removal or masking of details that directly identify a person, such as name, address, hospital number and date of birth, before data are used or shared. It reduces risk but does not make genomic data anonymous, because a genome sequence is itself a distinguishing characteristic that can sometimes be matched to a person.

Technical explanation

De-identification covers a range of techniques: removing direct identifiers, replacing them with pseudonyms held under separate key management, generalising dates and locations, and suppressing small cells in tabulated results. Pseudonymisation retains a link to identity through a key and is therefore treated as personal data under most data protection regimes, whereas genuine anonymisation requires that re-identification is no longer reasonably possible. Genomic data resist anonymisation because a modest number of independent variants is sufficient to distinguish an individual, relatives share large identifiable segments, and genealogical databases allow identification through distant relatives. Demonstrations have also shown that combining de-identified genomic data with public records can narrow identity. The practical consequence is that risk is managed by a combination of measures rather than by data transformation alone: controlled access, binding agreements, secure analysis environments, aggregation of outputs and limits on the granularity of accompanying metadata. Rare variants, small populations and detailed phenotype or location fields increase risk substantially, so de-identification should be assessed in context rather than treated as a fixed procedure.

Practical example

A surveillance team prepares pathogen sequences for public deposition. Patient identifiers are removed, sampling locations are reported at district rather than facility level, and collection dates are given by week where case numbers are low. The human reads that remain after host filtering are removed before upload, since they could otherwise carry identifiable human genetic information.

Common misunderstanding

It is widely assumed that stripping names makes genomic data anonymous. A sequence is an identifier in its own right, and relatives are implicated by the same data. Treating de-identified genomic data as outside data protection law is therefore usually incorrect, and claims of anonymity in consent materials should be avoided in favour of describing the safeguards actually in place.
