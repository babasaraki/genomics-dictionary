Somatic variant

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A somatic variant is a change in DNA that appears in a body cell during a person's lifetime rather than being inherited. It is found only in that cell and the cells descended from it, such as a tumour, and it cannot be passed to children. Somatic variants accumulate steadily with age and with exposure to agents that damage DNA.

Technical explanation

Somatic variants arise from replication errors, unrepaired DNA damage and the activity of mutagens, and they are confined to the clone of cells in which they occurred. In cancer genomics they are usually identified by sequencing tumour tissue alongside a matched blood or saliva sample, so that variants shared by both can be set aside as germline and the remainder reported as tumour-specific. Variant allele fraction is informative rather than incidental: it reflects tumour purity, copy number at the locus and the proportion of tumour cells carrying the variant, so subclonal changes appear at lower fractions than clonal ones. Detection is harder than for germline variants because the signal may be diluted, which is why somatic assays run at much greater depth and apply error-suppression methods such as unique molecular identifiers. Without a matched sample, rare germline variants absent from population databases can be misreported as somatic, and clonal haematopoiesis in blood can introduce variants that belong to neither the tumour nor the inherited genome.

Practical example

A pathology laboratory sequences a resected bladder tumour and a matched blood sample. A TP53 missense variant appears in about forty per cent of tumour reads but in none of the blood reads, so it is reported as somatic. A second variant is present in both samples at half the reads and is handled through the germline reporting pathway instead, because it may carry implications for relatives.

Common misunderstanding

A common error is to read any variant listed on a tumour report as heritable, and to worry that children are at risk. Somatic variants are acquired in body cells and are not transmitted through eggs or sperm. Only variants confirmed in a germline sample carry inheritance implications, which is why matched testing matters.
