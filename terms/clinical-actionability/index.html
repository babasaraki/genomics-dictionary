Clinical actionability

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Clinical actionability describes whether knowing a genomic result could change what is done for a person's care. A finding is actionable when an established intervention exists, such as earlier surveillance, a preventive measure, a change of medicine or a different treatment. Many genomic findings are informative and interesting without being actionable in this specific sense.

Technical explanation

Actionability is assessed for a gene-condition pair rather than for a gene alone, and structured frameworks score it on the severity of the outcome, the likelihood that it occurs, the effectiveness and the burden of the available intervention, and the strength of the evidence supporting each. Scores are used to decide what belongs on secondary findings lists, on population screening panels and in guideline recommendations. Actionability is not fixed: it rises when an effective therapy is licensed and falls when an intervention is shown not to work, and it is health-system dependent, because an intervention that is routine in one setting may be unavailable in another. In oncology, actionability more often refers to whether a somatic alteration predicts response to a specific therapy, which is a narrower and more rapidly changing sense of the word; entries and guidelines should make clear which sense is meant. Actionability should not be confused with clinical utility measured in outcomes, which requires evidence that acting on the result actually improves them.

Practical example

A tumour sequencing panel identifies an alteration that matches a licensed targeted therapy, and the molecular tumour board records the finding as actionable. A second alteration in the same report has biological interest but no linked intervention; it is recorded for research purposes rather than for management. The board also notes whether the matched therapy is actually accessible within its health service.

Common misunderstanding

Actionable is often read as meaning important or serious. A severe but untreatable condition may score low on actionability, while a modest risk with a simple preventive measure may score high. The term measures whether care can change, not how grave the finding is, and it depends on what the local health system can deliver.
