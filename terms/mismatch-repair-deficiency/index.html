Mismatch repair deficiency

dMMR

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Mismatch repair is the system that proofreads newly copied DNA and corrects mispaired bases and small slips. Mismatch repair deficiency, abbreviated dMMR, means that this system is not working in a tumour. Errors then accumulate, especially in repetitive stretches, giving the tumour an unusually large number of mutations compared with repair-proficient tumours.

Technical explanation

The core mismatch repair proteins function as heterodimers, and loss of one partner destabilises the other, which is why immunohistochemistry for the four main proteins is interpreted as paired patterns rather than as four independent results. Deficiency arises from biallelic somatic inactivation, from epigenetic silencing by promoter hypermethylation, or from a germline pathogenic variant in a repair gene combined with a somatic second hit, the last corresponding to an inherited predisposition syndrome. Because the paired loss pattern points towards particular genes, reflex algorithms combine immunohistochemistry, methylation testing and, where relevant, germline analysis. Deficient tumours show microsatellite instability and a characteristic mutational signature, and the resulting neoantigen load underlies their responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibition across several tumour types. Limitations matter in practice: immunohistochemistry can be difficult to interpret with weak or heterogeneous staining, prior treatment can alter expression, and a proportion of deficient tumours are missed by any single assay, so orthogonal testing is usual in diagnostic laboratories.

Practical example

A pathology laboratory stains a tumour for the four mismatch repair proteins and finds that one heterodimer partner pair is absent while the other is retained. Methylation testing of the relevant promoter is then performed to distinguish a sporadic epigenetic cause from a possible inherited one, and the outcome determines whether the team discusses germline testing with the patient.

Common misunderstanding

Deficiency is often assumed to be inherited whenever it is found. Most deficient tumours result from somatic or epigenetic events confined to the tumour. It is also wrong to assume that deficiency guarantees benefit from immunotherapy; it shifts the probability of response, and outcomes still vary between individuals and tumour types.
