Genome

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A genome is the complete set of genetic material in an organism or a cell. For a person this means all the DNA in the nucleus, inherited from both parents, plus the small separate genome inside mitochondria. The genome includes genes and the much larger amount of DNA between and around them that helps control how genes behave.

Technical explanation

The human nuclear genome comprises roughly three billion base pairs distributed across twenty-two autosome pairs and the sex chromosomes, together with a circular mitochondrial genome of about sixteen and a half kilobases. Sequencing a genome means determining that sequence from an individual sample; interpreting it means comparing the result against a reference assembly and a catalogue of known variation. Successive reference assemblies have improved completeness, and a telomere-to-telomere assembly resolved repetitive regions that short-read methods had left as gaps, while pangenome resources represent multiple haplotypes instead of a single linear coordinate system. This matters because a single linear reference built largely from a few donors represents global human variation unevenly, and variants common in under-represented populations are more likely to be missed or misclassified. Genome in common usage also denotes the organism-level sequence of a pathogen, where assemblies are small enough to generate routinely during outbreaks. A limitation of whole-genome data is coverage of difficult regions: segmental duplications, long repeats and high-GC sequence remain harder to call confidently, so a genome described as complete is complete only within the limits of the technology used.

Practical example

A national reference laboratory assembling bacterial genomes from an outbreak compares each isolate with the others rather than with a distant reference strain. Doing so lets them count the small number of differing sites that separate isolates and judge whether cases form a plausible transmission cluster, a question that genome-wide comparison can answer but single-gene typing cannot.

Common misunderstanding

It is often assumed that there is one human genome and that sequencing reveals everything about a person. Each individual carries millions of differences from any reference, and reference assemblies are coordinate conventions, not ideal sequences. Sequencing also does not read every base equally well, and the clinical meaning of most variation found remains unknown.
