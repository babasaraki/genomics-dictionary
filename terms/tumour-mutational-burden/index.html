Tumour mutational burden

TMB

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Tumour mutational burden, abbreviated TMB, counts how many somatic changes a tumour carries, usually expressed per million bases of DNA examined. Tumours with a high burden may display more abnormal proteins on their surface, which can make them more visible to the immune system. It is used as a marker alongside other tests, not as a diagnosis.

Technical explanation

TMB is calculated from somatic variants detected by tumour sequencing and reported as mutations per megabase of the territory assessed. Values are not directly comparable between laboratories, because they depend on the size and content of the panel, on whether a matched normal sample was used, on how germline variants and synonymous changes are handled, on variant allele fraction thresholds and on tumour purity. Panel-based estimates are noisier than exome-based ones, particularly at small panel sizes, and harmonisation efforts exist precisely because of this variability. Biologically, a high burden is often the consequence of an underlying process such as mismatch repair deficiency, polymerase proofreading defects or heavy mutagen exposure, and it is that process rather than the count itself that may drive immunogenicity. TMB has been used as a biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibition, but its predictive performance varies substantially between tumour types, and a single universal threshold is not supported across all settings. It should be interpreted together with microsatellite instability status and other contextual findings.

Practical example

A tumour panel report gives a burden above the laboratory's threshold, together with a mutational signature consistent with defective mismatch repair. The molecular tumour board considers the burden alongside microsatellite instability testing and the tumour type rather than in isolation, and notes that the panel used and the absence of a matched normal sample both affect how the number should be read.

Common misunderstanding

A single burden figure is often treated as comparable across reports and as a direct predictor of response to immunotherapy. Values depend heavily on the assay and the bioinformatic rules used, and predictive value differs by tumour type. A high burden raises the possibility of benefit; it does not establish it on its own.
