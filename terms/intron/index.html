Intron

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

An intron is a section of a gene that is copied into the initial RNA molecule but then cut out before the RNA is used. The remaining pieces, called exons, are joined together. Introns are common in the genes of plants, animals and fungi, and although they are removed, sequences within and around them help control how the RNA is assembled.

Technical explanation

Introns are removed from precursor messenger RNA by the spliceosome, which recognises the donor site at the intron's five-prime end, a branch point, a polypyrimidine tract and the acceptor site at the three-prime end. Canonical introns begin with GT and end with AG in genomic sequence, with a minority of non-canonical introns using different dinucleotides. Introns are typically far longer than the exons they separate and contain regulatory elements, small nucleolar and microRNA genes, and sequences that influence splice-site choice. Variants deep within introns can create new splice sites or activate cryptic exons, and such variants are invisible to exome sequencing, which is one explanation for negative exome results in cases with strong clinical suspicion. RNA sequencing of a relevant tissue can demonstrate aberrant splicing directly, though the causal tissue is often inaccessible, which limits the approach. Intron retention also occurs physiologically and is a recognised regulatory mechanism rather than always an error.

Practical example

A team re-examines an unsolved case after exome sequencing found nothing. Genome sequencing identifies a deep intronic change, and RNA from a skin biopsy shows inclusion of an extra exon in the transcript, giving functional evidence for an effect that sequencing of coding regions alone could not have revealed.

Common misunderstanding

Introns are often dismissed as junk that the cell discards. They contain regulatory sequence, host other genes and shape splicing outcomes, and intronic variants are a recognised cause of disease. Assuming an intronic variant is benign because it is outside the coding sequence is a frequent error in interpretation.
