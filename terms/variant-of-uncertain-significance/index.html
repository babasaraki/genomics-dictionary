Variant of uncertain significance

VUS

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A variant of uncertain significance, usually shortened to VUS, is a DNA change for which there is not yet enough evidence to say whether it affects health. It sits between benign and pathogenic on the classification scale. A VUS is an honest statement about incomplete knowledge rather than a weak positive result, and it may be reclassified later in either direction.

Technical explanation

Uncertain significance is the middle tier of clinical variant classification and is assigned when the available evidence neither supports nor refutes a disease-causing role, or when evidence points in conflicting directions. Missense changes are over-represented in this tier because predicting the functional consequence of a single amino acid substitution remains difficult. Uncertainty is not distributed evenly across populations: because reference datasets and published case series have historically drawn most heavily on people of European genetic ancestry, individuals whose ancestry is under-represented in those resources receive uncertain classifications more often, which is a limitation of the evidence base rather than a property of their genomes. Resolution usually comes from accumulating evidence rather than from re-testing the same sample: larger and more diverse population datasets, family segregation studies, RNA analysis and systematic functional assays. Laboratories do not act clinically on an uncertain classification, and a VUS should not drive management decisions. Reclassification is routine, so laboratories keep a record of the assessment date and the evidence considered.

Practical example

A laboratory reports a missense variant in a cancer-susceptibility gene as uncertain in a person with a suggestive family history. Two years later, a functional assay and additional family segregation data are published, and the laboratory reclassifies the variant as likely pathogenic. The change comes from new evidence about the variant itself, not from any change in the person or in the original sample.

Common misunderstanding

A VUS is often read as a mild or partial positive result. It is neither positive nor negative; it means the evidence is currently insufficient. Treating an uncertain variant as though it were pathogenic can lead to unnecessary intervention, while treating it as benign can give false reassurance. The correct response is to wait for evidence.
