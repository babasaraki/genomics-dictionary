Wastewater surveillance

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Wastewater surveillance means testing sewage for traces of pathogens shed by the people who use the connected toilets. Because samples pool whole communities, they can show that an infection is circulating without anyone needing to be tested individually. Laboratories measure how much pathogen material is present over time, and may sequence it to identify which variants are there.

Technical explanation

Wastewater-based epidemiology samples influent at treatment works or at points within a sewer network, concentrates nucleic acid, and quantifies targets by quantitative or digital PCR, often followed by amplicon or metagenomic sequencing. The approach is independent of care seeking and clinical testing capacity, so it can detect circulation before cases present, follow trends when testing policy changes, and monitor pathogens with substantial asymptomatic shedding, such as poliovirus and some enteric bacteria. Interpretation requires normalisation, because flow varies with rainfall, industrial discharge and population movement; faecal indicators or flow data are commonly used to adjust concentrations. Degradation during transit, inhibitory substances and differences in concentration method make absolute comparisons between laboratories unreliable, so trends within a site are more robust than levels between sites. Sequencing from wastewater yields mixtures rather than single genomes, requiring deconvolution methods that estimate lineage proportions with wide uncertainty. Catchments also vary in sanitation coverage, which limits representativeness where many households are not connected to sewers, and small catchments raise questions about the privacy of the groups being monitored.

Practical example

A city environmental health team samples several sewer catchments weekly for poliovirus. One catchment shows repeated detections while clinical surveillance reports no cases. The finding triggers a review of childhood immunisation coverage in the neighbourhoods served by that catchment and targeted vaccination activity, rather than any attempt to identify individual shedders.

Common misunderstanding

People often assume a wastewater signal can be converted directly into a number of infected people. The relationship is indirect: shedding varies between individuals and over the course of infection, recovery from the sample is variable, and dilution changes with weather. Wastewater data are best used for direction and timing of change, not as a case count.
