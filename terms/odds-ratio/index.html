Odds ratio

OR

Bioinformatics and statistics Human and population genomics Published

Definition

An odds ratio compares the odds of an outcome between two groups. In genomic association studies it usually compares the odds of a trait in people carrying one allele with the odds in those carrying another. A value of one means no difference, above one means higher odds in the first group, and below one means lower odds. Both directions matter.

Technical explanation

Odds are the probability of an event divided by the probability of its complement, and the odds ratio is the ratio of those odds between exposure groups. It is the natural effect measure from logistic regression, which is why it dominates case-control designs and genome-wide association analyses, where it is reported per copy of the effect allele together with a confidence interval. The odds ratio approximates the relative risk only when the outcome is uncommon; for common outcomes it exaggerates the relative risk, sometimes substantially, so the two should not be used interchangeably. Interpretation requires the baseline risk, because a large ratio applied to a very small absolute risk remains a small absolute change. Three further cautions apply in genomics. Effect estimates are specific to the populations studied and may not transfer, particularly where linkage disequilibrium patterns differ between groups with different genetic ancestry. Estimates from the discovery sample are inflated relative to those from independent replication. And an odds ratio quantifies association, not mechanism or causation.

Practical example

An association study reports an odds ratio of 1.15 per copy of a common allele. Explaining this to a seminar, the presenter converts it to absolute terms using the background frequency of the condition, showing that the difference in lifetime risk between carriers and non-carriers is small, even though the association is statistically robust across cohorts.

Common misunderstanding

An odds ratio is frequently read as the factor by which an individual's risk is multiplied. It describes odds in groups, not risks in people, and it overstates relative risk when the outcome is common. Without the baseline risk it cannot be translated into anything a person could use to understand their own situation.
