Messenger RNA

mRNA

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Messenger RNA, usually abbreviated to mRNA, is the type of RNA that carries the instructions from a gene to the machinery that makes proteins. It is produced by copying a gene, edited to remove intervening sections, and then read three bases at a time to assemble a protein. The amount of a given messenger RNA in a cell reflects how active that gene currently is.

Technical explanation

Mature eukaryotic messenger RNA carries a seven-methylguanosine cap, a spliced coding sequence flanked by untranslated regions, and a polyadenylated tail. These features support export from the nucleus, ribosome recruitment and transcript stability, and they are also what sequencing protocols exploit, since poly-A selection enriches messenger RNA while ribosomal depletion retains non-polyadenylated species and degraded material. Transcript abundance is quantified from RNA sequencing after normalisation for library size and composition, and differential expression testing uses count-based models that account for overdispersion. Interpretation requires care: abundance depends on both synthesis and decay, correlates only moderately with protein levels, and is sensitive to sample handling, since RNA degrades quickly without stabilisation. Synthetic messenger RNA is also used therapeutically, as in messenger RNA vaccines, where modified nucleosides and lipid formulation address instability and innate immune activation. Note that in other contexts mRNA is occasionally used loosely for any coding transcript, including unspliced precursors, so precision in writing is worthwhile.

Practical example

A core facility receives tissue samples collected in theatre with variable delay before freezing. Quality scores show partial degradation in the delayed samples, which biases coverage towards transcript ends, so the analysts include collection delay as a covariate and exclude the worst samples rather than comparing groups as though handling were uniform.

Common misunderstanding

Messenger RNA is often treated as a direct readout of protein level. Transcript and protein abundance correlate imperfectly because translation and degradation are regulated separately. A second misunderstanding is that messenger RNA vaccines alter a person's DNA; the molecule is transient, acts in the cytoplasm and has no mechanism for integrating into the genome.
