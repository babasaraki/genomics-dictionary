ACMG/AMP variant classification

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

ACMG/AMP variant classification is a shared framework that clinical laboratories use to decide how confident they are that a DNA variant causes disease. It sorts evidence into weighted criteria and combines them into five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign and benign. The aim is that different laboratories reading the same evidence reach the same conclusion.

Technical explanation

The framework, published jointly by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology, defines criteria covering population frequency, computational prediction, functional data, segregation, de novo status, allelic data and reputable sources, each carrying a strength such as supporting, moderate, strong or very strong. Combining rules then map the collected criteria onto one of five tiers. In practice the framework is adapted rather than applied literally: expert panels issue gene-specific specifications that set frequency thresholds, decide which functional assays count and constrain the use of computational evidence, because defaults calibrated on one gene mislead on another. A separate framework exists for somatic variants in tumours, which are tiered by clinical and therapeutic significance rather than by pathogenicity, and the two should not be conflated. Limitations are well recognised: evidence codes can be applied inconsistently, uncertain classifications dominate for missense variants, and under-representation of many populations in reference datasets reduces the power of frequency-based criteria for people of under-sampled genetic ancestries.

Practical example

Two laboratories independently assess the same splice-region variant. One counts a published minigene assay as strong functional evidence; the other treats it as supporting because the assay is not validated for that gene. They reach different tiers, submit both interpretations to a public variant archive, and the conflict is then resolved by an expert panel that issues a gene-specific rule for such assays.

Common misunderstanding

The five tiers are often read as a scale of severity, as though likely pathogenic meant a milder disease than pathogenic. They describe confidence in the evidence, not clinical severity or the chance that an individual becomes unwell. A likely pathogenic variant is one the laboratory is slightly less certain about, not one that causes a lesser condition.
