Genetic counselling

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Genetic counselling is a conversation between a trained professional and a person or family about how a genetic condition or test result might affect them. It covers what a result can and cannot say, what it may mean for relatives, and what choices exist. The counsellor supports the person in reaching their own decision rather than directing it.

Technical explanation

Genetic counselling combines risk assessment, information-giving and psychosocial support. A session typically begins with a three-generation pedigree and a review of medical records, from which inheritance pattern and recurrence risk are assessed, and proceeds to explaining the analytical and clinical limits of the available tests. Pre-test counselling addresses what each possible outcome would mean, including uncertain results and secondary findings, and establishes informed consent; post-test counselling places the result in the context of penetrance, expressivity and family implications, and discusses cascade testing for relatives. The non-directive stance is a professional norm developed in explicit reaction to the history of eugenics, and it is particularly important for reproductive and predictive testing. Practice varies with health system capacity: in many settings counselling is delivered by clinical geneticists, specialist nurses or trained clinicians rather than by dedicated counsellors, and task-sharing models are an active area of development in countries where the specialist workforce is small. Cultural context, language and confidentiality within extended families all shape how information is communicated.

Practical example

An adult whose parent has a known pathogenic variant in a cancer-susceptibility gene attends for predictive testing. The pre-test session covers what a positive or negative result would mean for surveillance, what it would not tell them about timing or severity, the implications for siblings and children, and the option not to be tested. The decision is left to them.

Common misunderstanding

Counselling is often expected to end with a recommendation about whether to test or how to use a result. The role is to clarify options and their consequences so that the person can decide in line with their own values. A counsellor who steered the choice would be departing from the professional standard the field has deliberately adopted.
