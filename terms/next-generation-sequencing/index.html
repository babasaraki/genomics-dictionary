Next-generation sequencing

NGS

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Next-generation sequencing, abbreviated NGS, is a family of methods that read millions of DNA fragments at the same time instead of one at a time. The sample is broken into pieces, prepared into a sequencing library and loaded onto an instrument that reads every fragment in parallel. Computers then assemble or map those reads to work out the original sequence.

Technical explanation

Next-generation sequencing covers the massively parallel platforms that displaced single-amplicon chemistry for most genome-scale work. The common pattern is the same across chemistries: nucleic acid is fragmented or amplified into defined pieces, adapters are ligated or added by tagmentation, the resulting library is immobilised or loaded, and many millions of templates are read simultaneously. Output is a set of reads with per-base quality scores, usually in FASTQ format, which are mapped to a reference or assembled de novo. The term is deliberately broad and now covers both short-read and long-read instruments, so stating the platform and chemistry is more informative than the label itself. The practical consequence of parallelism is that depth and breadth become design choices rather than fixed properties: the same instrument can read one genome deeply or hundreds of amplicons shallowly. Limitations follow from the workflow rather than the detector. Amplification introduces duplicate and composition biases, short fragments cannot span long repeats, index misassignment can move reads between multiplexed samples, and every step from extraction onwards contributes error that the quality score alone does not capture.

Practical example

A public health laboratory sequences several hundred bacterial isolates collected during a hospital outbreak investigation. Each isolate is extracted, prepared as a barcoded library, and the libraries are pooled onto a single run. One instrument run yields enough data for every isolate to be assembled and compared, which would have been impossible one fragment at a time.

Common misunderstanding

A frequent error is to treat next-generation sequencing as a single technology with one accuracy figure. It is a category covering several chemistries with different read lengths, error profiles and costs. Accuracy also depends on the library, the depth and the region being read, so a claim about sequencing performance means little until the platform, the workflow and the target are specified.
