Base pair

bp

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A base pair is two nucleotide bases on opposite strands of a double-stranded nucleic acid that are held together by hydrogen bonds. In DNA, adenine pairs with thymine and cytosine pairs with guanine. Because of this fixed pairing, each strand carries the information needed to rebuild the other. Base pairs are also the standard unit for measuring the length of a DNA sequence.

Technical explanation

Watson-Crick base pairing joins adenine to thymine through two hydrogen bonds and cytosine to guanine through three, which is why sequences rich in cytosine and guanine melt at higher temperatures and behave differently in amplification and capture. In RNA, adenine pairs with uracil, and non-canonical pairings such as guanine-uracil wobble allow the folded structures that give functional RNAs their shapes. The abbreviation bp denotes a single base pair, with kilobase, megabase and gigabase used for larger spans, and lengths of single-stranded molecules are quoted in bases rather than base pairs. Complementary pairing is the physical basis of hybridisation-based methods: probe design, primer specificity, target capture and melting-curve analysis all depend on predictable duplex stability. A limitation is that predicted and observed hybridisation behaviour can diverge, because secondary structure, mismatches near the three-prime end of a primer, salt concentration and repetitive sequence all alter performance, which is why assays are validated empirically rather than designed on sequence rules alone.

Practical example

A laboratory designing primers for an amplicon scheme finds that one amplicon consistently drops out in a subset of samples. Sequence inspection shows a variant under the primer binding site, which weakens pairing and reduces amplification, so the team redesigns the primer rather than reporting the resulting coverage gap as a deletion.

Common misunderstanding

Base pair and base are often used as if interchangeable. A read length of one hundred and fifty bases is not one hundred and fifty base pairs of information unless the molecule is double-stranded, and the distinction matters in reporting. A second error is assuming pairing is perfectly specific; mismatched duplexes form readily under permissive conditions, which is how off-target amplification arises.
