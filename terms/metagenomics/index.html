Metagenomics

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Metagenomics is the sequencing of all the genetic material in a sample, without first growing or targeting any single organism. From a swab, a stool sample or river water, it produces reads from bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites and the host together. Analysts then sort the reads by likely origin to see what is present.

Technical explanation

Metagenomic sequencing extracts total nucleic acid from a sample and sequences it untargeted, usually in shotgun fashion. Reads are classified taxonomically by comparison with reference databases, for example through fast exact-match classifiers that assign each read to the lowest common ancestor of its matches, or they are assembled into contigs and binned into metagenome-assembled genomes. The approach is hypothesis-free, which makes it valuable for unexplained illness, for detecting organisms that do not grow in culture, and for characterising resistance gene content of whole communities. Three limitations dominate practice. Host nucleic acid often makes up the great majority of reads in clinical specimens, so depth must be high or host depletion applied. Classification is bounded by the reference database, so poorly represented taxa are missed or misassigned, and a positive classification does not establish that the organism is alive or causing disease. Contamination from reagents and the laboratory environment produces reproducible false signals, making negative controls essential. Quantitative claims need careful normalisation because genome size and extraction efficiency vary.

Practical example

A patient has encephalitis of unknown cause and standard tests are negative. Metagenomic sequencing of cerebrospinal fluid returns a small number of reads from an unexpected virus. Before this is reported, the team checks the negative controls, confirms the reads span several genome regions, and seeks confirmation by a specific amplification assay, because low read counts alone are not conclusive.

Common misunderstanding

Metagenomics is often described as detecting everything in a sample. In practice it detects what is abundant enough to sequence, present in the reference database and distinguishable from contamination. Detecting a sequence also says nothing about viability or causation: commensal and environmental organisms are routinely found, so clinical interpretation needs the whole picture.
