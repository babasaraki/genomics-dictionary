Microsatellite instability

MSI

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Microsatellites are short stretches of DNA in which a very small motif repeats many times. These regions slip easily during copying, and a cell with working repair machinery corrects the errors. Microsatellite instability, abbreviated MSI, is the pattern of varying repeat lengths seen in a tumour whose repair system is no longer correcting those slips.

Technical explanation

MSI is the measurable consequence of mismatch repair deficiency and is assessed either by a polymerase chain reaction panel of mononucleotide and dinucleotide markers compared between tumour and matched normal tissue, or computationally from sequencing data by examining repeat length distributions across many loci. Tumours are classified as microsatellite instability-high, low or stable, and the high category is the clinically relevant one. MSI testing is often paired with immunohistochemistry for the mismatch repair proteins, since the two assays interrogate the same underlying defect from different directions and can disagree, for example when a missense variant leaves a non-functional protein detectable by antibody. Practical limitations include dependence on tumour cellularity, reduced sensitivity in some tumour types where the classical marker panels were not developed, and poorer performance of sequencing-based callers at low purity or shallow depth. MSI-high status in a tumour may reflect a somatic event or an underlying germline cause, and the distinction requires separate germline investigation rather than being inferred from the tumour result.

Practical example

A laboratory compares the lengths of a panel of repeat markers between a colorectal tumour and the patient's matched normal tissue. Several markers show additional, shorter alleles only in the tumour, and the case is classified as instability-high. The report recommends that the referring team consider whether germline assessment for an inherited mismatch repair condition is appropriate, as a separate question.

Common misunderstanding

MSI is often equated with an inherited condition, so a high-instability tumour is taken to mean the person has a hereditary cancer syndrome. Many instability-high tumours arise from somatic events, including epigenetic silencing of a repair gene. Establishing whether the cause is germline requires testing a normal sample, not reading it off the tumour.
