Research ethics approval

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Research ethics approval is formal permission from an independent committee to carry out a study involving people, their samples or their data. The committee examines the scientific justification, the risks and benefits, how participants will be recruited and consented, and how data will be protected. Work should not begin before approval is granted.

Technical explanation

Research ethics committees, also called institutional review boards, review protocols against recognised standards derived from the Declaration of Helsinki and national regulations. For genomic studies, reviewers pay particular attention to the consent model, the plan for data sharing and secondary use, arrangements for return of results, data protection and cross-border transfer, involvement of children or others who may be vulnerable, community engagement, and benefit sharing in multi-country work. Approval is conditional and continuing: substantial amendments require review, progress and final reports are expected, and serious events must be notified. Multi-country studies usually need approval in every country where participants are recruited, and local committees may impose requirements that differ from those of the sponsor's committee, which has to be planned for rather than discovered late. Committee capacity varies, and genomic protocols place specific demands on reviewers that not every committee is resourced to meet, so training and access to specialist advice are recognised needs. Approval establishes that a study was independently judged acceptable at a point in time; it does not transfer responsibility for conduct away from the investigators.

Practical example

A team planning to sequence stored clinical isolates checks whether the original consent covers the proposed work. Because the samples were collected for diagnosis rather than research, the protocol is submitted to the ethics committee with a request for a waiver of individual consent on defined grounds, together with a plan for de-identification and for reporting aggregate results only.

Common misunderstanding

Approval is sometimes regarded as a hurdle cleared once at the start. It is a continuing relationship: amendments, new analyses outside the approved scope and new data sharing arrangements all require review. Nor does approval substitute for consent, benefit-sharing terms or transfer agreements, which are separate requirements that must be consistent with one another.
