Diagnostic yield

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Diagnostic yield is the proportion of people tested in whom a test provides an explanation for their condition. If a laboratory sequences one hundred people with a suspected genetic disorder and reaches a confident answer in thirty, the yield is thirty per cent. Yield describes the performance of a test in a particular group of patients, not in everybody.

Technical explanation

Diagnostic yield is a service-level metric that depends as much on who is tested as on the assay. Cohorts selected by strict phenotypic criteria, by consanguinity or by early onset yield more than broadly referred cohorts, so figures from one centre transfer poorly to another. Yield also depends on the breadth of the assay, on whether parents are sequenced alongside the proband, on reanalysis policy and on the laboratory's threshold for reporting. Reanalysis of previously undiagnosed cases raises yield over time without any new sample being taken, because gene-disease associations accumulate and variant classifications are revised. Counting conventions vary: some centres count only confirmed pathogenic and likely pathogenic findings, others include strong candidate genes or partial explanations, and these choices can shift a reported figure substantially. Yield is not the same as clinical utility, since an explanation may end a diagnostic odyssey and inform family counselling without altering treatment. Under-representation of many populations in reference datasets lowers yield for under-served groups, which is an equity problem rather than a biological one.

Practical example

A paediatric genomics service reports that sequencing trios of a child and both parents gives a higher yield than sequencing the child alone, because de novo variants and compound heterozygous combinations are easier to recognise with parental data. Two years later the service reanalyses unsolved cases against updated gene-disease evidence and resolves a further group of families without collecting new samples.

Common misunderstanding

Quoted yield figures are often compared as if they measured which laboratory performs better. They mostly reflect differences in referral criteria, assay breadth and counting rules. A higher yield can simply mean a more tightly selected cohort, and a lower yield can mean a service that accepts broad referrals rather than one using an inferior test.
