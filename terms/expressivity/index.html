Expressivity

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Expressivity describes how differently a trait or condition can appear among people who carry the same genotype. Where penetrance asks whether a carrier is affected at all, expressivity asks how severely and in which tissues. Variable expressivity means that two relatives with an identical variant may have very different features, ages of onset or degrees of difficulty.

Technical explanation

Variable expressivity reflects the many influences that sit between a genotype and an observable phenotype. Modifier variants elsewhere in the genome, polygenic background, somatic mosaicism, stochastic developmental events, sex, age and environmental exposures all contribute, as does the mechanism of the variant itself: a residual-function allele often produces a milder presentation than a complete loss-of-function allele in the same gene. Allelic series within a gene can therefore span a wide clinical spectrum. Expressivity matters for both diagnosis and research design. In the clinic it explains why a relative identified through family testing may have features so mild that they were never recognised, and why a condition defined from severely affected index cases can look different once milder carriers are ascertained. In research it inflates phenotypic variance and reduces the power of genotype-phenotype correlation studies unless phenotyping is deep and standardised. A limitation is that expressivity is usually described qualitatively, because quantitative phenotype scales are available for only a minority of conditions.

Practical example

A clinical team examines several members of one family who share the same pathogenic variant. One has features severe enough to have prompted referral in childhood; another has only a subtle skin finding noted during examination; a third reports no symptoms but shows a change on imaging. The genotype is identical across all three, and the differences lie in expressivity.

Common misunderstanding

Identical variants are expected to produce identical disease, so a mild presentation in a relative is sometimes taken as evidence that the laboratory result was wrong. Variable expressivity is common and does not invalidate a classification. Severity is shaped by modifier genes, chance and environment, so a family member's mild features cannot be used to predict another's course.
