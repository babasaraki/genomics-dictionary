Transcription

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Transcription is the process by which a cell copies a stretch of DNA into RNA. An enzyme called RNA polymerase binds near the start of a gene, unwinds the two DNA strands and builds an RNA molecule that matches one of them. The resulting RNA may carry instructions for a protein or may itself do a job in the cell.

Technical explanation

Transcription begins when RNA polymerase and associated factors assemble at a promoter, aided in eukaryotes by distal enhancers brought into proximity by chromatin folding. The enzyme synthesises RNA in the five-prime to three-prime direction using the template strand, so the transcript matches the coding strand with uracil replacing thymine. Eukaryotic transcripts are processed co-transcriptionally: a cap is added at the five-prime end, introns are removed by the spliceosome and a polyadenylated tail is added after cleavage. Regulation operates at initiation, pausing and elongation, and is shaped by transcription factor availability, chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation at regulatory regions. Measuring transcription is not the same as measuring steady-state RNA: standard RNA sequencing quantifies abundance, which reflects both synthesis and degradation, so methods such as metabolic labelling are needed to separate the two. A further limitation is that short-read data infer transcript structure statistically, and isoform-level estimates are less certain than gene-level ones, particularly for genes with many overlapping isoforms.

Practical example

A research group comparing treated and untreated cells finds a gene with higher transcript abundance after treatment. Because abundance reflects both synthesis and decay, they cannot conclude that transcription increased, and they add an experiment that labels newly made RNA before making that claim in the manuscript.

Common misunderstanding

Transcription and translation are frequently swapped in casual use. Transcription makes RNA from DNA; translation makes protein from RNA. A further error is to assume a transcribed region must be functional, since pervasive low-level transcription occurs across much of the genome and detecting a transcript is not evidence that it does anything.
