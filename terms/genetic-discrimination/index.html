Genetic discrimination

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Genetic discrimination is unfair treatment of a person because of their genetic information or a presumed inherited risk, for example in employment, insurance or access to services. Fear of it is one of the main reasons people hesitate to take part in genomic research or to have a test, so protections matter for public trust as well as for fairness.

Technical explanation

Genetic discrimination involves decisions made on the basis of genotype, family history or test results rather than current health or demonstrated capacity. The information concerned is frequently probabilistic: most risk variants alter the chance of a condition rather than determining it, and penetrance varies with other genetic factors and environment, so decisions based on a result often rest on an overestimate of what it predicts. Legal protections differ widely by jurisdiction and are usually partial, covering some domains such as employment and health insurance while leaving others, for instance life or income protection insurance, to voluntary codes or to no restriction at all. Protections may also not extend to relatives, who share risk information without having been tested. Historical context explains the sensitivity of the subject, since eugenic programmes used claims about heredity to justify coercion. Related harms include stigmatisation of groups when risk alleles are described as belonging to a population, which conflates genetic ancestry with social or national identity and ignores the continuous distribution of variation within and between groups.

Practical example

A university runs a workshop for prospective research participants and is asked whether taking part could affect employment. The honest answer sets out what local law does and does not cover, what the study does with data, and that relatives are not tested but may share risk. The study records the questions raised and revises its information sheet accordingly.

Common misunderstanding

People often assume that a genetic test result predicts disease definitively, which is also the assumption underlying much discrimination. Most results shift probabilities. A second error is the belief that data protection law by itself prevents discrimination; it governs how data are handled, while discrimination is addressed by separate, uneven and often incomplete legislation.
