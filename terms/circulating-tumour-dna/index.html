Circulating tumour DNA

ctDNA

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Circulating tumour DNA, abbreviated ctDNA, is the fraction of DNA in a person's blood that came from tumour cells. Dying cells release short DNA fragments into the bloodstream, and in someone with cancer a portion of those fragments carries the tumour's changes. Testing blood in this way is often called a liquid biopsy.

Technical explanation

ctDNA is a subset of total cell-free DNA, and the tumour-derived fraction is frequently very small, so assays must distinguish genuine variants from sequencing and library preparation errors. Error suppression using unique molecular identifiers, deep targeted sequencing, digital polymerase chain reaction for known variants and tumour-informed panels designed from a patient's own tumour profile are the usual strategies. Applications include genotyping when tissue is insufficient or inaccessible, monitoring response and resistance during treatment, and detecting residual disease after curative-intent therapy. The fragmentation pattern and methylation state of cell-free DNA carry additional signal about tissue of origin. Interpretation has clear limits: the shed fraction varies with tumour type, burden and site, so a negative result does not exclude disease, and central nervous system tumours shed poorly into blood. Clonal haematopoiesis in blood cells generates variants that are not tumour-derived and is a well-recognised source of false attribution unless a matched blood cell sample is analysed alongside.

Practical example

A patient's tumour cannot be rebiopsied safely at progression, so the team sequences cell-free DNA from a blood sample. A resistance variant in the drug target is detected at a low allele fraction, consistent with an emerging subclone. The report records the limit of detection of the assay and notes that a negative result would not have excluded resistance.

Common misunderstanding

Liquid biopsy is often understood as a straightforward replacement for tissue sampling and even as a general cancer test. Sensitivity depends on how much DNA a tumour sheds, which varies widely, so absence of signal is not absence of disease. Variants from clonal haematopoiesis can also be mistaken for tumour-derived ones.
