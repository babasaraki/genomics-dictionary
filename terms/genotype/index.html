Genotype

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A genotype is the particular combination of alleles an individual carries at one or more places in the genome. It can describe a single position, such as which two letters a person has at one site, or a set of positions across many genes. Genotype is determined by inheritance and, unlike many observable traits, does not usually change during life.

Technical explanation

Genotyping measures sequence state at defined positions, whereas sequencing reads a region without prior commitment to which positions will be informative. Array-based genotyping interrogates a fixed, pre-selected set of markers cheaply and reproducibly, and imputation against a reference panel can infer additional untyped sites, though accuracy falls for rare variants and for populations poorly represented in the panel. Genotype is reported per locus as homozygous for one allele, heterozygous, or hemizygous where only one copy exists, as on the X chromosome in males. In diploid analysis, genotype calls carry quality metrics such as read depth and genotype quality, and low-coverage positions produce no-calls rather than reference calls, a distinction that must be preserved in downstream analysis. Somatic genotypes in tumours differ from the germline genotype of the same person, so a tumour-only test cannot by itself distinguish inherited from acquired variants. Genotype also underdetermines phenotype: penetrance, modifier loci, epigenetic state and environment all intervene.

Practical example

A research team genotyping participants on an array obtains a set of marker calls and imputes additional sites against a reference panel. For participants of African ancestry the panel contains fewer matching haplotypes, so imputation confidence is lower at many positions, and the analysts filter on imputation quality before testing for association rather than treating all sites as equally reliable.

Common misunderstanding

A common error is to treat a missing genotype call as evidence that a person carries the reference allele. No-calls mean the assay produced no reliable result, which is different from a negative finding. A related error is reading a genotype as a prediction of a trait; most genotypes shift probabilities rather than determining outcomes.
