CPIC guideline

Pharmacogenomics Published

Definition

A CPIC guideline is a published document from the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium that explains what to do with a pharmacogenomic test result once it exists. It does not advise whether to order a test; it translates a genotype into a predicted phenotype and sets out prescribing implications, with the strength of evidence stated for each recommendation.

Technical explanation

CPIC guidelines are peer-reviewed, gene-drug or gene-drug-group documents produced by an international consortium with a standardised process. Each guideline assigns a level of evidence to the gene-drug pair, provides tables translating diplotypes into allele function and predicted phenotype, and grades therapeutic recommendations by strength. The design principle is deliberately narrow: guidelines assume a result is already available and address only its interpretation, which allows them to be embedded in electronic prescribing systems as decision support rules. They are updated as evidence accumulates, so the version and date matter when a recommendation is cited. Other bodies, including the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group and various regulators and professional societies, issue guidance on the same pairs, and recommendations occasionally differ in threshold or emphasis, so local policy usually specifies which source is followed. Guidelines describe implications at the level of gene-drug pairs; they do not account for an individual's comorbidities, interacting medicines or treatment goals, which remain the prescriber's responsibility.

Practical example

A hospital implementing pharmacogenomic decision support maps the diplotype-to-phenotype tables from the relevant guidelines into its electronic prescribing system, so that a stored genotype triggers an advisory message when a relevant medicine is prescribed. The project team records the guideline version used for each gene-drug pair and schedules a review when updates are published.

Common misunderstanding

These guidelines are sometimes read as recommendations to test. They deliberately take the result as given and address only interpretation, leaving the decision to test to health services and professional bodies. They are also not the only source: other groups publish guidance on the same gene-drug pairs, and recommendations do not always agree.
