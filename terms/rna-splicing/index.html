RNA splicing

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

RNA splicing is the editing step in which a cell removes the intervening sections of a freshly made RNA molecule and joins the remaining pieces together. This produces a continuous sequence ready to be read for protein synthesis. Many genes can be spliced in more than one way, so a single gene can give rise to several different RNA molecules and proteins.

Technical explanation

Splicing is catalysed by the spliceosome, a complex of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins and associated proteins that recognises donor and acceptor sites, the branch point and auxiliary splicing enhancer and silencer elements located in both exons and introns. Alternative splicing, including exon skipping, mutually exclusive exons, alternative donor or acceptor use and intron retention, expands proteome diversity and is regulated in a tissue-specific and developmentally staged manner. Variants that disrupt canonical splice dinucleotides are strong candidates for loss of function, while variants further from the junction require prediction tools and, ideally, functional confirmation. Splicing is measured from RNA sequencing by quantifying junction-spanning reads and by isoform-level inference, but short reads make full-length isoform reconstruction uncertain, and long-read sequencing resolves this more directly. Interpretation depends on tissue: a splicing effect present in neurons may be absent in blood, so a negative result in an accessible tissue does not exclude an effect elsewhere.

Practical example

A laboratory assessing a variant at the first base of an intron uses RNA extracted from blood and sees no abnormal junctions. Because the gene is expressed mainly in muscle, the team treats the blood result as uninformative rather than as evidence against an effect, and notes the limitation in the report.

Common misunderstanding

Alternative splicing is sometimes described as an occasional phenomenon affecting a few genes. It is the usual situation for multi-exon human genes. A second error is to assume that computational splice predictions are definitive; they rank plausibility and should be treated as supporting evidence rather than as demonstrated functional consequence.
