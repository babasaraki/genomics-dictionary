Data sharing

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Data sharing is making research data available to others, so that findings can be checked, combined and reused. In genomics it ranges from publishing summary statistics openly to releasing individual-level sequence data under application and approval. Sharing accelerates discovery, especially for rare conditions and outbreaks, but must respect consent, confidentiality and the interests of the communities who contributed.

Technical explanation

Genomic data sharing operates on a spectrum. Fully open release suits aggregate resources such as allele frequency summaries and reference panels, which support interpretation worldwide without distributing individual genotypes. Individual-level data are normally released through managed access, with a data access committee, a signed agreement and defined permitted uses. Public archives provide the infrastructure for both models, and community standards such as the FAIR principles describe how data should be made findable, accessible under clear conditions, interoperable and reusable. Pathogen sequence sharing has its own rhythm, because value depends on speed, and release before publication is now expected during outbreaks. Obstacles are rarely technical. Consent terms may not permit onward sharing; national law may restrict transfer of human genetic data across borders; and researchers in countries that generate data may fear being bypassed by better-resourced groups. Workable arrangements therefore combine timely release with authorship and collaboration norms, capacity support and provisions for benefit sharing. Metadata quality determines reuse value, and sequences without collection date, location or consent status contribute little.

Practical example

A laboratory sequencing an outbreak pathogen deposits consensus genomes in a public archive within days, with collection dates and districts but no information that could identify patients. A collaborating group in another country reanalyses them with its own sequences. The depositing laboratory is included in the resulting publication under a data sharing agreement settled before the outbreak began.

Common misunderstanding

Sharing is often treated as an all-or-nothing choice between open data and locked data. Most genomic resources sit between the two, with summary data open and individual-level data under managed access. A second error is to equate sharing with publishing a file: without documented metadata, provenance and consent terms, the data cannot lawfully or usefully be reused.
