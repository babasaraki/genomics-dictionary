Transmission cluster

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

A transmission cluster is a group of infections whose pathogen genomes are so similar that the cases are likely to be linked by recent spread rather than by chance. Clusters are identified by counting genetic differences between sequences or by their position on a phylogenetic tree. Finding a cluster tells investigators where to look for a shared setting, contact or source.

Technical explanation

A transmission cluster is defined operationally, using a distance threshold or a tree-based rule. Distance approaches count differing single nucleotide variants or allele mismatches between genomes and group isolates falling below an organism-specific cut-off; tree-based approaches select well-supported clades whose tips fall within a defined time window. The appropriate threshold depends on the substitution rate, the generation interval and the sequencing error profile, so a cut-off derived for Mycobacterium tuberculosis cannot be transferred to a respiratory virus. Clustering is sensitive to sampling: unsampled intermediate cases inflate apparent distances, while dense sampling of a single ward can create clusters that reflect sequencing effort rather than biology. Clusters should be treated as hypotheses about epidemiological connection, to be confirmed or discarded using contact data, admission records or exposure histories. Within-host diversity adds further uncertainty, because a consensus sequence summarises a population of viral or bacterial genomes and may change between samples from the same person. Reporting should therefore state the threshold used, the method, and the proportion of local cases sequenced.

Practical example

In a tuberculosis programme, routine sequencing shows five isolates separated by no more than a handful of single nucleotide variants, collected over eighteen months in one urban district. The cluster prompts a review of shared settings. Interviews reveal that three of the five attended the same informal workplace, which becomes the focus of active case finding and screening offered to other attendees.

Common misunderstanding

It is tempting to read a cluster as a list of people who infected each other in that order. A cluster only indicates recent common ancestry of the sampled pathogens. Direction, timing and intermediate cases are usually unresolved, and the cluster may extend to people who were never tested. Naming individuals as sources from cluster membership alone is unwarranted.
