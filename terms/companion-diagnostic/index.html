Companion diagnostic

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A companion diagnostic is a test that is formally tied to a particular medicine and is used to decide whether that medicine is suitable for a person. The test identifies a feature, often a genomic alteration or a protein level, that the medicine's evidence depends on. Regulators approve the test and the medicine as a linked pair.

Technical explanation

Companion diagnostics are regulated devices whose intended use names a specific therapeutic product, and their analytical and clinical validity are assessed in relation to the trials that supported the drug's approval. They differ from complementary diagnostics, which inform treatment decisions without being mandatory, and from laboratory-developed tests validated locally. Examples span oncology, where a tumour alteration selects a targeted therapy, and pharmacogenomics, where a germline genotype identifies people at risk of severe toxicity, as with screening for a particular HLA allele before an antiretroviral medicine is started. Practical difficulties are well documented: multiple companion assays may exist for the same biomarker with different thresholds and antibodies, which complicates comparison; a test tied to one drug may not be validated for another in the same class; and broad sequencing panels used in routine practice are often not the assay on which approval was based. Access is also uneven, since a licensed medicine is of little use where the linked test is unavailable.

Practical example

A laboratory is asked whether its broad tumour sequencing panel can be used to select a therapy whose approval was based on a specific companion assay. The team confirms that the panel detects the alteration class concerned but documents that it is not the approved companion test, and records the analytical validation and thresholds used so that the treating team can take this into account.

Common misunderstanding

Companion diagnostics are often assumed to be interchangeable whenever they target the same biomarker. Assays differ in antibodies, thresholds and the alterations they detect, and approval is specific to a drug and an assay. A result produced by a different platform may not correspond to the evidence on which the medicine was licensed.
