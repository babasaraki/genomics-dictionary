Normalisation

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

Normalisation adjusts measurements so that samples can be compared fairly. In sequencing, one sample may simply have produced more reads than another, and longer genes attract more reads than shorter ones. Normalisation removes such technical differences in scale, so that remaining differences between samples are more likely to reflect biology rather than how much data each sample yielded.

Technical explanation

Normalisation methods differ according to what they assume. Simple scaling by total counts is vulnerable to a few very highly expressed features dominating the library, so methods based on a median or trimmed ratio of counts across samples are standard for bulk expression analysis; both rest on the assumption that most features are not differentially expressed, which fails when global transcriptional output changes. Within-sample measures expressed per kilobase per million reads adjust for length and depth and are suitable for comparing features within a sample but not across samples. Transcript-level quantification tools additionally correct for fragment length, sequence composition and positional bias. Single-cell data require their own treatment, because counts per cell vary by orders of magnitude and zeros are abundant. Modern differential expression frameworks estimate size factors internally and model raw counts directly, so pre-normalised values should not be supplied to them. The general caution is that normalisation encodes an assumption about what should be comparable; when that assumption is wrong, it will manufacture apparent differences or conceal real ones, and no amount of normalisation repairs a confounded design.

Practical example

An RNA sequencing experiment includes one sample with half the sequencing depth of the others. Raw counts make its genes look uniformly lower. After size factors are estimated and applied, the sample sits with the rest, and the comparison of interest is no longer dominated by the difference in library size.

Common misunderstanding

Normalisation is sometimes assumed to make samples equivalent, so that any remaining difference must be biological. It corrects only the specific technical effects its method targets, typically depth and composition. Batch structure, degraded input material and differences in cellular composition survive normalisation untouched and require separate handling in the design or the model.
