Aneuploidy

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Aneuploidy means having a number of chromosomes that differs from the usual complement for that organism, such as one extra copy or one missing copy. In humans it usually arises when chromosomes fail to separate correctly during the formation of eggs or sperm. Aneuploidy is also very common in cancer cells, where whole chromosomes are frequently gained or lost.

Technical explanation

Aneuploidy results from non-disjunction during meiosis or mitosis, or from anaphase lag, and produces gain or loss of whole chromosomes rather than the segmental imbalances described as copy-number variants. Most autosomal aneuploidies are not compatible with continued development, and those that are, together with sex-chromosome aneuploidies, produce varied phenotypes influenced by mosaicism and by which chromosome is involved. Maternal age is an established risk factor for certain meiotic segregation errors. In tumours, aneuploidy is pervasive and arises from chromosomal instability, contributing to clonal diversity and treatment resistance. Detection methods differ in what they can see: karyotyping visualises whole-chromosome change directly, arrays and sequencing infer it from coverage or allele ratios, and cell-free DNA screening in pregnancy estimates chromosomal proportions from a maternal blood sample. The last is a screening approach, not a diagnostic one, and results require confirmatory testing because placental mosaicism and other factors can produce discordant findings.

Practical example

A laboratory analysing tumour sequencing data plots coverage and allele ratios across the genome and observes whole-chromosome gains and losses. Recording this chromosome-scale pattern alongside the point variants gives a fuller description of the tumour genome than a list of substitutions alone would provide.

Common misunderstanding

Aneuploidy is often equated with a single well-known trisomy. It covers any departure from the usual chromosome number, including losses, sex-chromosome changes and the widespread gains and losses seen in tumours. A separate error is treating a screening result based on cell-free DNA as diagnostic, when it estimates risk and requires confirmation.
