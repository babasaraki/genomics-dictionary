Biobank

Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

A biobank is an organised collection of biological samples and linked data, stored so that many researchers can study them over long periods. Samples may be blood, saliva or tissue, and the linked data can include questionnaires, measurements and health records. Participants consent to this future use, and governance arrangements determine who may access which data and under what conditions.

Technical explanation

Biobanks combine sample storage with longitudinal data linkage, and their scientific value comes from scale, breadth of phenotyping and the ability to follow participants prospectively. Cohorts with genome-wide genotyping, exome or genome sequencing and linked health records support association analysis, polygenic score development and validation, and studies of gene-environment interaction. Design choices constrain inference: most volunteer cohorts are shaped by healthy-volunteer selection, so prevalence estimates are biased even where association estimates remain usable, and participants are rarely representative of a national population. Governance is as much part of the infrastructure as the freezers, encompassing consent frameworks, access committees, controlled-access data distribution, de-identification standards, and policies on return of results and on benefit sharing with participating communities. Because genomic data cannot be fully anonymised, access models increasingly keep data inside a controlled analysis environment rather than distributing copies, while still aiming to satisfy FAIR principles for findability and reuse. Equitable partnership is especially important where samples are collected in one country and analysed in another.

Practical example

A national biobank established in an African country collects samples under a broad consent framework and sets up an access committee to review applications. Proposals involving external collaborators are required to include local analysts as full partners. Approved researchers work on the data within a secure analysis environment rather than downloading copies, and publications acknowledge the contributing communities.

Common misunderstanding

Broad consent is often taken to mean that participants agreed to anything, and open data to mean that genomic datasets can simply be published. Broad consent is bounded by a stated scope and overseen by access committees, and genomic data are generally shared under controlled access precisely because complete anonymisation cannot be guaranteed.
