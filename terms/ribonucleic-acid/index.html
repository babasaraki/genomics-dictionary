Ribonucleic acid

RNA

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Ribonucleic acid, or RNA, is a nucleic acid closely related to DNA that cells use to carry and act on genetic information. It is usually single-stranded and uses the base uracil where DNA uses thymine. Some RNA molecules carry the instructions for making a protein, while others help build proteins, regulate which genes are active, or form part of the cell's machinery.

Technical explanation

RNA is a polymer of ribonucleotides in which the sugar is ribose and the four bases are adenine, cytosine, guanine and uracil. The extra hydroxyl group on ribose makes RNA chemically less stable than DNA, which is why RNA work demands cold chains, RNase-free reagents and prompt stabilisation of samples. Functionally RNA is diverse: messenger RNA conveys coding sequence from gene to ribosome; transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA carry out translation; small nuclear RNAs drive splicing; and microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs modulate transcript stability and transcription. Many viruses, including influenza and coronaviruses, use RNA as their genetic material, so pathogen surveillance frequently begins with reverse transcription of viral RNA to complementary DNA. Because RNA abundance reflects cellular state rather than inherited sequence alone, transcriptome measurements are context-dependent: the same genome yields different RNA profiles in different tissues, at different times and under different stimuli. That context dependence is informative but also a limitation, since an RNA measurement without matched metadata is difficult to interpret.

Practical example

A surveillance laboratory receiving nasopharyngeal swabs extracts total nucleic acid, reverse-transcribes any viral RNA, and runs a quantitative polymerase chain reaction. Samples held too long at room temperature give weak or absent signal because the RNA has degraded, so the team records collection and receipt times alongside each result and repeats testing when transport conditions were poor.

Common misunderstanding

People often assume RNA is simply a disposable copy of DNA. Messenger RNA does act as an intermediate, but most cellular RNA is never translated and instead performs structural, catalytic or regulatory work. Assuming that RNA levels equal protein levels is a second error: transcript abundance and protein abundance correlate only loosely, because translation and protein turnover are separately regulated.
