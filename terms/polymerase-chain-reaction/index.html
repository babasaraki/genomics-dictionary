Polymerase chain reaction

PCR

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

The polymerase chain reaction, abbreviated PCR, is a laboratory method that makes many copies of a chosen piece of DNA. Short synthetic primers mark the start and end of the region, and repeated cycles of heating and cooling let an enzyme copy it again and again. A small amount of starting material can become enough to detect or sequence.

Technical explanation

PCR amplifies a defined region exponentially through repeated thermal cycles of denaturation, primer annealing and extension by a thermostable DNA polymerase. Specificity comes from the primer pair, which must bind the intended flanking sequences and not elsewhere; sensitivity comes from the exponential increase, which in principle allows a single template molecule to be detected. Variants of the method extend its reach: reverse transcription PCR converts RNA to complementary DNA first, multiplex PCR amplifies many targets in one reaction, and digital PCR partitions the reaction to count molecules absolutely. PCR underpins much of modern genomics, from amplicon sequencing and library amplification to diagnostic detection of pathogens. Its limitations are well characterised. Primer binding sites can carry variants that reduce or abolish amplification, producing a false negative in a diagnostic assay or a dropout in an amplicon panel. The polymerase introduces errors that are then amplified, and amplification is not perfectly even across templates, so the proportions of products do not reliably reflect the proportions of input molecules. The extreme sensitivity also makes carry-over contamination a constant risk.

Practical example

A surveillance laboratory using a tiled amplicon scheme to sequence a virus notices one amplicon failing repeatedly in recently collected samples. Inspection shows a variant beneath a primer binding site in the circulating lineage. The primer scheme is updated, and in the interim the affected region is reported as ambiguous rather than as reference sequence.

Common misunderstanding

PCR is widely assumed to be an all-or-nothing test of whether a sequence is present. Failure to amplify has several explanations besides absence of the target: a variant under a primer site, inhibitors carried through extraction, degraded material or too little input. Equally, a product does not prove viable organism, only that the target sequence was present.
