Amplicon sequencing

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Amplicon sequencing is a method in which chosen regions of a genome are copied many times by the polymerase chain reaction and only those copies are sequenced. Because the target is enriched before sequencing, very small amounts of pathogen material can be read even when a sample is dominated by human or environmental DNA. Tiled sets of overlapping primers can cover a whole small genome.

Technical explanation

In amplicon sequencing, primers define the regions to be amplified, and the resulting products are converted into a sequencing library. For pathogen work the commonest designs are a single marker region, such as a ribosomal RNA gene for bacterial community profiling, and tiled whole-genome schemes in which overlapping amplicons of one or two kilobases span a viral genome. Enrichment gives high on-target depth from low-biomass samples and keeps sequencing costs modest, which is why tiled schemes became the standard approach for routine viral genomic surveillance. The dependence on primer binding is also the main weakness. Variants under a primer site cause amplicon dropout, producing systematic gaps that must be masked rather than read as deletions, so schemes need periodic revision as the target evolves. Amplification introduces bias in relative abundance, limits detection of structural rearrangements that span primer boundaries, and makes cross-contamination between high-titre and low-titre samples a real risk. Primer sequences must be trimmed before variant calling, otherwise primer-encoded bases can be mistaken for genuine variants.

Practical example

A laboratory running a tiled scheme for a respiratory virus notices that genomes from the newest circulating lineage all lack the same region. Coverage plots show one amplicon failing consistently. Aligning the primer sequences against recent genomes reveals two mismatches at the three-prime end of the forward primer. The scheme is updated with a redesigned primer and the affected samples are repeated.

Common misunderstanding

Amplicon data are often treated as though they measure how much of each organism is present. Amplification is not uniform: primer mismatches, template quality and cycle number all change yields, so read counts reflect amplification efficiency as well as abundance. Likewise, a gap in an amplicon genome usually means the amplicon failed, not that the sequence is deleted.
