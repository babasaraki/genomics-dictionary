Controlled access

Ethics and data governance Published

Definition

Controlled access means that individual-level genomic data are not downloadable by anyone, but released only to approved researchers who apply, state their purpose and agree to conditions. A committee checks that the request matches what participants consented to. Summary results from the same study are often published openly, because they carry much less risk of identifying anyone.

Technical explanation

Controlled access is the standard governance model for human individual-level sequence and genotype data. A data access committee evaluates applications against the consent terms attached to each dataset, the applicant's institutional standing and the proposed use, and approval is accompanied by a data access agreement prohibiting re-identification attempts, onward transfer and uses outside the stated purpose. Technical implementation ranges from authenticated download to trusted research environments where analysis is performed inside a controlled system and only aggregate results are exported, which reduces proliferation of copies. Federated analysis extends this further by sending the analysis to the data. Costs are real: application review takes time, delays disadvantage smaller groups, and inconsistent requirements across repositories create substantial administrative load, which has prompted work on standardised consent codes and machine-readable data use conditions. Controlled access reduces rather than eliminates risk, since approved users can misuse data, and aggregate outputs can leak information. It also depends on an enforcement mechanism, as agreements without monitoring or sanctions offer limited protection in practice.

Practical example

A research group wishes to study variant frequencies in a disease cohort. Summary statistics are available openly and answer part of the question. For individual-level analysis the group submits an application describing the purpose and the analysis environment, and the access committee approves use limited to that disease area, in line with the consent given by participants.

Common misunderstanding

Controlled access is sometimes presented as making data anonymous and therefore safe. Access control is a governance measure, not a property of the data: the genotypes remain identifiable. The protection comes from who is allowed in, what they agree to and whether the agreement is enforced, which is why trusted environments and auditing matter alongside the application process.
