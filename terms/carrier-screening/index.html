Carrier screening

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Carrier screening is testing that tells a person whether they carry one copy of a variant for a recessive condition. Carriers of such conditions are typically healthy, because the second working copy of the gene is enough. The information becomes relevant when two carriers of the same condition have children together, since the condition can then appear.

Technical explanation

Carrier screening targets autosomal recessive and X-linked conditions and is offered before or during pregnancy, or more broadly to populations in which particular variants are common. Panels range from a handful of conditions to several hundred genes, and expanded panels are usually offered without regard to reported ancestry, partly because self-reported identity is a poor guide to which variants an individual carries and partly because admixed ancestries are poorly served by ancestry-targeted panels. Analytical design matters: genotyping a fixed set of variants is cheap but detects only what it is designed to detect, while sequencing finds more variants at the cost of more uncertain classifications. Technically difficult genes require dedicated assays, for example because of pseudogene homology or common structural rearrangements. Residual risk is intrinsic to the approach, since no panel is exhaustive, and results are therefore reported as reduced rather than eliminated risk. Screening is most useful when paired with counselling that explains reproductive options without directing a choice.

Practical example

A reproductive genetics service offers an expanded carrier panel to both partners of a couple planning a pregnancy. Both are found to carry variants in the same gene for a recessive haemoglobin disorder. A genetic counsellor explains the inheritance pattern, the chance that a given pregnancy is affected, the residual risk left by the panel's limits, and the options available, without recommending any particular course.

Common misunderstanding

Being called a carrier is often heard as having a disease in mild form. For most recessive conditions carriers are unaffected, and the finding is reproductive information rather than a personal diagnosis. A negative screen is likewise not a guarantee: panels cover selected variants and genes, so some risk always remains after testing.
