Loss of heterozygosity

LOH

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Most human chromosomes come in pairs, so a person can carry two different versions of a gene. Loss of heterozygosity, abbreviated LOH, happens when a cell loses one of those versions, leaving only one kind present at that location. In tumours this often removes the remaining working copy of a gene that was restraining growth.

Technical explanation

LOH describes the conversion of a heterozygous locus to a state in which only one parental allele is represented. Mechanisms include deletion of one allele, mitotic recombination, gene conversion, chromosome loss and chromosome loss followed by duplication of the remaining homologue, the last producing copy-neutral LOH in which total copy number appears unchanged. Detection therefore requires allelic information rather than depth alone: single-nucleotide polymorphism arrays and sequencing-based analysis of B-allele frequency can identify copy-neutral events that read-depth methods miss. In cancer genetics LOH commonly supplies the second hit at a tumour suppressor locus, so its presence alongside a germline pathogenic variant supports a mechanistic interpretation. Broader LOH patterns across the genome contribute to homologous recombination deficiency scores. Interpretation is constrained by tumour purity and ploidy, by the density of informative heterozygous markers, and by the fact that LOH alone does not establish that the remaining allele is non-functional.

Practical example

A laboratory analyses allele fractions across a tumour genome and finds a chromosomal segment where heterozygous positions in the matched normal sample appear homozygous in the tumour, while total copy number is unchanged. This copy-neutral event spans a tumour suppressor gene in which the person carries a germline pathogenic variant, supporting loss of the remaining intact allele in the tumour.

Common misunderstanding

LOH is often assumed to mean that DNA has been deleted. Copy-neutral events leave the total amount of DNA unchanged while still removing one parental allele, so copy-number analysis alone will miss them. It is also not synonymous with gene inactivation: the retained allele must itself be defective for function to be lost.
