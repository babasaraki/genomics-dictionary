Homologous recombination deficiency

HRD

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Homologous recombination is a precise way of mending breaks that run through both strands of the DNA double helix, using the matching chromosome as a template. Homologous recombination deficiency, abbreviated HRD, means a tumour has lost this accurate repair route and must rely on error-prone alternatives. The resulting scars across the genome can be measured.

Technical explanation

HRD arises from loss of function in BRCA1, BRCA2 or other genes of the homologous recombination pathway, through germline or somatic variants, promoter methylation or copy-number loss. Because the functional defect can be reached by many routes, clinical assays usually combine a search for causative variants with a genomic scar score derived from patterns such as loss of heterozygosity, telomeric allelic imbalance and large-scale state transitions, and newer approaches use mutational signature analysis of substitutions, small insertions and deletions and rearrangements. The clinical relevance lies in synthetic lethality: cells without homologous recombination depend on alternative repair and are sensitive to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibition and to platinum agents. Important limitations apply. Scar scores record historical deficiency and may remain positive after a tumour has restored function, for example through reversion variants that repair the original reading frame, which is a recognised resistance mechanism. Thresholds differ between commercial assays, and scores are not interchangeable across tumour types or platforms.

Practical example

An ovarian tumour is tested with an assay that reports both pathogenic variants in homologous recombination genes and a genomic instability score. No causative variant is found, but the instability score is above the assay threshold, consistent with deficiency from another route. The molecular tumour board records which assay and threshold were used, since scores from different platforms are not directly comparable.

Common misunderstanding

A deficiency result is sometimes read as proof that the person carries an inherited BRCA variant. Deficiency can be entirely somatic or epigenetic, and germline status requires a separate test on a normal sample. The reverse error is also common: a tumour can regain repair function and resist treatment while its genomic scars persist.
