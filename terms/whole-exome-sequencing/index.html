Whole-exome sequencing

WES

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

Whole-exome sequencing, abbreviated WES, reads the protein-coding portions of the genome, known collectively as the exome. These make up a small fraction of the total DNA but contain a large share of the variants currently known to cause inherited disease. Sequencing only this fraction is cheaper than sequencing everything and produces a smaller, more tractable dataset.

Technical explanation

Exome sequencing uses capture probes to enrich coding exons and the immediately flanking splice regions before short-read sequencing. Capture efficiency is uneven: regions with extreme GC content, segmental duplications and homologous pseudogenes are poorly covered, so laboratories report coverage metrics per gene and may fill gaps with targeted assays. The exome does not include most regulatory and deep intronic sequence, mitochondrial DNA is covered only incidentally unless specifically targeted, and copy-number and structural variants are detectable only indirectly from read depth, with limited sensitivity for small events. Interpretation usually proceeds by filtering on population allele frequency, predicted consequence and inheritance model, and is considerably more powerful when parental samples are available. Whole-genome sequencing is increasingly chosen instead, because it gives more even coverage, captures non-coding and structural variation, and avoids capture bias, though it generates larger data volumes and more variants of uncertain significance. Exome sequencing remains widely used where sequencing capacity, storage or cost constrain the choice.

Practical example

A diagnostic laboratory sequences the exomes of a child with an unexplained neurodevelopmental disorder and both parents. Filtering for rare variants that are absent from both parents identifies a de novo missense change in a gene with an established association with that phenotype. The report notes the genes that fell below the coverage threshold, so the referring clinician knows which regions were not adequately assessed.

Common misunderstanding

The exome is sometimes described as the important part of the genome, implying that whatever is not coding does not matter. Coding sequence is where interpretation is currently strongest, not where all function lies. Regulatory, deep intronic and structural variation causes disease too, and a negative exome leaves those possibilities open.
