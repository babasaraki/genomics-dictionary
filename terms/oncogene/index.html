Oncogene

Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

An oncogene is a gene whose overactivity can drive a cell to divide when it should not. Most oncogenes are altered forms of normal genes, called proto-oncogenes, that control growth signals under tight regulation. A change that locks such a gene into a permanently switched-on state can contribute to the development of a tumour.

Technical explanation

Oncogenes act through gain of function, so a single altered copy is usually sufficient, in contrast to the two-hit loss of function typical of tumour suppressor genes. Activation occurs by several mechanisms: missense variants in hotspot codons that lock a signalling protein in its active conformation, gene amplification that multiplies copy number, chromosomal translocations that create fusion proteins or place a gene under a strong promoter, and insertions or deletions that remove regulatory domains. The resulting proteins often sit in growth factor receptor and intracellular signalling pathways. Because the altered protein is frequently essential to the tumour cell's survival, oncogenes are attractive drug targets, and several licensed therapies inhibit specific activated kinases or fusion proteins. Limitations are important: tumours commonly acquire resistance through secondary variants in the target, bypass signalling or selection of pre-existing subclones, and the presence of an activating alteration does not by itself predict response unless the association has been established for that tumour type.

Practical example

Sequencing of a lung tumour identifies a fusion that joins an intact kinase domain to the regulatory region of an unrelated, highly expressed gene. The kinase is therefore transcribed far above its usual level and signals constitutively. The alteration is recorded as an oncogenic driver, and the report notes the tumour types in which matched inhibitors have established evidence.

Common misunderstanding

Oncogene is often used as though it meant a gene that exists only to cause cancer. The unaltered counterparts are ordinary genes with essential roles in growth and development, and they become oncogenic only when a somatic change makes their activity excessive or unregulated. Inheriting a proto-oncogene is simply being human.
