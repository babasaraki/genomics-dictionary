FASTA format

Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

FASTA is a plain-text format for storing sequences. Each record begins with a header line starting with a greater-than sign, followed by the sequence itself on the lines beneath. It is used for reference genomes, gene and protein sequences, assemblies and consensus sequences. Unlike FASTQ, a FASTA record carries no quality information, only the letters of the sequence.

Technical explanation

FASTA is deliberately minimal: a header line introduced by a greater-than sign and then the residues, conventionally wrapped at a fixed line width. Nucleotide records use the IUPAC alphabet, so ambiguity codes such as N or R are legal and common in consensus sequences. Protein records use single-letter amino acid codes. Everything after that sign is free text, which is both the format's convenience and its weakness: identifier conventions differ between databases, and a mismatch between the contig names in a reference FASTA and those in an annotation or variant file is a routine source of pipeline failure. Reference FASTA files are usually accompanied by an index allowing random access to any region without reading the whole file, and by a dictionary used by variant callers. Limitations follow from the simplicity. There is no per-base confidence, no structured metadata and no record of how the sequence was produced, so a consensus with poorly covered regions masked as N looks superficially identical in form to a finished reference. Provenance must be recorded elsewhere.

Practical example

A laboratory submits a consensus genome to a public database and the upload is rejected for exceeding the permitted proportion of ambiguous bases. Inspecting the FASTA shows long runs of N where amplicon dropout left no coverage. The team re-sequences with a revised primer scheme rather than submitting a sequence whose gaps would distort lineage assignment.

Common misunderstanding

A FASTA header is often assumed to be a reliable identifier of what the sequence is. It is only free text written by whoever produced the file, and it is routinely truncated at the first space by downstream tools. Two files whose headers differ in naming style may hold the same chromosomes, and identical headers may hold different genome builds.
