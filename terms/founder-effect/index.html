Founder effect

Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

Definition

A founder effect occurs when a small group separates from a larger population and establishes a new one. The founders carry only part of the original population's variation, so whatever alleles they happen to have become the starting point for everyone descended from them. An allele that was rare in the source population can end up far more common in the new one.

Technical explanation

The founder effect is a special case of drift acting at the moment a population is established by a small number of individuals. Sampling error at foundation shifts allele frequencies away from the source, reduces overall diversity, and raises the probability that two descendants share an allele identical by descent. The consequences are visible in genomic data as longer haplotype blocks and extended linkage disequilibrium, elevated runs of homozygosity, and a frequency spectrum enriched for a subset of alleles while others are absent entirely. Clinically, founder populations often show raised frequencies of particular recessive conditions, which is why some carrier screening panels are designed around a specific population history rather than from a general variant list. The same structure can make gene mapping easier, since fewer distinct haplotypes segregate. The limitation is generalisability: a variant explaining many cases in a founder population may be absent elsewhere, and classification evidence gathered in one founder group should not be assumed to transfer to another.

Practical example

A clinical genetics service notices that a particular recessive condition appears more often than expected among families from one geographically isolated community, and that affected individuals share the same variant on the same surrounding haplotype. The pattern is consistent with a single ancestral introduction, and the service documents the haplotype in its regional variant database for future interpretation.

Common misunderstanding

Founder effects are often assumed to require a dramatic migration, and to imply that the founding group was somehow unhealthy. Neither is necessary. An ordinary demographic event, such as a few families settling a new area or a population contracting and later recovering, is sufficient, and the alleles that become common are simply those the founders happened to carry.
