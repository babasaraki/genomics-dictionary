Genotype imputation

Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

Definition

Genotype imputation fills in positions that were not directly measured, by comparing the positions that were measured against a reference panel of people whose genomes are known in detail. Because nearby variants travel together on shared stretches of chromosome, matching those stretches lets software predict the missing genotypes, together with a confidence value for each prediction.

Technical explanation

Imputation takes sparse genotypes, usually from an array, phases them into haplotypes, and matches those haplotypes against a dense reference panel using hidden Markov models, returning posterior genotype probabilities or dosages plus a quality metric such as an info or r-squared score. It raises effective marker density by one to two orders of magnitude, enabling meta-analysis across studies genotyped on different arrays and improving fine-mapping resolution. Accuracy depends on reference panel size, on how closely panel haplotypes resemble those in the target sample, and on allele frequency, since rare variants impute far less reliably than common ones. Panels built predominantly from European-ancestry samples impute African and admixed genomes poorly, which is a practical driver for expanding diverse and multi-ancestry panels. Downstream analyses should use dosages and filter on the quality metric rather than hard-calling genotypes, because treating uncertain imputed calls as though they had been observed propagates error into association statistics and into any score built from them.

Practical example

A group analysing samples from several West African cohorts imputes against a panel dominated by European-ancestry haplotypes and obtains poor quality scores for a large fraction of variants. Re-running the analysis against a multi-ancestry panel recovers a substantial number of well-imputed variants, and the association analysis is repeated on the improved dataset.

Common misunderstanding

Imputed genotypes are often treated as though they had been measured directly. They are probabilistic predictions conditional on a reference panel, and their reliability varies by variant, by allele frequency and by how well the panel represents the sample. Laboratories therefore confirm any imputed result by direct assay before it is used for reporting purposes.
