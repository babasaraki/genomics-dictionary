Chromosome

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

A chromosome is a long molecule of DNA wound around proteins and packaged so that it fits inside a cell and can be moved reliably when the cell divides. Human cells typically carry forty-six chromosomes arranged in twenty-three pairs, with one member of each pair inherited from each parent. Chromosomes become visible under a microscope when a cell is dividing.

Technical explanation

Eukaryotic chromosomes are linear DNA molecules organised with histones into nucleosomes and higher-order chromatin, with a centromere that anchors the spindle and telomeres that cap each end. Human somatic cells are diploid with twenty-two autosome pairs plus two sex chromosomes, while gametes are haploid. Chromosome structure is functionally important: compaction state influences transcription, and three-dimensional folding brings distant regulatory elements close to promoters. Changes in chromosome number or structure are detectable by karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridisation, chromosomal microarray and, increasingly, by sequencing-based copy-number analysis. Bacterial chromosomes differ substantially, typically being circular, lacking histones and often accompanied by plasmids that can be gained or lost. A practical limitation of chromosome-level analysis is resolution: classical karyotyping detects rearrangements of several megabases but misses smaller imbalances, whereas array and sequencing methods detect much smaller events yet may be ambiguous about whether two segments are joined, so methods are frequently combined.

Practical example

A cytogenetics laboratory investigating recurrent pregnancy loss cultures cells, arrests them in metaphase and examines banded chromosomes. A balanced reciprocal translocation seen in one partner would not change the total amount of DNA present, which is precisely why a copy-number array alone could miss it and why the microscope-based method retains a role.

Common misunderstanding

A frequent misunderstanding is that chromosomes are the X-shaped bodies drawn in textbooks at all times. That shape appears only in dividing cells, after replication has produced two sister chromatids; for most of the cell cycle chromosomes are extended chromatin fibres. It is also incorrect to equate the sex chromosomes with a person's gender identity or social role.
