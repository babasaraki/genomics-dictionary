Dominant inheritance

Genetics foundations Published

Definition

Dominant inheritance describes a pattern in which a single altered copy of a gene is enough to produce a trait or condition, even though the other copy is unchanged. A person with such a variant has, on average, a one in two chance of passing it to each child. Dominant conditions often appear in several generations of a family.

Technical explanation

Dominance is a property of the relationship between a variant and a phenotype, not an intrinsic property of a gene. Mechanisms include haploinsufficiency, where half the normal gene product is insufficient, and dominant-negative or gain-of-function effects, where the altered product interferes with or exceeds normal activity. Autosomal dominant pedigrees typically show affected individuals in successive generations with male-to-male transmission possible, but the pattern is frequently obscured by incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, de novo occurrence in the first affected person, mosaicism in a parent, and small family size. Age-dependent penetrance means an unaffected older relative does not exclude a variant. Quantitative traits rarely behave in a simply dominant manner, and most common conditions are polygenic rather than following Mendelian patterns. Recurrence figures quoted in counselling describe transmission of the variant, which is distinct from the probability of developing the condition when penetrance is incomplete; the two are often conflated in general writing.

Practical example

A clinical team reviewing a family tree sees an affected child and apparently unaffected parents. Testing shows one parent carries the same variant without features of the condition, consistent with reduced penetrance, so the family discussion covers variable outcomes rather than treating the parent's result as excluding inheritance.

Common misunderstanding

Dominant is often understood to mean stronger or more harmful. It describes only whether one altered copy suffices to produce the phenotype under study. It is also wrong to assume everyone carrying a dominant disease-associated variant will be affected: penetrance is frequently incomplete and severity varies widely within one family.
