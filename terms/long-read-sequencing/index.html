Long-read sequencing

Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

Definition

Long-read sequencing reads very long stretches of DNA in one piece, often tens of thousands of bases, rather than many short fragments. Long reads can span repeated regions and large rearrangements that short reads cannot cross, so they make assembly and structural analysis easier. Historically they made more errors per base, although the chemistries have improved substantially.

Technical explanation

Long-read platforms read single molecules without the short fixed read length imposed by cyclic short-read chemistry. Two approaches dominate in practice: single-molecule real-time sequencing, which observes a polymerase incorporating labelled nucleotides and can read the same circular template repeatedly to build a highly accurate consensus, and nanopore sequencing, which reads an intact strand as it passes through a pore. Read lengths are limited mainly by the length of the input molecules, so high-molecular-weight extraction becomes the critical laboratory step. Long reads resolve repeats, segmental duplications and structural variants directly, enable phasing of variants across long haplotype blocks, support contiguous de novo assembly, and in some chemistries detect base modifications such as methylation without a separate bisulfite step. Limitations remain: raw per-read error is higher than for short reads unless consensus or repeat-pass strategies are applied, homopolymer runs are a recurring difficulty, cost per base is higher, and the requirement for intact long molecules makes degraded or formalin-fixed material a poor substrate.

Practical example

A laboratory investigating a suspected large duplication in a family cannot determine its orientation or breakpoints from short-read coverage alone. Using carefully extracted high-molecular-weight DNA, long reads spanning the whole rearrangement are generated, and single reads crossing both junctions show the orientation directly rather than by inference.

Common misunderstanding

Long reads are often described as simply better than short reads. The trade is one of properties, not quality. Long reads provide contiguity and direct observation of structure; short reads provide low cost and high per-base accuracy at depth. The right choice depends on whether the question concerns single bases or the arrangement of large segments.
