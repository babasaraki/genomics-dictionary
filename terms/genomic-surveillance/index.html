Genomic surveillance

Pathogen genomics Published

Definition

Genomic surveillance is the routine sequencing of pathogens collected over time so that changes in the population of circulating strains can be seen early. Rather than testing one outbreak and stopping, laboratories sequence a sample of cases every week and share the results. This shows which lineages are spreading, when a new one arrives, and whether resistance or immune escape is increasing.

Technical explanation

Genomic surveillance is a continuous public health function that couples diagnostic testing to sequencing, analysis and timely reporting. Specimens are sampled according to a defined strategy, which may be random among positive diagnostic samples, targeted at travellers, severe cases or treatment failures, or drawn from environmental sources such as wastewater. Sequences are assigned to lineages or sequence types, placed on a phylogeny and compared with earlier national and international data, usually through shared repositories. The outputs feed risk assessment: detection of a new variant, growth of one lineage relative to others, emergence of resistance determinants, or evidence that a vaccine or treatment target is changing. Interpretation depends heavily on how representative the sampled isolates are, and on metadata quality, since a sequence without a collection date or location contributes little. Capacity is unevenly distributed, and a lineage that appears to originate in a well-sequencing country may simply have been detected there first. Sustainable surveillance therefore requires predictable funding, trained staff, local analytical capacity and governance arrangements that cover consent, data protection and equitable use of the sequences generated.

Practical example

A regional laboratory in West Africa sequences a fixed proportion of influenza-positive respiratory samples each week during the season. Over two months the proportion belonging to one clade rises steadily, and several sequences carry changes in the antigenic region of the surface glycoprotein. The team flags this to the national influenza programme, which uses it alongside vaccine composition advice and hospital admission trends.

Common misunderstanding

A common error is to treat the number of sequences in a lineage as a measure of how common that lineage is. It is a measure of what was sampled and sequenced. Without knowing the sampling strategy and the testing rate, counts describe laboratory activity as much as the underlying epidemic, so proportions should be read against a documented sampling frame.
