Pathogen genomics

Sequencing and analysis of bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites for outbreak investigation, antimicrobial resistance surveillance and public health decision-making.

27 entries. Open these in the searchable dictionary.

  • Amplicon sequencing

    Amplicon sequencing is a method in which chosen regions of a genome are copied many times by the polymerase chain reaction and only those copies are sequenced. Because the target is enriched before sequencing, very small amounts of pathogen material can be read even when a sample is dominated by human or environmental DNA. Tiled sets of overlapping primers can cover a whole small genome.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)

    Antimicrobial resistance, abbreviated AMR, is the ability of bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites to survive medicines that would normally stop them. The microbe becomes resistant, not the person or animal being treated. Resistance arises through genetic change or through genes acquired from other microbes, and it spreads when resistant organisms are passed on or when resistance genes move between them.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Antimicrobial resistance gene

    An antimicrobial resistance gene is a stretch of DNA whose product helps a microbe survive a drug, for example by destroying the drug, pumping it out or changing its target. Such genes may sit on the chromosome or on mobile pieces of DNA that move between microbes. Sequencing can detect known resistance genes directly from a genome.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST)

    The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, abbreviated BLAST, searches a query sequence against a database and returns the entries that resemble it most, together with a measure of how surprising each match is. It is the usual first question asked of an unknown sequence: what else looks like this, and how closely? Variants of the tool handle nucleotide and protein queries.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Pathogen genomics Published

  • Clade

    A clade is a group on an evolutionary tree made up of one ancestor and all of its descendants. In pathogen genomics, clade labels give convenient names to broad branches of a virus or bacterium, so that scientists discussing a seasonal influenza clade or a SARS-CoV-2 clade mean the same set of sequences. Clades are nested: a smaller clade sits inside a larger one.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Consensus sequence

    A consensus sequence is a single representative sequence built from many overlapping sequencing reads covering the same part of a genome. At each position the base seen in most reads is chosen, and positions with too little data are usually masked with the letter N. For pathogens, the consensus genome is the file that is shared, compared and used to assign a lineage.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Core genome MLST (cgMLST)

    Core genome multilocus sequence typing, abbreviated cgMLST, extends the idea of typing by gene alleles from a handful of genes to the hundreds or thousands of genes shared by almost all members of a species. Each gene is given an allele number, and two isolates are compared by counting how many of those numbers differ. The result is a portable, high-resolution measure of relatedness.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • De novo assembly

    De novo assembly reconstructs a genome from sequencing reads alone, without using a reference sequence of the same organism as a guide. It is the approach taken when no suitable reference exists, when the sample may contain sequence the reference lacks, or when the aim is to describe structure rather than catalogue differences from a template.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Pathogen genomics Published

  • Genomic surveillance

    Genomic surveillance is the routine sequencing of pathogens collected over time so that changes in the population of circulating strains can be seen early. Rather than testing one outbreak and stopping, laboratories sequence a sample of cases every week and share the results. This shows which lineages are spreading, when a new one arrives, and whether resistance or immune escape is increasing.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)

    Horizontal gene transfer, abbreviated HGT, is the movement of genetic material between organisms other than from parent to offspring. Bacteria can take up free DNA from their surroundings, receive DNA through direct contact with another cell, or be injected with it by a virus. This is why a resistance gene can appear in a species that never had it before.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Immune escape variant

    An immune escape variant is a version of a pathogen carrying changes that make it less easily recognised by antibodies produced after infection or vaccination. The changes usually sit in the parts of a surface protein that antibodies bind. Escape is partial and relative: it reduces the protection a population has built up rather than abolishing immunity.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • In silico serotyping

    In silico serotyping means working out which serotype an organism belongs to by examining its genome sequence on a computer, instead of testing it with antibodies in the laboratory. The method looks for the genes that produce the surface structures the antibodies would have recognised. It gives results that can be compared with decades of older laboratory typing data.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Metagenomics

    Metagenomics is the sequencing of all the genetic material in a sample, without first growing or targeting any single organism. From a swab, a stool sample or river water, it produces reads from bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites and the host together. Analysts then sort the reads by likely origin to see what is present.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Mobile genetic element (MGE)

    A mobile genetic element, abbreviated MGE, is a piece of DNA that can move, either within a genome or between organisms. Plasmids, transposons, insertion sequences, integrons and bacteriophages are all examples. Mobile elements matter in pathogen genomics because they carry resistance and virulence genes from one microbe to another, often across species.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Molecular clock

    A molecular clock is the idea that genetic changes accumulate in a population at a roughly steady average rate, so the number of differences between two sequences can be used to estimate how long ago they shared an ancestor. In outbreak work, clocks let analysts put dates on the branching points of a tree and estimate when an outbreak began.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)

    Multilocus sequence typing, abbreviated MLST, is a way of labelling bacterial strains by reading the sequence of a small set of housekeeping genes, usually around seven. Each distinct sequence of a gene gets a number, and the combination of numbers defines a sequence type. Because the labels are just numbers, laboratories anywhere can compare strains without exchanging the sequences themselves.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • One Health

    One Health is an approach that treats the health of people, animals and the environment as connected, and organises surveillance and response across all three. Many infections and most antimicrobial resistance problems move between these domains, so looking at only one gives an incomplete picture. In practice it means human, veterinary and environmental services sharing data and planning together.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Outbreak investigation

    An outbreak investigation is the organised effort to find out why more cases of an infection than expected are occurring, who is affected, and how to stop further spread. It combines case finding, interviews, laboratory testing and, increasingly, sequencing of the pathogen. The aim is practical: identify the source, interrupt transmission and prevent the same thing happening again.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Pango lineage

    A Pango lineage is a label from a naming system used to describe fine-grained groups of SARS-CoV-2 genomes that share recent common ancestry. Names such as B.1.1.7 or BA.2 use letters and dots to show how groups branch from one another. The system was designed so that newly spreading groups can be named quickly during an epidemic.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Pathogen genomics

    Pathogen genomics is the study of the complete genetic material of organisms that cause disease, including viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites. Reading a pathogen's genome shows which species or strain is present, how closely one sample resembles another, and which genetic changes it carries. Public health laboratories use these details to follow outbreaks, detect resistance to medicines and judge whether control measures are working.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Phylodynamics

    Phylodynamics is the study of how the shape of a pathogen's evolutionary tree is produced by the way the infection spreads. Because each transmission event leaves a trace in the genomes, the pattern of branching can be used to estimate things epidemiologists care about, such as whether an epidemic is growing, when it started and how populations are connected.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Phylogenetic tree

    A phylogenetic tree is a diagram that shows how sequences are related by descent from common ancestors. Each tip is a sampled sequence, each internal branching point is an inferred ancestor, and branch lengths usually represent the amount of genetic change or elapsed time. Trees are the main way outbreak analysts visualise which pathogen samples belong together.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Plasmid

    A plasmid is a small piece of DNA, usually circular, that sits inside a bacterial cell separately from the chromosome and copies itself independently. Plasmids are not essential for basic survival, but they often carry genes that are useful in particular conditions, such as resistance to antibiotics or heavy metals. Many can be passed from one bacterium to another.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Reproduction number (R0)

    The reproduction number is the average number of new infections caused by one infected person. The basic reproduction number, written R0, applies to a fully susceptible population, while the effective reproduction number applies to the population as it actually is, with immunity and control measures in place. A value above one means the epidemic is growing.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Transmission cluster

    A transmission cluster is a group of infections whose pathogen genomes are so similar that the cases are likely to be linked by recent spread rather than by chance. Clusters are identified by counting genetic differences between sequences or by their position on a phylogenetic tree. Finding a cluster tells investigators where to look for a shared setting, contact or source.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Wastewater surveillance

    Wastewater surveillance means testing sewage for traces of pathogens shed by the people who use the connected toilets. Because samples pool whole communities, they can show that an infection is circulating without anyone needing to be tested individually. Laboratories measure how much pathogen material is present over time, and may sequence it to identify which variants are there.

    Pathogen genomics Published

  • Zoonosis

    A zoonosis is an infection that can pass from animals to people. Rabies, brucellosis, Lassa fever and many others are zoonoses. Some spread only from animals to individual people, while others, once introduced, can then spread between people. Genomic methods help identify the animal source, estimate when the jump happened and distinguish repeated spillovers from sustained human transmission.

    Pathogen genomics Published
