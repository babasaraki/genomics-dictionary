Sequencing and laboratory methods

How genomic data is produced: extraction, library preparation, sequencing chemistries and platforms, quality control and the measurements that describe a run.

27 entries. Open these in the searchable dictionary.

  • Breadth of coverage

    Breadth of coverage is the proportion of the target region that was read at all, or that reached a stated minimum depth. It answers a different question from read depth: not how many times a position was read, but how much of the intended region was covered. It is usually reported as a percentage of target bases.

  • Contamination control

    Contamination control covers the laboratory practices that stop unwanted DNA or RNA entering a reaction or moving between samples. Because amplification and sequencing can detect tiny amounts of material, even invisible traces matter. Measures include separating work areas, using negative controls, handling reagents carefully and watching for suspicious results in the data.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Cycle threshold (Ct)

    The cycle threshold, written Ct, is the number of amplification cycles a quantitative PCR test needs before its fluorescent signal becomes clearly detectable. A sample containing more of the target reaches that point sooner, so a lower cycle threshold indicates more starting material. The value depends on the particular assay and instrument used.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • FASTQ format

    FASTQ is the standard text format for raw sequencing reads. Each read occupies four lines: an identifier, the sequence of bases, a separator, and a string of characters encoding how confident the instrument was about each base. Because the quality string travels with the sequence, downstream software can trim or discard unreliable positions rather than treating every base as equally trustworthy.

  • Library preparation

    Library preparation is the set of laboratory steps that turns extracted DNA or RNA into a form a sequencing instrument can read. The material is cut or copied into fragments of suitable length, short known sequences called adapters are attached to the ends, and sample-specific barcodes are added. The quality of this step largely determines the quality of the data.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Long-read sequencing

    Long-read sequencing reads very long stretches of DNA in one piece, often tens of thousands of bases, rather than many short fragments. Long reads can span repeated regions and large rearrangements that short reads cannot cross, so they make assembly and structural analysis easier. Historically they made more errors per base, although the chemistries have improved substantially.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Microarray

    A microarray is a slide or chip carrying many thousands of short known DNA sequences arranged in fixed positions. Labelled material from a sample sticks to the spots that match it, and the pattern of signal shows which sequences were present and in what quantity. Arrays measure predetermined targets rather than reading sequence directly.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Multiplexing and barcoding

    Multiplexing means sequencing many samples together in one run. Each sample is given its own short identifying sequence, called a barcode or index, during library preparation. After the run, software sorts the reads back to the sample they came from by reading those barcodes, a step called demultiplexing. This shares the cost of a run across many samples.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Nanopore sequencing

    Nanopore sequencing reads DNA or RNA by pulling a single strand through a tiny protein pore in a membrane. As each part of the strand passes through, it changes an electrical current in a characteristic way, and software converts that changing signal into a sequence of bases. The devices are small and produce data as the run proceeds.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Next-generation sequencing (NGS)

    Next-generation sequencing, abbreviated NGS, is a family of methods that read millions of DNA fragments at the same time instead of one at a time. The sample is broken into pieces, prepared into a sequencing library and loaded onto an instrument that reads every fragment in parallel. Computers then assemble or map those reads to work out the original sequence.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Nucleic acid extraction

    Nucleic acid extraction is the process of getting DNA or RNA out of a sample and into a clean solution that later methods can use. Cells are broken open, proteins and other material are removed, and the nucleic acid is captured, washed and released. How well this is done sets a ceiling on everything that follows.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Paired-end sequencing

    Paired-end sequencing reads each DNA fragment from both of its ends rather than from one end only. The two reads come from the same molecule and are a roughly known distance apart, which helps software place them correctly and reveals when that distance is unexpected. Reading from one end only is called single-end sequencing.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Phred quality score

    A Phred quality score tells you how confident a sequencing instrument is about a single base it has called. The scale is logarithmic: a score of twenty means roughly a one in a hundred chance the base is wrong, thirty means one in a thousand, and forty one in ten thousand. Every base in a read carries one.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

    The polymerase chain reaction, abbreviated PCR, is a laboratory method that makes many copies of a chosen piece of DNA. Short synthetic primers mark the start and end of the region, and repeated cycles of heating and cooling let an enzyme copy it again and again. A small amount of starting material can become enough to detect or sequence.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

    Quantitative PCR, abbreviated qPCR, measures how much of a target sequence is present while the copying reaction is running, rather than only looking at the end product. A fluorescent signal rises as copies accumulate, and the cycle at which it becomes detectable indicates how much target was there at the start. More starting material gives an earlier signal.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq)

    RNA sequencing, abbreviated RNA-Seq, reads the RNA molecules present in a sample to show which genes are being expressed and at what relative level. The RNA is converted to DNA, prepared as a sequencing library and read, and the resulting reads are counted against genes or transcripts. It captures a snapshot of activity at the moment of collection.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Read

    A read is one stretch of sequence produced by a sequencing instrument from a single fragment of DNA or RNA. Each read is a string of bases together with a quality value for every base, indicating how confident the instrument is in that call. A sequencing run produces millions of reads, which analysis software then maps or assembles.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Read depth

    Read depth is the number of sequencing reads that cover a particular position in the genome. A position covered by thirty reads is said to have thirty-fold depth. Greater depth gives more confidence that a difference seen at that position is real rather than an error, and it is usually reported as an average across the target region.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Sample quality control

    Sample quality control is the set of checks that confirm material is good enough to proceed at each stage of a laboratory workflow. Typical checks measure how much nucleic acid is present, how pure it is, how intact it is, and whether the library has the expected fragment sizes. Failing samples are repeated or reported as uninformative.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Sanger sequencing

    Sanger sequencing is a laboratory method for reading the order of bases along one short stretch of DNA, typically a few hundred letters at a time. Copying of the stretch is deliberately stopped at random positions by special building blocks, and the resulting fragments are sorted by length so that the base at each position can be read off in turn.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Sequencing adapter

    A sequencing adapter is a short, known piece of DNA attached to each end of a fragment during library preparation. It is not part of the sample. Adapters give the instrument somewhere to bind the fragment and start reading, and they usually carry the barcode that identifies the sample. Adapter sequence is removed before analysis.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Sequencing by synthesis

    Sequencing by synthesis works out a DNA sequence by watching a new strand being built against it. The instrument adds labelled building blocks one cycle at a time, records which base was added at each position by its colour or by another signal, and repeats. Doing this for millions of fragments at once is what makes modern sequencing fast.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Short-read sequencing

    Short-read sequencing reads DNA in small pieces, usually between about fifty and three hundred bases each, but reads enormous numbers of those pieces at once. Because each piece is short, software must work out where it came from by matching it against a reference sequence. The approach is accurate per base and inexpensive per sample.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq)

    Single-cell RNA sequencing, abbreviated scRNA-Seq, measures gene activity in individual cells rather than averaging across a whole tissue. Cells are separated, each one's RNA is tagged with a unique label, and everything is sequenced together. The labels let software reconstruct which reads came from which cell, revealing the different cell types present in a sample.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Targeted panel sequencing

    Targeted panel sequencing reads only a chosen set of genes or regions instead of the whole genome. The regions are captured or amplified before sequencing, so most of the data come from the targets. Concentrating the reading effort on a small target allows much greater depth for the same cost, which matters when looking for rare or low-level variants.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Turnaround time

    Turnaround time is how long it takes from a sample arriving, or being collected, to a result being issued. In genomics it covers extraction, library preparation, sequencing, analysis, interpretation and reporting. Different parts of that chain dominate for different tests, and a result that arrives too late to inform a decision has limited practical value.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published

  • Whole-genome sequencing (WGS)

    Whole-genome sequencing, abbreviated WGS, reads essentially the entire genome of an organism rather than selected parts of it. For a human sample that means both the coding regions and the much larger non-coding fraction; for a bacterium or virus it usually means the complete genome. It provides the broadest view a sequencing experiment can give.

    Sequencing and laboratory methods Published
