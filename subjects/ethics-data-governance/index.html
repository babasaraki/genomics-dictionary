Ethics and data governance

Consent, data sharing, access control, equitable partnership and the legal and ethical frameworks that govern genomic data and samples.

18 entries. Open these in the searchable dictionary.

  • Admixture

    Admixture happens when people from populations that had been separated for a long time have children together, so their descendants carry chromosome segments of different ancestral origin. Over successive generations recombination cuts these segments into shorter pieces. Most human populations today show some degree of admixture, and it is a feature of a genome's history rather than a defect.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Benefit sharing

    Benefit sharing means that the people and communities who contribute samples, data or effort to research receive a fair share of what the research produces. Benefits can be financial, but more often they are practical: training, equipment, authorship, local analysis capacity, access to results, or affordable access to any product that is developed.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Biobank

    A biobank is an organised collection of biological samples and linked data, stored so that many researchers can study them over long periods. Samples may be blood, saliva or tissue, and the linked data can include questionnaires, measurements and health records. Participants consent to this future use, and governance arrangements determine who may access which data and under what conditions.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Broad consent

    Broad consent is agreement to the future use of samples and data for a described range of research, rather than for one named study only. Participants are told the kinds of research that may be done, the governance that will decide on access, and how they can withdraw. It is not blanket permission for anything whatsoever.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Computational reproducibility

    Computational reproducibility means that someone else, given the same data and the same description of the analysis, obtains the same results. It is a weaker and more achievable goal than replication, which asks whether a finding holds in a new experiment with new samples. Reproducibility is about the transparency of the computation, not about whether the conclusion is true.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Controlled access

    Controlled access means that individual-level genomic data are not downloadable by anyone, but released only to approved researchers who apply, state their purpose and agree to conditions. A committee checks that the request matches what participants consented to. Summary results from the same study are often published openly, because they carry much less risk of identifying anyone.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Data sharing

    Data sharing is making research data available to others, so that findings can be checked, combined and reused. In genomics it ranges from publishing summary statistics openly to releasing individual-level sequence data under application and approval. Sharing accelerates discovery, especially for rare conditions and outbreaks, but must respect consent, confidentiality and the interests of the communities who contributed.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • De-identification

    De-identification is the removal or masking of details that directly identify a person, such as name, address, hospital number and date of birth, before data are used or shared. It reduces risk but does not make genomic data anonymous, because a genome sequence is itself a distinguishing characteristic that can sometimes be matched to a person.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Equitable research partnership

    An equitable research partnership is a collaboration in which the partners share decisions, resources, recognition and data rather than one group supplying samples while another does the science. It covers who sets the question, where the sequencing happens, who analyses the data, who is named on the paper, and who controls what happens to the data afterwards.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • FAIR principles

    The FAIR principles are a set of guidelines stating that research data should be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable. They describe how data and their descriptions should be organised so that both people and computers can locate and use them. FAIR concerns good data management; it does not mean data must be open to everyone.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Genetic ancestry

    Genetic ancestry describes where in the past the segments of a person's DNA came from, reconstructed by comparing their genome with samples from many populations. It is a statement about lines of descent, estimated with uncertainty. It is not the same as race, nationality, ethnicity or the culture someone was raised in, all of which are social categories that people define and claim for themselves.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Genetic discrimination

    Genetic discrimination is unfair treatment of a person because of their genetic information or a presumed inherited risk, for example in employment, insurance or access to services. Fear of it is one of the main reasons people hesitate to take part in genomic research or to have a test, so protections matter for public trust as well as for fairness.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Genomic data protection

    Genomic data protection is the set of legal and practical measures that govern how genetic data about people are stored, used and shared. Because a genome is permanent, identifiable and informative about relatives, most data protection laws treat it as a special category requiring stronger safeguards than ordinary personal information.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Informed consent

    Informed consent is the process by which a person agrees to take part in research, or to a test, after being given clear information about what is involved, what will happen to their samples and data, and what the possible benefits and harms are. It is a continuing conversation, not just a signature, and participation can be withdrawn.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Material transfer agreement (MTA)

    A material transfer agreement, abbreviated MTA, is a contract between institutions setting out the terms on which biological material is sent from one to another. It records what is being transferred, what the recipient may do with it, what happens to anything derived from it, and who may publish or own resulting intellectual property.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Nagoya Protocol

    The Nagoya Protocol is an international agreement under the Convention on Biological Diversity covering access to genetic resources and the sharing of benefits arising from their use. Countries that have joined it require users to obtain permission before collecting biological material and to agree terms for sharing benefits. Many research projects involving samples collected abroad must comply.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Research ethics approval

    Research ethics approval is formal permission from an independent committee to carry out a study involving people, their samples or their data. The committee examines the scientific justification, the risks and benefits, how participants will be recruited and consented, and how data will be protected. Work should not begin before approval is granted.

    Ethics and data governance Published

  • Return of results

    Return of results means giving participants information that came out of research on their samples or data. This can be the main finding they were tested for, or an unrelated finding noticed along the way. Studies differ in what they offer, and the plan should be explained during consent rather than decided after a finding appears.

    Ethics and data governance Published
