Genetics foundations

The vocabulary that everything else is built on: genes, chromosomes, alleles, transcription and translation, inheritance patterns and the chemistry of nucleic acids.

43 entries. Open these in the searchable dictionary.

  • Allele

    An allele is one of the alternative versions of a sequence found at a particular place in the genome. Because people inherit one copy of each chromosome from each parent, an individual carries two alleles at most positions, which may be identical or different. Alleles can differ by a single letter or by a larger stretch of sequence.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Aneuploidy

    Aneuploidy means having a number of chromosomes that differs from the usual complement for that organism, such as one extra copy or one missing copy. In humans it usually arises when chromosomes fail to separate correctly during the formation of eggs or sperm. Aneuploidy is also very common in cancer cells, where whole chromosomes are frequently gained or lost.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Base pair (bp)

    A base pair is two nucleotide bases on opposite strands of a double-stranded nucleic acid that are held together by hydrogen bonds. In DNA, adenine pairs with thymine and cytosine pairs with guanine. Because of this fixed pairing, each strand carries the information needed to rebuild the other. Base pairs are also the standard unit for measuring the length of a DNA sequence.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Centromere

    A centromere is the constricted region of a chromosome where the two copies produced by DNA replication are held together and where the cell attaches the machinery that pulls them apart during division. Its position gives each chromosome a characteristic shape under the microscope. Centromeres contain long stretches of highly repetitive DNA that are difficult to sequence.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Chromosome

    A chromosome is a long molecule of DNA wound around proteins and packaged so that it fits inside a cell and can be moved reliably when the cell divides. Human cells typically carry forty-six chromosomes arranged in twenty-three pairs, with one member of each pair inherited from each parent. Chromosomes become visible under a microscope when a cell is dividing.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Codon

    A codon is a group of three consecutive bases in a messenger RNA that specifies one amino acid during protein synthesis, or signals that synthesis should stop. Reading a coding sequence three bases at a time gives the order of amino acids in the protein. There are sixty-four possible codons, and most amino acids are specified by more than one of them.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • DNA methylation

    DNA methylation is the addition of a small chemical group to a DNA base, most often a cytosine followed by a guanine. The sequence itself is unchanged, but the mark influences whether nearby genes are switched on. Patterns of methylation differ between tissues, are copied when cells divide, and change during development, with age and in disease.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)

    Deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA, is the chemical that carries the inherited instructions used by almost every living organism. It is built from four chemical letters strung along two strands that wind around each other in a double helix. The order of those letters stores information that cells read to build proteins and to decide when each gene is switched on. Cells copy their DNA before dividing, so the instructions pass to the next generation of cells.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Dominant inheritance

    Dominant inheritance describes a pattern in which a single altered copy of a gene is enough to produce a trait or condition, even though the other copy is unchanged. A person with such a variant has, on average, a one in two chance of passing it to each child. Dominant conditions often appear in several generations of a family.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Epigenetics

    Epigenetics is the study of changes that affect how genes are used without altering the underlying DNA sequence. These include chemical marks added to DNA or to the proteins that package it, which influence whether a gene is switched on or off. Such marks differ between tissues and can change over time and with environment, and some persist through cell division.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Exon

    An exon is a part of a gene that is kept in the finished RNA molecule after the gene has been copied and edited. Most human genes are split into several exons separated by sequences that are removed. Exons may contain the instructions for part of a protein, or they may carry untranslated sequence at either end of the RNA.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Gene

    A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for making a product the cell uses, most often a protein but sometimes a functional RNA molecule. Each gene sits at a particular place on a chromosome and usually includes signals that control when and where it is switched on. People carry two copies of most genes, one inherited from each parent.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Gene expression

    Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make a product, usually a protein. It is also the term for how much of that product a cell makes. Expression varies between tissues, between individuals and over time, and it responds to signals, so the same genome produces very different cells.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genetic code

    The genetic code is the set of rules by which cells translate the sequence of bases in a messenger RNA into the sequence of amino acids in a protein. Bases are read in groups of three, and each group corresponds to one amino acid or to a stop signal. The same code is used by almost all living things, with a few known exceptions.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genetic drift

    Genetic drift is the change in how common an allele is from one generation to the next, caused simply by chance in who reproduces and which copies are passed on. It occurs in every population but matters most in small ones, where frequencies can swing widely. Drift can make an allele common, or lose it altogether, without the allele doing anything useful or harmful.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Genetic recombination

    Genetic recombination is the exchange of segments of DNA between two chromosomes or DNA molecules. In people it happens mainly during the formation of eggs and sperm, when matching chromosomes swap sections. The result is that the chromosome a child inherits is a mixture of the two copies carried by that parent, so each child receives a different combination.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genome

    A genome is the complete set of genetic material in an organism or a cell. For a person this means all the DNA in the nucleus, inherited from both parents, plus the small separate genome inside mitochondria. The genome includes genes and the much larger amount of DNA between and around them that helps control how genes behave.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Genotype

    A genotype is the particular combination of alleles an individual carries at one or more places in the genome. It can describe a single position, such as which two letters a person has at one site, or a set of positions across many genes. Genotype is determined by inheritance and, unlike many observable traits, does not usually change during life.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Haplotype

    A haplotype is a set of DNA variants that sit on the same copy of a chromosome and are usually inherited together as a unit. Because we carry two copies of most chromosomes, one from each parent, every person has two haplotypes across any region. Knowing which variants travel together often tells you more than knowing the list of variants alone.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Insertion or deletion (indel)

    An insertion or deletion, often shortened to indel, is a variant in which extra DNA letters are added or existing letters are removed compared with a reference sequence. Indels range from a single letter to many. Within protein-coding sequence, an indel whose length is not a multiple of three shifts the reading frame and usually disrupts the protein that follows.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Intron

    An intron is a section of a gene that is copied into the initial RNA molecule but then cut out before the RNA is used. The remaining pieces, called exons, are joined together. Introns are common in the genes of plants, animals and fungi, and although they are removed, sequences within and around them help control how the RNA is assembled.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Karyotype

    A karyotype is the complete set of chromosomes in a cell, and also the laboratory image in which those chromosomes are arranged in order by size and banding pattern. Examining a karyotype shows how many chromosomes are present and whether any large pieces are missing, duplicated or rearranged. It is one of the oldest techniques in genetics and remains useful.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Locus

    A locus is a specific location in the genome, described by the chromosome it sits on and its position along that chromosome. A locus can be as small as a single base or as large as a whole gene or region. The plural is loci. Giving a locus precisely requires stating which reference assembly the coordinates refer to.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Meiosis

    Meiosis is the specialised form of cell division that produces eggs and sperm. It halves the number of chromosomes so that each gamete carries one copy of each chromosome instead of two. When egg and sperm combine, the full number is restored. Meiosis also shuffles the genetic material inherited from each parent, so siblings are genetically different.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Mendelian inheritance

    Mendelian inheritance describes traits and conditions determined largely by variants in a single gene, passed on in predictable patterns first described by Gregor Mendel. The main patterns are dominant, recessive and those linked to the X chromosome. Many rare conditions follow these patterns, while most common traits and diseases involve many genes together with environmental influences.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Messenger RNA (mRNA)

    Messenger RNA, usually abbreviated to mRNA, is the type of RNA that carries the instructions from a gene to the machinery that makes proteins. It is produced by copying a gene, edited to remove intervening sections, and then read three bases at a time to assemble a protein. The amount of a given messenger RNA in a cell reflects how active that gene currently is.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)

    Mitochondrial DNA, or mtDNA, is the small circular genome inside mitochondria, the compartments that generate most of a cell's usable energy. It is separate from the chromosomes in the nucleus, present in many copies per cell, and in humans it is inherited from the mother. It carries a small number of genes, all serving energy production or the machinery that builds mitochondrial proteins.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Mutation

    A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence compared with what was there before. Mutations arise from errors during DNA copying, from chemical damage, or from exposure to agents such as radiation. They may be inherited from a parent or acquired during a person's lifetime. Most have no noticeable effect, some are harmful, and a few are advantageous.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Natural selection

    Natural selection is the process by which inherited differences that affect survival or reproduction become more or less common across generations. If carrying a particular allele means leaving more descendants in a given environment, that allele's frequency rises. Selection acts only on variation that already exists, and what counts as advantageous depends entirely on the environment at the time.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Nucleotide

    A nucleotide is the basic building block of DNA and RNA. Each one has three parts: a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base. Nucleotides join end to end to form long chains, and the order of their bases carries genetic information. DNA uses the bases adenine, cytosine, guanine and thymine, while RNA uses uracil in place of thymine.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Open reading frame (ORF)

    An open reading frame is a stretch of sequence that could be read as a continuous set of three-letter codons without hitting a stop signal. Because sequence can be read in several frames and on either strand, software looks for such stretches when predicting where protein-coding genes might lie. An open reading frame is a candidate for coding, not proof of it.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Phenotype

    A phenotype is any observable or measurable characteristic of an organism, from eye colour and height to blood test values, disease status and how someone responds to a medicine. Phenotypes arise from the combined action of genetic variation, developmental processes and environment. Because a phenotype has to be measured, how it is defined and recorded affects what any study can conclude.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Promoter

    A promoter is a stretch of DNA just before a gene where the machinery that makes RNA first assembles. It acts as a starting signal, setting where transcription begins and influencing how often it happens. Promoters contain short sequence patterns recognised by proteins, and their activity can differ between tissues and between conditions within the same organism.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • RNA splicing

    RNA splicing is the editing step in which a cell removes the intervening sections of a freshly made RNA molecule and joins the remaining pieces together. This produces a continuous sequence ready to be read for protein synthesis. Many genes can be spliced in more than one way, so a single gene can give rise to several different RNA molecules and proteins.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Recessive inheritance

    Recessive inheritance describes a pattern in which both copies of a gene must be altered before a trait or condition appears. Someone with one altered copy and one unaltered copy is usually unaffected and is described as a carrier. When both parents are carriers, each child has about a one in four chance of inheriting both altered copies.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Ribonucleic acid (RNA)

    Ribonucleic acid, or RNA, is a nucleic acid closely related to DNA that cells use to carry and act on genetic information. It is usually single-stranded and uses the base uracil where DNA uses thymine. Some RNA molecules carry the instructions for making a protein, while others help build proteins, regulate which genes are active, or form part of the cell's machinery.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)

    A single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP, is a position in the genome where people commonly differ by one DNA letter. One person may carry a C where another carries a T, and each version is called an allele. SNPs are the commonest form of human genetic variation. Most of them lie outside genes and have no known effect on health or appearance.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Single-nucleotide variant (SNV)

    A single-nucleotide variant, abbreviated to SNV, is a change of one DNA letter at one position compared with a reference sequence. It is the most common kind of difference between individuals and also a frequent change acquired by cells over time. Whether a single-nucleotide variant matters depends on where it falls and what it does, not on its size.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Telomere

    A telomere is the protective cap at each end of a chromosome, made of a short DNA sequence repeated many times together with bound proteins. Telomeres stop the ends of chromosomes from being treated as broken DNA and prevent them from fusing together. They shorten each time most cells divide, which limits how many divisions a cell can undergo.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Transcription

    Transcription is the process by which a cell copies a stretch of DNA into RNA. An enzyme called RNA polymerase binds near the start of a gene, unwinds the two DNA strands and builds an RNA molecule that matches one of them. The resulting RNA may carry instructions for a protein or may itself do a job in the cell.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Translation

    Translation is the process by which a cell builds a protein using the instructions carried in a messenger RNA. Ribosomes move along the messenger RNA reading its bases three at a time, and transfer RNA molecules deliver the matching amino acids. The amino acids are joined into a chain that folds into a working protein. Translation stops at a stop signal.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • Variant

    A variant is a difference between the DNA sequence observed in a sample and the sequence of the reference used for comparison. Variants are ordinary: every person carries millions of them. A variant is simply a difference, and saying that one exists tells you nothing about whether it affects health. That judgement requires separate evidence and a formal classification process.

    Genetics foundations Published

  • X-linked inheritance

    X-linked inheritance describes patterns produced by genes on the X chromosome. Because people with one X chromosome have only a single copy of those genes, an altered copy is expressed directly, whereas people with two X chromosomes have a second copy that may compensate. This produces family patterns that differ from those of genes on the other chromosomes.

    Genetics foundations Published
