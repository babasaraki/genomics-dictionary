Human and population genomics

How genetic variation is distributed within and between human populations, and the concepts used to study it: allele frequency, linkage disequilibrium, ancestry, association studies and reference resources.

26 entries. Open these in the searchable dictionary.

  • Admixture

    Admixture happens when people from populations that had been separated for a long time have children together, so their descendants carry chromosome segments of different ancestral origin. Over successive generations recombination cuts these segments into shorter pieces. Most human populations today show some degree of admixture, and it is a feature of a genome's history rather than a defect.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Association versus causation

    An association means two things vary together: a genetic variant is seen more often in people with a trait, or a gene's expression tracks a disease state. A causal relationship means that changing one would change the other. Association is observed; causation is inferred, and the inference requires evidence beyond the observation that the two travel together.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Human and population genomics Published

  • Biobank

    A biobank is an organised collection of biological samples and linked data, stored so that many researchers can study them over long periods. Samples may be blood, saliva or tissue, and the linked data can include questionnaires, measurements and health records. Participants consent to this future use, and governance arrangements determine who may access which data and under what conditions.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Copy number variant (CNV)

    A copy number variant, or CNV, is a stretch of DNA present in a different number of copies in one person than in another, either deleted or duplicated one or more times. Copy number differences are an ordinary part of human variation. Some change how much of a gene product is made, and a subset is associated with developmental conditions or disease.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Effective population size (Ne)

    Effective population size, written Ne, is the size of an idealised population that would lose genetic variation at the same rate as the real population being studied. It is usually much smaller than the number of people alive, because real populations have unequal family sizes, overlapping generations and uneven sex ratios. It is a bookkeeping device for predicting drift, not a headcount.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Founder effect

    A founder effect occurs when a small group separates from a larger population and establishes a new one. The founders carry only part of the original population's variation, so whatever alleles they happen to have become the starting point for everyone descended from them. An allele that was rare in the source population can end up far more common in the new one.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Genetic ancestry

    Genetic ancestry describes where in the past the segments of a person's DNA came from, reconstructed by comparing their genome with samples from many populations. It is a statement about lines of descent, estimated with uncertainty. It is not the same as race, nationality, ethnicity or the culture someone was raised in, all of which are social categories that people define and claim for themselves.

    Human and population genomics Ethics and data governance Published

  • Genetic drift

    Genetic drift is the change in how common an allele is from one generation to the next, caused simply by chance in who reproduces and which copies are passed on. It occurs in every population but matters most in small ones, where frequencies can swing widely. Drift can make an allele common, or lose it altogether, without the allele doing anything useful or harmful.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

    A genome-wide association study, or GWAS, tests hundreds of thousands to millions of positions across the genome to see whether any version of a position occurs more often in people with a trait than in people without it. It scans the genome without a prior hypothesis about which gene matters, and it reports statistical associations rather than proven causes.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Genotype imputation

    Genotype imputation fills in positions that were not directly measured, by comparing the positions that were measured against a reference panel of people whose genomes are known in detail. Because nearby variants travel together on shared stretches of chromosome, matching those stretches lets software predict the missing genotypes, together with a confidence value for each prediction.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Haplotype

    A haplotype is a set of DNA variants that sit on the same copy of a chromosome and are usually inherited together as a unit. Because we carry two copies of most chromosomes, one from each parent, every person has two haplotypes across any region. Knowing which variants travel together often tells you more than knowing the list of variants alone.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Haplotype phasing

    Haplotype phasing works out which of a person's genetic variants sit on the same copy of a chromosome. Standard genotyping records that someone carries two different versions at two positions, but not how those versions are paired between the maternal and paternal copies. Phasing resolves that arrangement, either by statistical comparison with known haplotypes or by reading long DNA molecules directly.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE)

    Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium describes the simple relationship expected between how often each allele occurs and how often each genotype occurs, in a large population that mates at random and in which nothing is disturbing the alleles. If the two alleles have frequencies p and q, the expected genotype proportions are p squared, two pq and q squared. Real populations only approximate this, so it serves as a baseline for comparison.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Heritability

    Heritability is the share of the differences between people in a trait, within a particular population and set of environments, that can be attributed to differences in their genes. It is a property of a group rather than of an individual, and it changes when the population or the environment changes. A high value does not mean a trait is fixed or beyond modification.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Linkage disequilibrium (LD)

    Linkage disequilibrium, or LD, means that particular versions of two nearby DNA positions turn up together more often than chance alone would predict. It arises because stretches of chromosome are passed down intact, and recombination only occasionally shuffles them. LD generally weakens with distance along the chromosome and with the number of generations since the alleles first appeared together.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Minor allele frequency (MAF)

    Minor allele frequency, or MAF, is how often the less common of two versions of a DNA position is seen in a group of people. If eight in every hundred copies of a position carry a G and ninety-two carry an A, the minor allele frequency is 0.08. The figure describes a sampled population, not an individual, and the same allele can be common in one population and almost absent in another.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)

    Mitochondrial DNA, or mtDNA, is the small circular genome inside mitochondria, the compartments that generate most of a cell's usable energy. It is separate from the chromosomes in the nucleus, present in many copies per cell, and in humans it is inherited from the mother. It carries a small number of genes, all serving energy production or the machinery that builds mitochondrial proteins.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Natural selection

    Natural selection is the process by which inherited differences that affect survival or reproduction become more or less common across generations. If carrying a particular allele means leaving more descendants in a given environment, that allele's frequency rises. Selection acts only on variation that already exists, and what counts as advantageous depends entirely on the environment at the time.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Odds ratio (OR)

    An odds ratio compares the odds of an outcome between two groups. In genomic association studies it usually compares the odds of a trait in people carrying one allele with the odds in those carrying another. A value of one means no difference, above one means higher odds in the first group, and below one means lower odds. Both directions matter.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Human and population genomics Published

  • Pangenome

    A pangenome is a representation of a species' DNA that holds many genomes at once, instead of a single linear sequence. For humans it is assembled from people of diverse ancestries, so that sequence present in some people but missing from the traditional reference is included. It is usually stored as a graph, with shared paths and alternative branches where genomes differ.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Polygenic risk score (PRS)

    A polygenic risk score, or PRS, adds up the small contributions of many genetic variants to give a single number summarising how a person's genome compares with others for a trait. It is a relative ranking within a reference group, not a diagnosis or a prediction of what will happen. For most common traits, non-genetic factors account for a large share of the variation between people.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Population structure

    Population structure means that the people in a dataset are not one randomly mixing group: allele frequencies differ systematically between subgroups because of geography, history, migration and patterns of partnership. If this patterning is ignored, it can create apparent links between a variant and a trait that actually reflect which subgroup a person belongs to rather than any biological effect.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Reference genome

    A reference genome is an agreed, assembled DNA sequence used as a shared coordinate system, so that laboratories everywhere can describe positions and variants in the same way. The current human reference is a mosaic built from a small number of donors. It is a convenient yardstick rather than a model of a healthy or typical person, and it does not contain all human variation.

    Human and population genomics Bioinformatics and statistics Published

  • Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)

    A single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP, is a position in the genome where people commonly differ by one DNA letter. One person may carry a C where another carries a T, and each version is called an allele. SNPs are the commonest form of human genetic variation. Most of them lie outside genes and have no known effect on health or appearance.

    Human and population genomics Genetics foundations Published

  • Structural variant (SV)

    A structural variant, or SV, is a difference in DNA involving a sizeable chunk of a chromosome rather than one or a few letters. Deletions, duplications, insertions, inversions and translocations all count. The usual working threshold is around fifty base pairs. Structural variants are far less numerous than single-letter differences, yet together they affect much more total DNA in any genome.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Y-chromosome haplogroup

    A Y-chromosome haplogroup is a branch of the family tree built from the male-specific part of the Y chromosome, which passes from father to son largely unchanged. Men who share the same set of defining variants are placed on the same branch. Haplogroups summarise a single paternal line of descent and are labelled with letters and numbers under an international naming convention.

    Human and population genomics Published
