Clinical and cancer genomics

Terminology used when genomic information informs diagnosis, screening and cancer care, including variant classification, penetrance, tumour evolution and the limits of what a genomic result can establish.

39 entries. Open these in the searchable dictionary.

  • ACMG/AMP variant classification

    ACMG/AMP variant classification is a shared framework that clinical laboratories use to decide how confident they are that a DNA variant causes disease. It sorts evidence into weighted criteria and combines them into five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign and benign. The aim is that different laboratories reading the same evidence reach the same conclusion.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Carrier screening

    Carrier screening is testing that tells a person whether they carry one copy of a variant for a recessive condition. Carriers of such conditions are typically healthy, because the second working copy of the gene is enough. The information becomes relevant when two carriers of the same condition have children together, since the condition can then appear.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Cell-free DNA screening (cfDNA screening)

    Cell-free DNA screening analyses short fragments of DNA circulating in a person's blood. In pregnancy, a sample from the pregnant person contains fragments from the placenta as well as from their own cells, and the mixture can be analysed to estimate the chance of certain chromosomal conditions in the pregnancy. It is a screening test, not a diagnosis.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA)

    Circulating tumour DNA, abbreviated ctDNA, is the fraction of DNA in a person's blood that came from tumour cells. Dying cells release short DNA fragments into the bloodstream, and in someone with cancer a portion of those fragments carries the tumour's changes. Testing blood in this way is often called a liquid biopsy.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Clinical actionability

    Clinical actionability describes whether knowing a genomic result could change what is done for a person's care. A finding is actionable when an established intervention exists, such as earlier surveillance, a preventive measure, a change of medicine or a different treatment. Many genomic findings are informative and interesting without being actionable in this specific sense.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Clonal evolution

    Clonal evolution describes how a tumour changes over time as its cells acquire new mutations and compete with one another. Cells whose changes give an advantage leave more descendants, so the makeup of the tumour shifts. Treatment is part of this process, because it removes sensitive cells and leaves resistant ones room to expand.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Companion diagnostic

    A companion diagnostic is a test that is formally tied to a particular medicine and is used to decide whether that medicine is suitable for a person. The test identifies a feature, often a genomic alteration or a protein level, that the medicine's evidence depends on. Regulators approve the test and the medicine as a linked pair.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Copy number variant (CNV)

    A copy number variant, or CNV, is a stretch of DNA present in a different number of copies in one person than in another, either deleted or duplicated one or more times. Copy number differences are an ordinary part of human variation. Some change how much of a gene product is made, and a subset is associated with developmental conditions or disease.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Diagnostic yield

    Diagnostic yield is the proportion of people tested in whom a test provides an explanation for their condition. If a laboratory sequences one hundred people with a suspected genetic disorder and reaches a confident answer in thirty, the yield is thirty per cent. Yield describes the performance of a test in a particular group of patients, not in everybody.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Driver mutation

    A driver mutation is a change in a tumour's DNA that gives the cell a growth advantage and so contributes to the cancer developing or progressing. Only a small number of the changes found in a tumour are drivers. The rest are bystanders that accumulated along the way without helping the tumour grow.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Expressivity

    Expressivity describes how differently a trait or condition can appear among people who carry the same genotype. Where penetrance asks whether a carrier is affected at all, expressivity asks how severely and in which tissues. Variable expressivity means that two relatives with an identical variant may have very different features, ages of onset or degrees of difficulty.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Founder effect

    A founder effect occurs when a small group separates from a larger population and establishes a new one. The founders carry only part of the original population's variation, so whatever alleles they happen to have become the starting point for everyone descended from them. An allele that was rare in the source population can end up far more common in the new one.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Genetic counselling

    Genetic counselling is a conversation between a trained professional and a person or family about how a genetic condition or test result might affect them. It covers what a result can and cannot say, what it may mean for relatives, and what choices exist. The counsellor supports the person in reaching their own decision rather than directing it.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Germline variant

    A germline variant is a difference in the DNA sequence that was present in the egg or sperm cell from which a person developed. Because it was there from the beginning, it is carried in essentially every cell of the body and can be passed on to children. Most germline variants are harmless differences between people; only a small minority affect health.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD)

    Homologous recombination is a precise way of mending breaks that run through both strands of the DNA double helix, using the matching chromosome as a template. Homologous recombination deficiency, abbreviated HRD, means a tumour has lost this accurate repair route and must rely on error-prone alternatives. The resulting scars across the genome can be measured.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Loss of heterozygosity (LOH)

    Most human chromosomes come in pairs, so a person can carry two different versions of a gene. Loss of heterozygosity, abbreviated LOH, happens when a cell loses one of those versions, leaving only one kind present at that location. In tumours this often removes the remaining working copy of a gene that was restraining growth.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Measurable residual disease (MRD)

    Measurable residual disease, abbreviated MRD, refers to the small number of cancer cells that can remain after treatment when standard scans and ordinary microscopy no longer detect anything. Sensitive molecular or cellular methods can sometimes find this remaining disease. The term was previously rendered as minimal residual disease, and both forms are still encountered.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Microsatellite instability (MSI)

    Microsatellites are short stretches of DNA in which a very small motif repeats many times. These regions slip easily during copying, and a cell with working repair machinery corrects the errors. Microsatellite instability, abbreviated MSI, is the pattern of varying repeat lengths seen in a tumour whose repair system is no longer correcting those slips.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR)

    Mismatch repair is the system that proofreads newly copied DNA and corrects mispaired bases and small slips. Mismatch repair deficiency, abbreviated dMMR, means that this system is not working in a tumour. Errors then accumulate, especially in repetitive stretches, giving the tumour an unusually large number of mutations compared with repair-proficient tumours.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Mutational signature

    A mutational signature is a characteristic pattern of DNA changes left behind by a particular process. Different causes of damage, and different failures of repair, favour different kinds of change in different sequence contexts. Reading the pattern across a tumour genome can suggest which processes have been at work in that tumour's history.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Newborn screening

    Newborn screening is a public health programme that tests babies shortly after birth for a small number of serious conditions that can be treated effectively if they are found early. It usually uses a few drops of blood taken from the heel. Screening does not diagnose; a result outside the expected range leads to confirmatory testing.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Oncogene

    An oncogene is a gene whose overactivity can drive a cell to divide when it should not. Most oncogenes are altered forms of normal genes, called proto-oncogenes, that control growth signals under tight regulation. A change that locks such a gene into a permanently switched-on state can contribute to the development of a tumour.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Passenger mutation

    A passenger mutation is a change in a tumour's DNA that happened along the way but does not itself help the tumour grow. Cells accumulate such changes continually, and when one cell gives rise to a tumour, all the changes it had already collected are carried along. Passengers greatly outnumber the changes that actually drive the cancer.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Pathogenic variant

    A pathogenic variant is a DNA change that the available evidence shows is capable of causing or strongly predisposing to a disease. It is a judgement about evidence, not a separate kind of molecule: the same sequence change is simply better supported as harmful than the many variants that make no difference. Classifications can be revised when new evidence arrives.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Penetrance

    Penetrance is the proportion of people carrying a particular genotype who actually show the associated trait or condition. If every carrier is affected, penetrance is complete; if only some are, it is reduced or incomplete. Penetrance is a property of a variant in a population and a context, not a prediction about any single person.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Polygenic risk score (PRS)

    A polygenic risk score, or PRS, adds up the small contributions of many genetic variants to give a single number summarising how a person's genome compares with others for a trait. It is a relative ranking within a reference group, not a diagnosis or a prediction of what will happen. For most common traits, non-genetic factors account for a large share of the variation between people.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Positive predictive value (PPV)

    Positive predictive value, abbreviated PPV, is the proportion of positive test results that are correct. It answers the question a person actually asks: given that my result is positive, how likely is it that the condition is really present? Unlike sensitivity and specificity, it depends strongly on how common the condition is in the group being tested.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Secondary findings

    Secondary findings are results that a laboratory looks for deliberately, but that are unrelated to the reason the test was requested. When a person has their exome or genome sequenced for one condition, a defined list of other genes may also be examined because finding a variant in them could prevent serious harm. Consent is sought beforehand.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Sensitivity and specificity

    Sensitivity is the proportion of people or samples that truly have a condition and are correctly identified by a test. Specificity is the proportion of those without the condition that the test correctly calls negative. Both are properties of the test measured against a reference standard, and they describe performance in two different groups, so they must be reported together.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Somatic variant

    A somatic variant is a change in DNA that appears in a body cell during a person's lifetime rather than being inherited. It is found only in that cell and the cells descended from it, such as a tumour, and it cannot be passed to children. Somatic variants accumulate steadily with age and with exposure to agents that damage DNA.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Structural variant (SV)

    A structural variant, or SV, is a difference in DNA involving a sizeable chunk of a chromosome rather than one or a few letters. Deletions, duplications, insertions, inversions and translocations all count. The usual working threshold is around fifty base pairs. Structural variants are far less numerous than single-letter differences, yet together they affect much more total DNA in any genome.

    Human and population genomics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Tumour heterogeneity

    Tumour heterogeneity means that the cells within one tumour are not all the same. Different regions can carry different mutations, behave differently and respond differently to treatment, and secondary deposits elsewhere in the body may differ again from the original site. A single small biopsy samples only part of this variation.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Tumour mutational burden (TMB)

    Tumour mutational burden, abbreviated TMB, counts how many somatic changes a tumour carries, usually expressed per million bases of DNA examined. Tumours with a high burden may display more abnormal proteins on their surface, which can make them more visible to the immune system. It is used as a marker alongside other tests, not as a diagnosis.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Tumour suppressor gene

    A tumour suppressor gene normally restrains cell growth, repairs damaged DNA or triggers the death of a damaged cell. Losing the function of such a gene removes a brake, making it easier for a tumour to develop. Because cells carry two copies of most genes, both usually have to be disabled before the effect is felt.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Variant Call Format (VCF)

    Variant Call Format, usually shortened to VCF, is the standard text format for recording differences between sequenced samples and a reference genome. Each line describes one position: where it sits, what the reference carries, what alternative alleles were seen, how confident the caller is, and the genotype of every sample in the file. Annotations and filters are added as extra fields.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Variant annotation

    Variant annotation adds meaning to a list of genomic positions. For each variant it records which genes and transcripts it falls in, what effect it is predicted to have on the protein, how often the allele is seen in population databases, and what clinical databases already say about it. Annotation organises the evidence; it does not by itself decide significance.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Variant calling

    Variant calling is the step that decides, from the reads piled up at each position of a genome, whether a sample differs from the reference and what its genotype is. The caller weighs the number of reads supporting each allele, their base and mapping qualities, and the expected behaviour of the sequencing technology, then emits the sites it judges to be real.

    Bioinformatics and statistics Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS)

    A variant of uncertain significance, usually shortened to VUS, is a DNA change for which there is not yet enough evidence to say whether it affects health. It sits between benign and pathogenic on the classification scale. A VUS is an honest statement about incomplete knowledge rather than a weak positive result, and it may be reclassified later in either direction.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published

  • Whole-exome sequencing (WES)

    Whole-exome sequencing, abbreviated WES, reads the protein-coding portions of the genome, known collectively as the exome. These make up a small fraction of the total DNA but contain a large share of the variants currently known to cause inherited disease. Sequencing only this fraction is cheaper than sequencing everything and produces a smaller, more tractable dataset.

    Clinical and cancer genomics Published
